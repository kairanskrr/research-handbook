# Archival research

This page covers **archival research**: going to an archive to read unpublished original records (letters, diaries, minutes, institutional files and the like), or their digitized versions, and using them to answer a question about the past. In 2012 Ithaka S+R interviewed 39 US historians and graduate students; its report concluded that working with primary sources is still at the core of historical research methods[^rutner2012supporting-e19]. Researchers in literature, law, political science, and sociology also go to archives. Fields disagree about what counts as a primary source[^saaacrl2018primary-e2], but the steps of finding records and scrutinizing them are the same.

The difference between primary and secondary sources, the basic rules of an archive, and the background of that 2012 study of 39 interviewees are covered in the [History and archaeology field overview](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md#working-in-an-archive), so they are not repeated here. This page draws on three kinds of sources. First, two public documents from the US archival profession: the Society of American Archivists (SAA) guide to using archives (the "SAA archives guide" below), and the *Guidelines for Primary Source Literacy* it produced with the Association of College and Research Libraries (ACRL) (the "literacy guidelines" below). Second, web pages of the UK National Archives and the US National Archives and Records Administration, which speak only for those two institutions. Third, a few sources already used in the history and archaeology field overview. So this page reflects **British and American practice**; in other countries, follow the rules of local archives and local law. Note that "SAA" in the history and archaeology field overview means the Society for American Archaeology, a different organization.

**Scope**: this page was first written for the history and archaeology field pages. Its sources are mainly documents from British and American archival institutions and the archival profession, plus guidelines and methods articles from the US history profession (two software engineering papers appear only for comparison). Besides [History and archaeology](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md), the field overviews for [Arts](../07_fields/arts/README.md), [Languages and literature](../07_fields/languages-literature/README.md), and [Sociology](../07_fields/sociology/README.md) also link here. Each field has its own habits about what counts as a primary source and how to cite archives; check your field's conventions before you rely on this page.

**Strategies it usually serves**: [Interpretation and argument](../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md) (building an argument about the past from fragmentary records). The basis is the literacy guidelines' expectation that you examine and synthesize multiple sources to build, support, or refute an argument[^saaacrl2018primary-e16]; see Step 9 below.

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- turn a topic into a question archives can answer, and find where the relevant records are likely held;
- prepare before a visit: check the rules, sort out access, and budget time and money;
- work efficiently in the reading room and record citations that let someone else find the original later;
- scrutinize a document: who wrote it, when, why, and for whom, and what the archive does **not** contain;
- know the pitfalls of digitized records, restricted records, and copyright.

## 1. What this kind of research is and when to use it

**Archives are not libraries.** Archival material is often unique[^schmidt2016using-e1]. The UK National Archives' introductory page explains that libraries arrange books by subject and author, while archives are arranged by **the person or organization that created them**, so you will probably need to look at records from several creators and several archives[^tnastarthere-e1]. Finding archival material therefore starts not with subject keywords but with the question "who would have left a record?"

**When to use it:** your question can only be answered from unpublished records left by people at the time, such as how decisions were made inside an institution or what someone thought in private. If published material is enough, or you are studying something happening now, archival research may not be the first choice.

**How it differs from related methods** (our own comparison, based on the sources below):

| | Archival research | Case study (see [Case study and ethnography](case_study.md) under research methods) | Oral history (see the [History and archaeology field overview](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md#oral-history-the-narrators-consent-and-control)) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What you study | Records left by the past | A phenomenon happening now[^ralph2020empirical-e414] | Eyewitnesses' accounts of the past |
| Can you create new data? | No, you can only use what survives | Yes, through interviews and observation | Yes, the interview itself is new data |
| Main difficulty | Records are fragmentary and have gaps[^aha2023standards-e14] | Getting access to the field site | Narrators' consent and the subjectivity of memory |

In software engineering, Wohlin argues that studies analyzing only existing repository data should be called "archival analysis" rather than case studies[^wohlin2021case-e3]. Archival research as this page means it pays more attention to **who left the records and why they survived**.

## 2. How to do it

### Step 1: Turn your topic into a question archives can answer

**How:**
1. **Read the secondary literature first and pull out names of people, organizations, and dates.** Archives file material under the names of people and organizations[^cronon2009learning-e29]. Cronon and colleagues' introductory website on historical research uses the origins of Earth Day as an example: first identify the people and organizations involved, then work out which years matter most for the question[^cronon2009learning-e30].
2. **Ask "who would have left a record?"** For every person and organization in the secondary literature, ask: did they leave written records?[^cronon2009learning-e40]
3. **Make a "wish list" of ideal sources**, and keep updating it as you meet the real documents[^cronon2009learning-e41].
4. **Check the question itself.** The literacy guidelines say a good research question is answerable, arguable, and neither too broad nor too narrow; the question determines what information you need and which sources count as primary[^saaacrl2018primary-e22].

**Why:** you can only tell which repositories to contact once you have defined your topic and worked out what material you are looking for[^schmidt2016using-e2].

**Where people go wrong:** One mistake is fixing the question too rigidly. The literacy guidelines remind you that research is iterative and your question may change once you find sources[^saaacrl2018primary-e8]. The other is changing topic after the first document. Cronon and colleagues advise going in with a question you really care about and not dropping it lightly, while letting it change through your conversation with the documents; write down your thinking, because abandoned questions can become future projects[^cronon2009learning-e35].

### Step 2: Find the collections and learn to read finding aids

**How:**
- **Follow other people's footnotes.** Check the bibliographies of works on your topic to see where their information came from[^schmidt2016using-e3], and ask experts in the field which archives they have used[^schmidt2016using-e4]. The 2012 US study of 39 interviewees also found that following citation trails is an important way to discover sources[^rutner2012supporting-e18].
- **Use the national archives' catalog.** The UK National Archives' catalog, Discovery, also searches records held by other archives[^tnastarthere-e7]. For union catalogs in other countries, ask your university librarian.
- **Use common sense.** Archives tend to collect material connected to their own institutional affiliation[^cronon2009learning-e31].
- **Read the finding aid** (a document describing what a collection contains, like a book's table of contents[^schmidt2016using-e5]). Sometimes it also explains how the collection was formed, how the archive acquired it, and how the material is arranged[^schmidt2016using-e6]. Look closely at the **bulk dates**, the years most of the material comes from[^schmidt2016using-e33]: a collection labeled "1921–2005" does not have material for every year. Also read the **access and use** section, which states restrictions, copyright, and the preferred citation[^schmidt2016using-e34].
- **Contact the archival staff.** Go through the catalog, finding aids, and website first, then call or email to confirm[^schmidt2016using-e10]. In your email, describe your project, list the finding aids you have read and the materials you want to see, and ask whether there is other relevant material[^schmidt2016using-e9]. For archives that do not list their collections online, contacting the staff is the only way to learn what they hold[^schmidt2016using-e11]. The person who processed the collection is one of the best helpers you can find[^cronon2009learning-e33].

**Why:** archival catalogs are not like Google, and a simple keyword search may not work[^tnastarthere-e2]. Finding aids and staff can show you what the catalog cannot.

**Where people go wrong:** searching only by subject and concluding nothing exists when nothing comes up. Archives are arranged by creator[^tnastarthere-e1], and finding aids themselves can contain errors and omissions[^schmidt2016using-e32].

### Step 3: Prepare for the visit: rules, access, time, and money

**How (confirm each item before you go):**
- **Access.** Some archives require a letter of introduction or special permission to get in[^schmidt2016using-e20]. At the UK National Archives, for example, you need a reader's ticket to see original documents, and you must bring specified identity documents[^tnastarthere-e8].
- **Whether the material is there and how to request it.** Tell the staff in advance when you are coming and what you want to see. Material stored off-site can take hours or even days to retrieve[^schmidt2016using-e15]. Cronon and colleagues arrived to find that one box had been lent to another institution, which a phone call beforehand would have revealed[^cronon2009learning-e32]. Also ask how much you can request at a time and when requests can be submitted[^schmidt2016using-e16]; rules and fees for photocopying, scanning, and photography vary between archives[^schmidt2016using-e17].
- **Unprocessed collections** (material the archive has received but not yet arranged and cataloged): get in touch early and ask what the rules for using them are[^schmidt2016using-e28]. The 2012 interview study also notes that such collections call for different preparation[^rutner2012supporting-e15].
- **Time and money.** Both will limit the scope of your project[^cronon2009learning-e40]. Leave room for surprises and plan for a second visit to check and fill gaps[^schmidt2016using-e19]; ask whether the archive offers research grants[^schmidt2016using-e18].
- **Try remote options first.** Ask whether you can pay for scans[^schmidt2016using-e12] or hire a research assistant to look for you[^schmidt2016using-e13]; staff will often answer simple questions directly[^schmidt2016using-e14]. Interviewees in the 2012 study said that when they could not travel to an archive, they relied on archivists through many phone calls and emails[^rutner2012supporting-e12].

**Why:** if you arrive without the right paperwork, or the material cannot be retrieved, the trip is wasted.

**Where people go wrong:** packing the schedule so tightly that there is no time to follow new leads. The SAA archives guide recommends leaving time for the unexpected[^schmidt2016using-e19].

### Step 4: Work in the reading room

**How:**
- **Follow the rules, and know why they exist.** Use pencil only, because if you accidentally mark a document, pencil can be erased[^schmidt2016using-e21]. Keep material in the order you received it, so it can be found again and read in its original context[^schmidt2016using-e22].
- **Start with what matters most.** Request the most useful material first so you are sure to get through it[^schmidt2016using-e29].
- **Give every note and every photo a full citation**: the collection name and the number the archive assigned[^schmidt2016using-e30], plus box and folder numbers[^schmidt2016using-e34]. For photography tips, and the cost of photographing without thinking, see the [History and archaeology field overview](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md#working-in-an-archive).
- **Read the margins.** Many letters take on another layer of meaning because of marginal notes[^cronon2009learning-e37]. A question mark will not prove an argument, but it can make you see other evidence from a new angle[^cronon2009learning-e38].
- **Follow leads and record gaps.** Documents point you to where to look next[^cronon2009learning-e39]. Write down gaps and uncertainties too, because later research may bring new leads[^cronon2009learning-e36].

**Why:** a full citation lets you find the original again, and lets your readers trace it[^schmidt2016using-e31].

**Where people go wrong:** skipping "irrelevant" files. Cronon and colleagues found they had three times passed over files that interested them because they seemed "unrelated to the question"; in fact those files raised many questions that they had not let themselves ask[^cronon2009learning-e34].

### Step 5: Scrutinize every source

The history and archaeology field overview already lists the basic questions: who wrote it, whether it really dates from that period, and who it was written for. The literacy guidelines expand these into a fuller practice:
1. **Say what it is first**: how it was produced, by whom, when, and what it is[^saaacrl2018primary-e11].
2. **Ask about perspective and purpose**: how the creator's tone, subjectivity, and biases relate to the source's original purpose and audience[^saaacrl2018primary-e13].
3. **Look at the physical object**: the source's physical and material characteristics belong in your interpretation too[^saaacrl2018primary-e15].
4. **Know which version you have**: what you are reading may be an excerpt, a transcription, or a translation[^saaacrl2018primary-e12].
5. **Authentic does not mean reliable**: a record can be genuine and its content still be unreliable[^saaacrl2018primary-e20].
6. **Think about who has already selected for you**: between the source's creation and your reading of it, archivists and database designers have acted as intermediaries[^saaacrl2018primary-e3]; finding aids, catalogs, and the way material is arranged are forms of mediation too[^saaacrl2018primary-e27]. For example, folders sorted by subject can hide what a person was doing at the same time on other matters; only by stringing the material together by date did Cronon and colleagues see the fuller picture[^cronon2009learning-e43][^cronon2009learning-e44].

**Silences in the archive.** The archive is not the whole past:
- The US National Archives and Records Administration says that only 1% to 3% of all the documents and materials created in the course of US federal government business are kept permanently because of their legal or historical value[^naragettingstarted-e1]. Cronon and colleagues also point out that almost everything ever written never made it into an archive[^cronon2009learning-e36].
- The literacy guidelines say that the collections of cultural heritage institutions reflect and reinforce society's power structures[^saaacrl2018primary-e5]. "Silences" are gaps in the historical record, often because some people could not create records, or their records were not valued or were suppressed; that is different from a particular archive simply not collecting that kind of material[^saaacrl2018primary-e21].
- So ask: which sources were never created, which no longer exist, and which were collected[^saaacrl2018primary-e4]; and what explains the gaps, contradictions, and traces of power relations[^saaacrl2018primary-e14].

**Why:** the professional standards of the American Historical Association (AHA) say historical work must discuss what sources tell us **and also what they leave in the dark**[^aha2023standards-e24].

**Where people go wrong:** treating "not recorded in the archive" as "did not happen", and judging people of the past by today's standards (presentism; see the [common mistakes in the History and archaeology field overview](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md#common-mistakes)).

### Step 6: Organize and cite

**How:**
- **Record citations in the format the repository recommends.** The sample finding aid in the SAA archives guide gives the citation format "item, folder, box, collection name, repository"[^schmidt2016using-e34]. The literacy guidelines also say to follow the repository's citation conventions where possible[^saaacrl2018primary-e17].
- **Choose a citation style.** The notes-bibliography system of *The Chicago Manual of Style* can handle unusual sources that do not fit the author-date system[^chicago2024citation-e3]; ask your advisor which system to use[^chicago2024citation-e5].
- **Mark any change you make to the original text**, in digital and print versions alike[^aha2023standards-e22]. Digitized records are easy to copy, which makes accurate citation all the more important[^aha2023standards-e23]. Also state whether you cite the original, a microfilm, or an online digital version.
- **Do not cite secondhand as if you saw the source yourself.** The AHA considers it improper to borrow a primary-source citation from someone else's book without acknowledging that book[^aha2023standards-e11].
- **Organize your photos and notes.** The literacy guidelines list transcriptions, photographs, and downloads as research material you need a way to manage[^saaacrl2018primary-e7]. For concrete practice, see [Organizing research materials](../08_throughout/organizing_materials.md#5-keep-three-kinds-of-notes-apart) under Throughout the project.

**Why:** the AHA standards expect you to be ready to make your sources and evidence available[^aha2023standards-e15]; with incomplete citations you cannot do that.

### Step 7: Use digitized archives well, and know their limits

**Digitized archives save time, but they are not everything.**
- The SAA archives guide says digital collections are often only a small part of an archive's holdings[^schmidt2016using-e7]. The UK National Archives' website states that its online records are a little over 5% of its holdings (the page is undated)[^tnastarthere-e4]; another portion has been digitized by commercial companies and usually costs money to view[^tnastarthere-e5]. When browsing online, you may not be able to tell whether you are seeing a whole collection or part of one; go up to the top-level collection to see the full picture[^schmidt2016using-e8].
- Distinguish two kinds of websites: catalogs that offer information *about* sources, and sites that offer digital versions of the sources themselves[^saaacrl2018primary-e10]. Digital collections are selected too, and reflect many people's decisions about what to select, reproduce, and present[^saaacrl2018primary-e6].

**Keyword search has blind spots.** In the 2012 US study of 39 interviewees, some worried that the searchable material was not comprehensive[^rutner2012supporting-e16], and those working on international topics noted that Google Books has limited foreign-language material[^rutner2012supporting-e17]. A better approach is to use digitized archives and online finding aids to **locate** material, then visit archives with a clear target. In the same study, most interviewees said digitized finding aids and collections had not reduced their travel, but had made it more targeted[^rutner2012supporting-e13].

### Step 8: Handle restricted records, privacy, and copyright

**Why records are restricted.** The SAA archives guide (which describes the US situation) gives two kinds of reasons: donors set time limits or conditions, usually because of privacy or sensitive material[^schmidt2016using-e25]; and the law requires it, as with US census records, which are closed for 72 years[^schmidt2016using-e26]. Sometimes you can apply for permission to see them[^schmidt2016using-e27]. The literacy guidelines add that archivists may redact parts of records because of law, donor agreements, or professional ethics[^saaacrl2018primary-e23].

**How:**
- **Honor the conditions you agreed to.** The AHA accepts restrictions based on national security, proprietary rights, and privacy as legitimate, and opposes unnecessary restrictions[^aha2023standards-e21]. Historians sometimes legitimately agree to conditions on the use of certain sources[^aha2023standards-e26], and they must keep the commitments they make[^aha2023standards-e8]; they also do nothing that would prevent later researchers from accessing the sources[^aha2023standards-e10].
- **Consider the effect on the people involved.** The literacy guidelines ask you to respect privacy and cultural context when using sources and to comply with copyright and privacy law[^saaacrl2018primary-e18], and to consider how your research may affect the creators, donors, and owners of the sources[^saaacrl2018primary-e19]. Even when a source is in the public domain (no longer protected by copyright), privacy still has to be weighed[^saaacrl2018primary-e26].
- **Owning the original is not owning the copyright.** An archive that owns the physical document does not necessarily own its copyright[^schmidt2016using-e23]. According to the SAA archives guide, in the US it is the researcher's responsibility to find the copyright holder before publishing or quoting such material[^schmidt2016using-e24]; unpublished material copied for you may need separate permission before you publish it[^schmidt2016using-e35]. Copyright law differs between countries[^saaacrl2018primary-e24]; for the rules in your country, ask your university's copyright office or library.

### Step 9: Build an argument from fragments

**How:**
- **Synthesize multiple sources.** The literacy guidelines call for examining and synthesizing multiple sources to build, support, or refute an argument[^saaacrl2018primary-e16]. How strong the evidence is, how it was gathered, and how it is used to support a claim all affect how credible the conclusion is[^saaacrl2018primary-e25].
- **Corroborate**: for any key statement, try to check it against records left by different people and different organizations. For one meeting, for example, read both the organizer's minutes and the participants' letters.
- **String it together in time**: careful attention to dates can reveal unexpected connections[^cronon2009learning-e43].
- **Do not cherry-pick.** Do not misrepresent sources, and do not leave out evidence that runs against your interpretation[^aha2023standards-e25].
- **State the limitations.** The records of the past are fragmentary, and absolute historical knowledge is impossible[^aha2023standards-e14]. Put what you looked for and did not find, the silences in the archive, and how they limit your conclusions in the text or the notes.

**Why:** putting your evidence in conversation with your research question may be the most important part of the research process[^cronon2009learning-e45].

**Where people go wrong:** the evidence you actually find is often not as good as you imagined[^cronon2009learning-e42]. When that happens, narrowing your conclusion is safer than propping up a big argument.

## 3. Checklist

Our sources contain no reporting standard specific to archival research. The list below draws on the AHA standards, the SAA archives guide, and the literacy guidelines; the grouping into tiers is our own.

**Must do**
- [ ] Every archival source you cite has a full citation (repository, collection name, box number, folder number or identifier) that lets someone else find the original[^schmidt2016using-e30][^schmidt2016using-e34][^schmidt2016using-e31]
- [ ] Every change to the original text is marked[^aha2023standards-e22]
- [ ] No evidence that runs against your interpretation has been left out[^aha2023standards-e25]
- [ ] When you borrowed a primary-source citation from someone else's work, you acknowledged that work[^aha2023standards-e11]
- [ ] You kept the commitments you made[^aha2023standards-e8], and dealt with copyright and permissions before publishing[^schmidt2016using-e24][^schmidt2016using-e35]
- [ ] For every key source you asked: who, when, why, and for whom[^saaacrl2018primary-e11][^saaacrl2018primary-e13]

**Better practice**
- [ ] You discussed the archive's silences and gaps and how they limit your conclusions[^saaacrl2018primary-e14][^aha2023standards-e24]
- [ ] You stated whether you used the original or another version, such as an excerpt, transcription, or translation[^saaacrl2018primary-e12]
- [ ] Key statements draw on multiple sources[^saaacrl2018primary-e16]
- [ ] You considered how your research affects the people in the sources[^saaacrl2018primary-e19]

**Fine to skip; not a flaw**
- Not visiting in person, and using the archive's scans or having someone look for you instead: the SAA archives guide lists these as remote options you can ask an archive about[^schmidt2016using-e12][^schmidt2016using-e13]. But say in your notes which version you looked at.

## 4. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Searching archives by subject keywords and concluding nothing exists when nothing turns up | Archives are arranged by the person or organization that created them[^tnastarthere-e1], and their catalogs are not like Google[^tnastarthere-e2] |
| Looking only at what has been digitized online | Digital collections are often a small part of the holdings[^schmidt2016using-e7]; the UK National Archives has put a little over 5% of its holdings online[^tnastarthere-e4] |
| Setting off without contacting the archive | Material may be in off-site storage and take days to arrive[^schmidt2016using-e15], or may even have been lent to another institution[^cronon2009learning-e32] |
| Taking photos or notes without recording citations | You cannot find the original again, and neither can your readers[^schmidt2016using-e31] |
| Disturbing the order of the material | Others cannot find it later, and it loses its original context[^schmidt2016using-e22] |
| Treating what an archival document says as fact | A record can be genuine and its content still be unreliable[^saaacrl2018primary-e20] |
| Treating "not in the archive" as "did not happen" | Only 1%–3% of US federal government documents are kept permanently[^naragettingstarted-e1], almost everything ever written never reached an archive[^cronon2009learning-e36], and some people's records were ignored or suppressed[^saaacrl2018primary-e21] |
| Assuming that because the archive gave you a copy, you can publish it freely | Owning the original is not owning the copyright[^schmidt2016using-e23]; unpublished material may need separate permission before publication[^schmidt2016using-e35] |
| Changing topic after the first document, or clinging to the original question | Let the question adjust through your conversation with the documents, but do not give it up lightly[^cronon2009learning-e35] |

## 5. Further reading

- **SAA, *Using Archives: A Guide to Effective Research*** (by Laura Schmidt; free web page, written for US readers): from finding archives and remote requests to planning a visit, reading-room rules, copyright, and restricted material, with an annotated sample finding aid. Read it through before your first archive visit.
- **SAA and ACRL, *Guidelines for Primary Source Literacy*** (2018, free PDF): how to ask questions about, find, read, evaluate, and use primary sources, with a glossary ("silences", "mediation", "authenticity" and more). Useful for checking whether your source criticism goes far enough.
- **Cronon et al., the Manuscripts page of *Learning to Do Historical Research***: uses a real collection to show how to find collections, read files in the reading room, and follow leads.
- **The UK National Archives' Start here section** and **the US National Archives and Records Administration's Getting Started page**: each institution's own introduction to what is online, what is not, and how to prepare a visit.
- **AHA, *Statement on Standards of Professional Conduct***: the American Historical Association's professional standards on citation, marking changes, access restrictions, and commitments.
- For what is distinctive about how literary scholars use archives, see the [Languages and literature field overview](../07_fields/languages-literature/README.md#literary-studies-archives-and-scholarly-editions).
