# Process tracing and comparative case studies

This page covers two closely linked qualitative methods in political science. The first is **process tracing**: working step by step through how something happened inside a single case, in order to test or develop a causal explanation. The second is the **comparative case study**: deliberately choosing a small number of cases and comparing them. The main sources for this page are political science methodology texts. Bennett and Checkel note that contemporary political scientists and sociologists agree that answering many questions requires combining the structural and institutional context of society with individual decision-making[^bennett2012process-e1]; they also see process tracing as closely related to what the historian Clayton Roberts calls "historical explanation"[^bennett2012process-e6]. Comparative-historical research in sociology often borrows these practices too; for how that work is judged, see the [Sociology field overview](../07_fields/sociology/README.md#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence) `[Our analysis]`.

For the different research traditions in political science, see the [Political science field overview](../07_fields/political-science/README.md#several-research-traditions-side-by-side). General case study practice (defining the case, corroborating across multiple sources, keeping a chain of evidence, analytic generalization) is covered under research methods in [Case study and ethnography](case_study.md), and this page does not repeat it. For how to work in an archive, see [Archival research](archival_research.md); for the general interview process, see [Interview studies](interview_study.md).

**Scope** `[Our analysis]`: this page was first written for the Political science field. Its sources are mainly political science methodology texts, plus documents on ethics and transparency from the American Political Science Association and political science journal editors. Besides [Political science](../07_fields/political-science/README.md), the field overviews for [History and archaeology](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md) and [Sociology](../07_fields/sociology/README.md) also link to this page. Other fields that use it may have different expectations for case selection and evidence transparency; check your own field's standards before you start.

**Strategies it usually serves**: [Causal explanation](../05_research-strategies/explain_causality.md#3-common-research-methods).

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- explain how process tracing differs from "telling the story in chronological order", turn each candidate explanation into a list of "what we should expect to see", and use the four tests to judge how much weight each piece of evidence carries;
- use archives, memoirs, and elite interviews critically;
- select cases for a comparative study and avoid the trap of "choosing only cases with the same outcome";
- write a process-tracing paper that readers can check.

## 1. What this kind of research is, and when to use it

**Definition.** Collier defines process tracing as the systematic selection and analysis of diagnostic evidence, guided by the research questions and hypotheses the researcher has posed[^collier2011understanding-e1]. Bennett and Checkel are more specific: it is the analysis of processes, sequences, and conjunctures of events within a case, for the purpose of developing or testing hypotheses about causal mechanisms[^bennett2012process-e3]. It can run in two directions: start from existing theory and test for the traces that theory should leave in the case, or start from evidence in the case and inductively develop new hypotheses[^bennett2012process-e4]. A **case study**, in Gerring's definition, is the intensive study of a single unit (a country, a reform, a war) with the aim of generalizing to a larger set of similar units[^gerring2004case-e1].

**What evidence it uses.** Statistical analysis uses "data set observations": the values a set of cases takes on a set of variables. Process tracing uses "causal process observations": individual observations about context, process, or mechanism[^bennett2012process-e5]. For example, meeting minutes showing that the minister read a certain report before making the decision are a causal process observation `[Our analysis]`.

**Origins.** According to Bennett and Checkel's account, the term "process tracing" comes from American cognitive psychology in the late 1960s or early 1970s; in 1979 the political scientist Alexander George borrowed it to mean using within-case evidence to infer historical explanations[^bennett2012process-e2]. Compared with historians' "historical explanation", they stress one difference: the hypotheses about causal mechanisms should be stated explicitly so they can be rigorously tested, even if this makes the narrative less smooth[^bennett2012process-e7]. In their words, process tracing is neither "glorified historiography" nor does it follow the logic of frequentist statistics[^bennett2012process-e8].

**When it fits.** Gerring argues that case studies are more useful in seven situations, including: when the inference is descriptive; when depth matters more than breadth; when you care more about causal mechanisms than about the size of an effect; when the research is exploratory; and when useful variation exists in only a few units[^gerring2004case-e7]. He also points out that research design always trades off knowing a lot about a few units against knowing a little about many[^gerring2004case-e2]. Cells in the table below without a footnote are our own summary `[Our analysis]`.

| | Process tracing (within one case) | Comparative case study (a few cases) | Large-N statistics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question answered | How did this happen here? Which explanation is right? | Why do these cases have different (or the same) outcomes? | How much does X affect Y on average? |
| Main evidence | Causal process observations | Comparison across cases, plus process tracing within each case | Data set observations |
| Strengths | Gerring: better at revealing causal mechanisms[^gerring2004case-e4] | Can control for some other factors | Effect size, representativeness |
| Weaknesses | Gerring: often weak on representativeness[^gerring2004case-e3] | Even the most similar cases rarely differ in only one factor[^bennett2012process-e42] | The mechanism is often a black box |

The three can be combined. Bennett and Checkel give one combination: pick a few cases from a statistical analysis and process-trace them to check whether the causal direction is reversed or the correlation is spurious[^bennett2012process-e24]. For the general approach to multi-method designs, see [Mixed methods](mixed_methods.md).

## 2. How to do it

### Step 1: Gather all the candidate explanations, and treat them evenhandedly

**How:** Bennett and Checkel's first standard: at a minimum, test the explanations that political scientists, sociologists, historians, regional specialists, and subject-matter experts have offered for the case; claims made by participants and journalists can also be turned into theoretical explanations and tested[^bennett2012process-e26]. Then check for gaps against the broad families of explanation: material power, institutional constraints and opportunities, and social norms or legitimacy[^bennett2012process-e27]. **Why:** if you leave out an explanation a reader would think of right away, the whole process-tracing account loses its persuasiveness[^bennett2012process-e25].

**Where people go wrong:** giving your favored explanation a "first-mover advantage": tracing only that one and turning to other explanations only when something does not fit. A better approach is to write down each explanation's predictions in advance and then look at the evidence for and against each one in turn[^bennett2012process-e29]. Treating them evenhandedly does not mean digging equally deep into every one: some explanations are irrelevant to the case, and some can be ruled out in a footnote with one or two pieces of obvious evidence[^bennett2012process-e28].

### Step 2: Translate each explanation into "what we should expect to see"

**How:** Collier suggests starting with a timeline or narrative that sets out the sequence of events, then drawing causal ideas from it and working out what evidence you need[^collier2011understanding-e12]. He also stresses solid description: first describe the situation clearly at a series of key moments (take good "snapshots"), and only then discuss change and sequence[^collier2011understanding-e3]. Then ask of each explanation: if it is true, who should have known, said, or done what, and when? Who should have been in contact with whom, worried about whom, allied with whom[^bennett2012process-e44]?

**Why:** theories are usually stated in general terms and rarely spell out "what we should see in this case"; you have to do that translation yourself[^bennett2012process-e17]. The more clearly you state your hypotheses, the better you know what evidence to look for, how to interpret it, and how to weigh it[^collier2011understanding-e4].

**An illustration** `[Our analysis]`: you want to explain why a country adopted a reform. Explanation A is "pressure from international organizations"; then you should see that the pressure came before the reform decision, and that the decision makers' internal discussions mention this pressure. Explanation B is "the ruling party wanted to win the next election"; then you should see that the reform's design favored key constituencies, and that internal party documents discussed votes. Both lists are written before you start going through the archives.

### Step 3: Use the four tests to judge the weight of each piece of evidence

Collier, and Bennett and Checkel, all use the following set of four tests (many of the examples come from Van Evera). To judge how much weight a piece of evidence carries, ask two things: if the hypothesis is true, is the evidence **certain to appear**; and is it predicted **only by this hypothesis**[^bennett2012process-e10][^bennett2012process-e12]? This yields four tests:

| Test | Passing means | Failing means | Notes or example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straw-in-the-wind test | Slightly raises plausibility | Slightly lowers plausibility | Neither necessary nor sufficient; only slightly weakens rivals[^collier2011understanding-e6]; any single one is not very decisive[^bennett2012process-e14] |
| Hoop test (the hypothesis has to "jump through the hoop" to stay in the running) | It merely stays under consideration | The hypothesis is eliminated[^collier2011understanding-e7] | Was the defendant in the state on the day of the crime?[^bennett2012process-e10] |
| Smoking-gun test | Strong support | Does not disconfirm | Being caught holding a smoking gun; someone not holding a gun is not necessarily innocent[^collier2011understanding-e8] |
| Doubly decisive test | Confirms the hypothesis and eliminates the others | Eliminated | A bank camera that recorded the faces of all the robbers[^bennett2012process-e13] |

**Key points:**

- **Weigh the evidence, don't count it.** The probative value of evidence relative to rival explanations matters more than the number of pieces[^bennett2012process-e9]. Once evidence on the same step is repetitive enough to be predictable, more of it does nothing to distinguish between explanations[^bennett2012process-e15].
- **Single pieces of doubly decisive evidence are rare.** Collier says these are uncommon in the social sciences, but you can combine several tests: some eliminate rivals, others support the remaining explanation; once every plausible rival is eliminated, what remains is the answer[^collier2011understanding-e9][^collier2011understanding-e10]. Bennett and Checkel add that a series of straw-in-the-wind tests can also raise confidence in an explanation, just as a suspect can be convicted on many pieces of circumstantial evidence[^bennett2012process-e14]. Also, when the condition for passing a hoop test is common anyway, passing it tells you little[^bennett2012process-e11].
- **The classification is not fixed.** Which test a piece of evidence counts as depends on your prior knowledge, your research assumptions, and the exact wording of the hypothesis[^collier2011understanding-e5]. So write down the reasoning behind your judgment in the paper `[Our analysis]`.
- **Not finding evidence does not necessarily mean it is not there.** If the actors deliberately concealed it, failing to find evidence does not much lower the chance that it exists[^bennett2012process-e23]; evidence for socially stigmatized views is hard to find in the first place[^bennett2012process-e49].

### Step 4: Decide where to start and where to stop

**Where to start.** There is no single answer; it depends on how you define the outcome you are explaining[^bennett2012process-e35]. A reasonable starting point is a **critical juncture**: a moment when an institution or practice could have gone down different paths, and actors or external events decided which one it took[^bennett2012process-e36]. Explain in the paper why you start there `[Our analysis]`. **Where to stop:** stop when a type of evidence has become so repetitive that collecting more is unlikely to change your judgment of the explanations[^bennett2012process-e40]. But evidence you expect to have high probative value is worth working hard for: a single meeting or a single memo can settle the question[^bennett2012process-e37]. **Where people go wrong:** underestimating the time. Wood's study of the civil war in El Salvador took fifteen years from start to finish; think through your funding and time constraints before you begin[^bennett2012process-e39].

### Step 5: Use your sources critically

For the practical work in the archive, and how to scrutinize the provenance and reliability of each source, see [Archival research](archival_research.md#step-5-scrutinize-every-source). Here we cover only the points that matter especially for process tracing:

- **Ask "who said this, and why".** The person providing the evidence may want to sell a particular explanation. First discount the evidence according to their likely motives, then use what they said to infer their motives in turn[^bennett2012process-e30].
- **Context changes the weight.** Spontaneous remarks differ from prepared speeches, and public statements differ from private or confidential ones[^bennett2012process-e31]. Also find out who actually wrote a public statement[^bennett2012process-e48].
- **Archives have their own selection bias.** A side that has newly come to power and controls the archives often releases only bad news about its opponents and good news about itself[^bennett2012process-e32].
- **Secondary works select their sources too.** Historians may also pick only the sources that support their argument, so consult several secondary works representing different historiographical schools[^bennett2012process-e33].
- **Triangulation is not a cure-all.** Documents, press reports, and interviews that look like independent sources may all trace back to the same one or two people trying to sell a particular explanation[^bennett2012process-e38].
- **Cite down to the page.** This is a recommendation from political science's Qualitative Transparency Deliberations (summarized by Jacobs, Büthe, et al. in 2021): make it possible for others to find the exact passage you used[^jacobs2021qualitative-e16], and explain where sources left by others came from and how you used them critically[^jacobs2021qualitative-e20].

### Step 6: Use elite interviews as evidence

Elite interviews are interviews with officials, legislators, diplomats, and leaders of parties and organizations `[Our analysis]`. For the general interview process, see [Interview studies](interview_study.md); for ethical issues, see the [Political science field overview](../07_fields/political-science/README.md#ethics-doing-research-in-real-political-settings). When you use interviews as evidence for process tracing, keep these in mind:

- **Discount what interviewees say, too.** The analysis of motives in Step 5 applies here as well[^bennett2012process-e30]; officials often have reasons to embellish their own role in events `[Our analysis]`.
- **Check recollections against documents.** Recollections backed by documents are more credible than those without[^bennett2012process-e48]. You can compare actors' public statements with private discussions they did not expect to become public any time soon[^bennett2012process-e47].
- **Use interviews to predict material not yet released.** First predict what confidential archives will show once declassified; a prediction that comes true is strong evidence[^bennett2012process-e20]. Checkel made predictions based on interviews and primary documents that were later confirmed by declassified meeting records[^bennett2012process-e21].
- **Be clear about confidentiality.** The American Political Science Association's 2020 principles say that when studying powerful elites, you may choose not to promise confidentiality, but you must make this clear to interviewees[^apsa2020humansubjects-e19]. In other countries and institutions, local rules apply `[Our analysis]`.
- **Keep interview records in a checkable form.** Make an interview table listing the key information for every interview[^jacobs2021qualitative-e21]; when you quote an answer, give the full answer to that question; when you cannot publish the full text, report how many interviews fit the hypothesis and how many did not[^jacobs2021qualitative-e17].
- **Whom to interview.** Mendez 2020, citing Tansey 2007, says that if you care about a specific decision made by a few people, random sampling leaves it to chance; Mendez goes on to say that a growing number of methodologists accept selecting interviewees non-randomly[^mendez2020enlisting-e2]. In his study of Plan Colombia in the United States and Colombia, Mendez used snowball sampling (asking each interviewee to introduce the next) and reached more than half of the known decision makers; he considers the key to be building relationships with the first few interviewees[^mendez2020enlisting-e1]. This is one researcher's experience, not a systematic study `[Our analysis]`.
- **How to request an interview.** An undated tip sheet on elite interviewing from the Duke Initiative on Survey Methodology at Duke University in the US advises: first send a formal request letter or email explaining the gist of the research, how much time it will take, and how the conversation will be recorded and used, then follow up by phone; you can also ask interviewees to help you contact other interviewees[^clifford2020tipsheet-e1]. Keep the length moderate, for example 20 to 45 minutes[^clifford2020tipsheet-e2]. Mendez advises using your institutional email or letterhead for all correspondence[^mendez2020enlisting-e3]; for other details, follow your advisor and the conventions of your subfield.

### Step 7: Take surprises and disconfirming evidence seriously

- **Pay attention to the feeling of surprise**, and try to explain surprising facts theoretically[^bennett2012process-e43].
- **If you revise a hypothesis, test it on new evidence.** The new evidence can come from the same case, but it must be independent of the evidence that led you to the hypothesis[^bennett2012process-e18][^bennett2012process-e19].
- **When you meet disconfirming evidence, the bigger the change you make, the more you need new tests**; otherwise you end up with a post hoc story that explains away anomalies one by one[^bennett2012process-e22]. Bennett and Checkel sum up the central challenge as avoiding "lazy mechanism-based storytelling"[^bennett2012process-e50].
- **Moving from exploration to testing is common, but say so honestly.** Seawright and Gerring warn that a design suited to exploration is not necessarily suited to testing[^seawright2008case-e15]; you can state openly that the initial research was exploratory[^seawright2008case-e16], and report which hypotheses were dropped, revised, or newly added[^jacobs2021qualitative-e23].

### Step 8: When comparing, select cases purposively

**How:** Seawright and Gerring consider case selection the first task: the cases you choose determine how you study them[^seawright2008case-e1]. There are two goals: representativeness, and useful variation on the dimensions you care about[^seawright2008case-e6]. They list seven selection techniques[^seawright2008case-e7]; the table below takes four of them, and the last row comes from Bennett and Checkel:

| Technique | What kind of case | What it is good for |
|---|---|---|
| Typical case | Fits a general cross-case pattern | Probing the mechanism behind the pattern[^seawright2008case-e8] |
| Deviant case | A case that "shouldn't happen" under the general pattern and that it cannot explain[^seawright2008case-e11] | Searching for new explanations[^seawright2008case-e12] |
| Most-similar cases | Differ only on the factor you care about[^seawright2008case-e13] | Seeing what that factor does `[Our analysis]` |
| Most-different cases | Only the factor you care about varies together with the outcome; all other possible factors differ | Analysts generally consider it weaker than the most-similar design[^seawright2008case-e14] |
| Most-likely / least-likely case | A case where the theory should most / least hold | If the theory fails in the first or succeeds in the second, that is where you most need to revise your views[^bennett2012process-e34] |

**Why not sample at random.** Seawright and Gerring's simulations show that with five cases or fewer, randomly drawn samples are often clearly unrepresentative of the population[^seawright2008case-e5]. **Where people go wrong:**

- **Saying only "convenience".** Time, money, and access are legitimate reasons, but they are not methodological reasons for choosing A over B[^seawright2008case-e3]. Afterward, you still need to establish where these cases sit in the population[^seawright2008case-e4], and report what you already knew when you chose the cases and which cases nearly made the cut[^jacobs2021qualitative-e22]. For general purposive selection, see [Case studies](case_study.md#step-3-select-cases-purposefully-and-say-why).
- **Believing that most-similar cases really differ in only one factor.** This can rarely be achieved; use process tracing to show that the other differences cannot explain the outcome[^bennett2012process-e42].

**The biggest trap: choosing only cases with the same outcome (selecting on the dependent variable).** Geddes noted in 1990 that studying only a few revolutions in order to understand revolutions was one of the most enduring conventions in her field[^geddes1990cases-e1]. The problem, she argues, is that if you study only A and B, which share the same outcome, you get only half the information, namely what they have in common[^geddes1990cases-e3]; what such a sample can actually explain is only the variation among the selected cases[^geddes1990cases-e4]. Her example: several fast-growing newly industrializing countries all repressed labor, but perhaps other countries repressed labor just as much without developing[^geddes1990cases-e5]; once slow-growing countries were included as well, the apparent relationship disappeared[^geddes1990cases-e6]. **How to avoid it:** include cases with different outcomes too[^geddes1990cases-e6]. Seawright and Gerring consider it acceptable to choose extreme cases, provided you do not treat them as representative of the population[^seawright2008case-e9] and you read them against the larger set of cases behind them[^seawright2008case-e10].

### Step 9: Writing and transparency

- **State your mechanism hypotheses openly**, even if the narrative becomes less smooth; this is Bennett and Checkel's position[^bennett2012process-e7].
- **Explain how the evidence was generated and how the analysis was done.** The summary of the Qualitative Transparency Deliberations reports clear agreement on giving a detailed account of how evidence was generated[^jacobs2021qualitative-e19]. Journals that signed the Journal Editors' Transparency Statement require authors to make clear the analytic steps their conclusions rest on[^dart2014jets-e4]; if data are restricted, you must tell the editor at submission[^dart2014jets-e3]. For the background to this transparency debate, see the [Political science field overview](../07_fields/political-science/README.md#data-access-and-research-transparency-da-rt).
- **You do not have to make all your raw material public.** The working groups of the Qualitative Transparency Deliberations agreed that sharing qualitative data should not be a blanket requirement[^jacobs2021qualitative-e15]. You can use tools such as Annotation for Transparent Inquiry, developed by the Qualitative Data Repository at Syracuse University, to overlay citations, source excerpts, and links to sources on passages of your paper[^jacobs2021qualitative-e18].
- **Say how far your findings generalize**[^jacobs2021qualitative-e23]; the weak point of a single case is often representativeness[^gerring2004case-e3]. **Acknowledge the uncertainty that remains**[^bennett2012process-e45].
- **How much of your reasoning should you spell out?** Formalizing steps such as the probative value of evidence makes it easier for readers to evaluate[^jacobs2021qualitative-e24]; but overly long appendices can bury the main points, and formal Bayesian write-ups may be less readable than narrative[^jacobs2021qualitative-e25]. One option: write the main text as narrative, say which test each piece of key evidence counts as and why, and put the details in an appendix `[Our analysis]`.

> **Where sources disagree: can a single case test a theory?**
> - Gerring holds that confirming or disconfirming theories is not a strength of case studies[^gerring2004case-e5]; but a single case can refute hypotheses of the "necessary condition" or "sufficient condition" type[^gerring2004case-e6].
> - Bennett and Checkel hold that with the right evidence, single-case process tracing can produce a convincing explanation; for many purposes, though, comparing cases is more powerful[^bennett2012process-e41].
>
> In practice `[Our analysis]`: for a single-case study, it is best to choose a "most-likely" or "least-likely" case and to state the scope of your conclusions narrowly; if you want to make a more general claim, add comparison cases or combine the study with statistical analysis.

## 3. Checklist

Bennett and Checkel suggest that their ten standards can serve as a starting point and a checklist[^bennett2012process-e46]. The list below draws on them together with our other sources; the split into three tiers is our own `[Our analysis]`.

**Must do**

- [ ] Listed all the candidate explanations offered by scholars, regional specialists, and participants[^bennett2012process-e26], and checked for gaps against the broad families of power, institutions, and norms[^bennett2012process-e27]
- [ ] Wrote down each explanation's observable implications before going through the sources[^bennett2012process-e44][^bennett2012process-e29]
- [ ] For the key evidence your conclusion depends on, stated which test it counts as and why: the classification depends on the researcher's judgment[^collier2011understanding-e5], so it has to be written down `[Our analysis]`
- [ ] Considered the motives of the people providing sources and the selection bias of archives[^bennett2012process-e30][^bennett2012process-e32]; explained where the tracing starts and why[^bennett2012process-e35][^bennett2012process-e36]
- [ ] Cited archives and documents down to the page[^jacobs2021qualitative-e16]
- [ ] Explained the reasons for case selection and where the cases sit in the population[^seawright2008case-e4]; did not infer causes from cases chosen only because they share the same outcome[^geddes1990cases-e3]
- [ ] Acknowledged the uncertainty that remains[^bennett2012process-e45]

**Better practice**

- [ ] Provided an interview table[^jacobs2021qualitative-e21], and reported how many interviews fit the hypothesis and how many did not[^jacobs2021qualitative-e17]
- [ ] Reported hypotheses that were dropped, revised, or newly added[^jacobs2021qualitative-e23], and cases that nearly made the cut[^jacobs2021qualitative-e22]
- [ ] Predicted not-yet-released evidence in advance, and checked it later[^bennett2012process-e20]
- [ ] Used an annotation tool to link key passages to the original sources[^jacobs2021qualitative-e18]

**Fine to skip; not a flaw**

- Not applying every one of the ten standards: they do not all fit every study[^bennett2012process-e46]
- Ruling out some alternative explanations in a footnote with one or two pieces of evidence[^bennett2012process-e28]
- Not publishing full interview transcripts[^jacobs2021qualitative-e15]
- Not selecting a small number of cases at random[^seawright2008case-e5]

**Criticisms you may face, and how the sources respond** (these criticisms are our own examples `[Our analysis]`)

- "With only one case, you cannot make causal inferences": Bennett and Checkel hold that with the right evidence, single-case process tracing can produce a convincing explanation[^bennett2012process-e41]; but there are other views, see "Where sources disagree" at the end of Section 2
- "You only have a few pieces of evidence": Bennett and Checkel hold that what matters is the weight of the evidence, not the count[^bennett2012process-e9]
- "You selected extreme cases on the dependent variable": Seawright and Gerring hold that this is fine as long as you do not treat them as representative of the population and you read them against the larger set of cases behind them[^seawright2008case-e9][^seawright2008case-e10]

## 4. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Telling the story in chronological order without stating hypotheses or rival explanations | Readers cannot test it; process tracing requires explicit mechanism hypotheses[^bennett2012process-e7] and consideration of alternative explanations[^bennett2012process-e25] |
| Digging deep only into your favored explanation | Gives it a "first-mover advantage"[^bennett2012process-e29] |
| Piling up lots of evidence of the same kind | Repetitive evidence cannot distinguish between rival explanations[^bennett2012process-e15] |
| Treating a passed hoop test as confirmation | A hoop test can only eliminate; passing it does not confirm[^collier2011understanding-e7] |
| Treating officials' accounts and memoirs as fact | The source may have motives to sell a particular explanation[^bennett2012process-e30]; recollections without documentary support are less credible[^bennett2012process-e48] |
| Concluding "it didn't happen" because you found no evidence | The evidence may have been deliberately concealed[^bennett2012process-e23] |
| Choosing only cases with the same outcome | You get only half the information[^geddes1990cases-e3], and the apparent relationship may vanish in a larger sample[^geddes1990cases-e6] |
| Patching the explanation ad hoc whenever disconfirming evidence turns up | It becomes a post hoc story; you need new, independent tests[^bennett2012process-e22] |

## 5. Further reading

- **Collier 2011, Understanding Process Tracing** (PS: Political Science & Politics): walks through the four tests one by one using the Sherlock Holmes story "Silver Blaze", with online teaching exercises[^collier2011understanding-e2]. Read it when you first learn the four tests.
- **Bennett & Checkel 2012, Process Tracing: From Philosophical Roots to Best Practices** (Simon Fraser University working paper; the paper notes that it is a preprint of Chapter 1 of their edited book Process Tracing in the Social Sciences; we have not checked the published version): the definition of process tracing, the four tests, and their ten standards of good practice. Read it when designing your study and checking your own work.
- **Seawright & Gerring 2008, Case Selection Techniques in Case Study Research** (Political Research Quarterly): seven ways to select cases, and how to use large-N data to select them. Read it when you plan a comparative study.
- **Gerring 2004, What Is a Case Study and What Is It Good For?** (American Political Science Review): what case studies are good and not good at. Read it when deciding whether to do a case study.
- **Geddes 1990, How the Cases You Choose Affect the Answers You Get** (we used the University of California, Berkeley working paper version; the published version appeared in Political Analysis): shows with real examples what wrong conclusions you reach by choosing only cases with the same outcome. Read it before you select cases.
- **Jacobs, Büthe, et al. 2021, The Qualitative Transparency Deliberations** (Perspectives on Politics; we used the authors' accepted manuscript): how qualitative research can be transparent while protecting participants, with many concrete practices. Read it when writing and preparing appendices.
