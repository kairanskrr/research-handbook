# Oral history

This page explains how to run an oral history project: choosing a topic, selecting narrators, getting release forms signed, recording, interviewing, transcribing, archiving, and then using interviews as evidence, citing them, and publishing. The ethical principles of oral history (informed consent, review by the narrator, not promising what you can't deliver) are already covered in the [history and archaeology field overview](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md#oral-history-the-narrators-consent-and-control); this page doesn't repeat them and covers only how to do the work.

The main sources for this page are: two documents from the Oral History Association (OHA) in the US, from 2018 and 2019; the 2012 online introduction from the Baylor University Institute for Oral History in the US (one university's teaching materials, called "the Baylor guide" below); guidance from the UK's Oral History Society (OHS); a 2014 essay by Neuenschwander on US law; and a 2002 introductory article by Shopes. So what follows is mostly advice from these few US and UK organizations and authors, not rules that hold everywhere. **Laws, archive systems, and ethics review rules differ in other countries; follow the rules of your own country and institution**.

**Scope**: This page was written for the history and archaeology section, and its sources come from the oral history organizations and historians above. Besides [History and archaeology](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md), the field overviews for [Languages and literature](../07_fields/languages-literature/README.md) and [Sociology](../07_fields/sociology/README.md) also link here. When those fields do interviews, their habits around anonymity, archiving, and ethics review may differ from oral history's; check your own field's conventions before you rely on this page.

**Strategies it usually serves:** [Interpretation and argument](../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md) (interpreting the accounts of people who lived through events, treating them as evidence that needs checking, and reaching your own conclusions). The basis: when interviews conflict with other evidence, historians need to explain the differences[^shopes2002making-e5]; your conclusions can differ from the narrator's, but they must come from properly cited evidence[^oha2018principles-e31]. See Step 10 below for details.

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- Judge whether your question suits oral history, and how oral history differs from ordinary interview research;
- Before you record anything, have your list of narrators, interview outline, equipment, release forms, and repository ready;
- Use open-ended questions, follow-up questions, and silence to produce an interview that can be preserved for the long term;
- Transcribe, create metadata, archive, and use interviews as evidence that needs checking;
- Know where the legal risks lie, such as defamation and subpoenas.

---

## 1. What this kind of study is, and when to use it

The Baylor guide defines oral history as recorded interviews that preserve a person's life story, or their firsthand account of past experiences[^baylor2012introduction-e1]. It has two strengths: you can ask "what did you think at the time," not just "what happened"; and you can ask narrators to explain their own experiences from their own perspective and in their own words[^baylor2012introduction-e2]. People who were there can fill gaps in the written record, and sometimes correct or even overturn it[^baylor2012introduction-e3]. The guide also says oral historians seek out people who may not leave much of a written record, and ask questions no one has asked before[^baylor2012introduction-e4].

**When to use it.** Ask two questions first: is oral history the best way to get this information? And are there people you can find who are able and willing to tell you[^baylor2012introduction-e5]? If the people who lived through it are gone, or the question can be fully answered from archives, don't force it.

**How it differs from ordinary interview research.** For interviewing techniques (how to ask questions, transcribe, and code), see [Interview studies](interview_study.md) among the research methods; for in-depth interviews in sociology, see the [sociology field overview](../07_fields/sociology/README.md). The most important differences (the account enters the public record, and narrators are not anonymous by default) are already covered in the [history and archaeology field overview](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md#oral-history-the-narrators-consent-and-control). The table below adds a few more points:

| | Oral history | Ordinary qualitative interview research |
|---|---|---|
| Narrator's identity | OHA: narrators may, in some circumstances, choose to use a pseudonym[^oha2018principles-e14]; in other words, a pseudonym is the exception | Often anonymous by default |
| Where the recording goes | OHA: into the public record[^oha2018principles-e13], deposited in a repository that will make it publicly available[^oha2018principles-e34] | Often used only for this project's analysis, kept confidential or destroyed as agreed |
| What confidentiality means | The UK OHS cites the duties listed by the UK Data Archive: a duty of confidentiality toward narrators, but not necessarily anonymity[^ohs2019legal-e3] | Usually both confidentiality and anonymity |
| Who holds copyright | OHA statement of ethics: the narrator holds copyright in the original recording, which is not made public without the narrator's formal permission[^oha2018principles-e37] | Usually not an issue |

So an oral history recording is itself a **lasting historical source**, not just raw data for your one paper. You may be the only person who ever records this narrator's story, so the Baylor guide suggests also recording memories beyond your immediate research interest[^baylor2012introduction-e42]. People doing language documentation (recording and archiving a language) face similar archiving and community issues; see the [languages and literature field overview](../07_fields/languages-literature/README.md#linguistics-fieldwork-and-endangered-languages).

## 2. How to do it

### Step 1: Choose a topic, and find a repository first

- **How:** The Baylor guide's criteria for a topic are: background research is feasible, suitable narrators exist, the project can be finished in the time available, and it may yield information not recorded elsewhere[^baylor2012introduction-e7]. At the very start of the project, approach a repository (an archive, a library, or similar) and ask what recording formats they accept, which legal agreements they need, and what accompanying materials they want[^baylor2012introduction-e6]. When choosing a repository, the OHA best practices ask you to check four things: it fits the project's goals, it can preserve the material, it can honor the signed agreements, and it will provide public access[^oha2018principles-e34].
- **Why:** The UK OHS points out that repositories tend not to accept interviews that lack documentation of provenance, permitted uses, copyright status, and the like[^ohs2019legal-e15].
- **Ethics review:** Rules differ by country. The UK OHS says that for interviews done at a UK university, the research plan must go to an ethics committee[^ohs2019legal-e2]. Neuenschwander says that in the US, humanities and social science researchers who must go through an Institutional Review Board (IRB, the university committee that approves research involving people) routinely have to show they have a rigorous informed consent process[^neuenschwander2014legal-e12]. In other countries, ask your institution first.
- **Where people go wrong:** recording a lot first and only then thinking about where to deposit it, and finding that the formats and release forms don't meet the repository's requirements.

### Step 2: Select narrators

- **How:** The OHA asks you to choose narrators by how relevant their firsthand experience is to the topic, while including as diverse a range of voices as possible[^oha2018principles-e15]; taken together, the narrators should present multiple perspectives[^oha2018principles-e16]. The Baylor guide likewise suggests aiming for a representative group of people who can speak from different angles[^baylor2012introduction-e9].
- **Visit once first:** The Baylor guide suggests meeting in person before formal recording if you can, to learn about the person's relevant experience, memory, and stamina; for people who tire easily, several short interviews may yield more than one long one[^baylor2012introduction-e10].
- **Interview one person at a time:** The same guide warns that with an extra person present, the interviewer may hesitate to probe and the narrator may not answer candidly[^baylor2012introduction-e11].
- **Special groups:** The UK OHS recommends that for interviews with children under 16, you get consent from a parent or guardian, and that children aged 13 and over also consent themselves[^ohs2019legal-e5].
- **Where people go wrong:** interviewing only people who are easy to find and articulate, so that the whole collection ends up with a single voice.

### Step 3: Do background research and write an interview outline

- **How:** The OHA asks you to research primary and secondary sources until you are familiar with the narrator, the topic, and the historical context[^oha2018principles-e17], and to be proficient with the recording equipment you will use[^oha2018principles-e36]. For archival research methods, see [Archival research](archival_research.md) and [Working in the archive](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md#working-in-an-archive) in the history and archaeology field overview. The OHA recommends preparing an open-ended outline listing the topics to cover and general questions[^oha2018principles-e18]. The Baylor guide warns against scripting every question word for word: that gets in the way of candid answers and follow-up on details, and makes what should be an open exchange rigid[^baylor2012introduction-e12].
- **Why:** The Baylor guide says background research lets you ask questions that draw out stories, and lets you understand the names and places that come up in the interview[^baylor2012introduction-e8]. Research doesn't stop before the interview, either: research between interviews is for clarifying and verifying, and research afterward is for checking accuracy[^baylor2012introduction-e8].

### Step 4: Explain clearly and get the release forms signed

For the principles of informed consent, see the [history and archaeology field overview](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md#oral-history-the-narrators-consent-and-control). This section covers how to prepare the documents.

- **Keep the two kinds of document separate.** The UK OHS uses two: a "participation agreement" signed before the interview, and a "recording agreement" covering copyright and access conditions, signed after it[^ohs2019legal-e6]. The OHA likewise asks that once both sides agree on how the interview will proceed, the agreement be formally recorded before recording starts[^oha2018principles-e27]; the legal release form is best signed at the end of the interview[^oha2018principles-e25].
- **Who owns the recording.** Before anything is signed, be clear about the following:
  - The OHA's 2019 archiving guidelines say an oral history is jointly created, and until an agreement transfers it, copyright is generally considered to belong to **both the interviewer and the narrator**[^oha2019archiving-e6]. The UK OHS also says both sides hold copyright in the recording[^ohs2019legal-e8], and the narrator can keep or transfer their share[^ohs2019legal-e12].
  - The Baylor guide says the narrator donates the interview to the interviewer, the sponsoring institution, or the repository through a contract or a deed of gift (a document that hands over the interview as a gift); ask the repository which to use[^baylor2012introduction-e26].
  - The OHA's 2019 guidelines also say that ownership of the physical medium (a hard drive, a tape) and the copyright can belong to different people[^oha2019archiving-e10].
- **Spell out the uses.** Neuenschwander (author of *A Guide to Oral History and the Law*) argues in his 2014 essay that clauses like "for such scholarly and educational purposes as the oral history program shall deem appropriate" are too generic in the digital age; the form should list uses such as research, online presentation, media productions, publication, teaching materials, and museum exhibits[^neuenschwander2014legal-e2]. With video and online presentation becoming more common, you also need permission from narrators and interviewers to use their names and likenesses[^neuenschwander2014legal-e3].
- **Write restrictions as specific dates.** The Baylor guide says restrictions that run "for my lifetime" are hard to enforce; ask narrators to give a specific release date[^baylor2012introduction-e29]. The UK OHS asks that restricted content be marked with time codes and a release date[^ohs2019legal-e13].
- **Where people go wrong:** recording without a signed agreement, or forgetting to get it signed afterward. The UK OHS warns that without signed agreements, tracking people down later is usually time-consuming, and once a narrator or interviewer has died or moved away, they often can't be found at all[^ohs2019legal-e7].

> **Where sources disagree: when should the release form be signed?**
> - The Baylor guide (2012): ideally, the release form is signed before a series of interviews begins[^baylor2012introduction-e28].
> - The US OHA (2018): the release form is best signed at the end of the interview[^oha2018principles-e25]; many oral historians prefer to have the rights-transfer release signed after the interview, so they can deal with sensitive material that came up during it[^oha2018principles-e26].
> - The UK OHS (2019): the "recording agreement" covering copyright and access conditions is signed after the interview, but the narrator should be shown it and have it explained before recording starts[^ohs2019legal-e6].
> - **Our suggestion**: before recording, get written consent to take part and let the person see the release form; after the interview, once you both know what was said, have them sign the release that transfers copyright and sets any restrictions. Follow your repository's requirements on the details.

### Step 5: Prepare the equipment and record well

- **How:** The OHA asks for the highest-quality audio or video recording you can manage, with future listeners and long-term preservation in mind[^oha2018principles-e19]; use cross-platform, non-proprietary file formats wherever possible[^oha2018principles-e33]. The Baylor guide (2012) recommends recording uncompressed WAV or AIFF files, at least CD quality (16-bit, 44.1 kHz), and avoiding highly compressed proprietary formats[^baylor2012introduction-e23]. Specific equipment models change quickly, so go by your repository's current requirements.
- **On site:** The OHA recommends a quiet place with few interruptions[^oha2018principles-e20]. The Baylor guide warns you to listen for noises that will end up on the recording, such as chiming clocks, refrigerators, and clattering dishes; before you start, record a short test of both voices, play it back, and then adjust the microphone position[^baylor2012introduction-e25]. Compare built-in and external microphones; practice before you go, test before you start, and protect the recording when you finish[^baylor2012introduction-e24].
- **Record an introduction at the start:** The OHA asks you to record background information at the start of every interview; when giving the location, say only "the narrator's home," not the address[^oha2018principles-e21].

### Step 6: The interview

For general interviewing technique, see [Interview studies](interview_study.md). The oral history sources especially stress the points below (the first comes from the OHA, the rest from the Baylor guide):

- **Ask open-ended questions, then follow up.** Ask the narrator to clarify, expand, and reflect[^oha2018principles-e22] rather than settling for surface answers; at the same time, let narrators talk about what matters to them, in their own way and their own words[^oha2018principles-e23], going beyond the project's focus when needed[^oha2018principles-e24].
- **Don't lead.** Loaded questions reveal your biases. The guide's example: don't ask "Wasn't Sandtown a poor, hard place to grow up in?"; ask "Tell me how you felt about the place where you grew up"[^baylor2012introduction-e13]. Ask one question at a time[^baylor2012introduction-e14].
- **Learn to be silent.** After the narrator finishes, sit quietly for a moment; they will very likely add more. Silence is part of the interview[^baylor2012introduction-e15].
- **Keep your responses quiet and neutral.** Your "mm-hm" will drown out the narrator's voice on the recording; your responses should not signal agreement or disagreement[^baylor2012introduction-e16]. Note unfamiliar words by how they sound and ask for the spelling after the interview, rather than interrupting[^baylor2012introduction-e17].
- **Challenge the account, not the person.** If you think a statement is inaccurate, you can present other accounts you know of and ask the narrator to respond, but don't question their memory or honesty[^baylor2012introduction-e19].
- **Don't turn the recorder off and on.** When sensitive material comes up, tell the narrator that this part can be restricted from public access[^baylor2012introduction-e20].
- **Watch the length.** The guide considers an average of 60 to 90 minutes per session about right; go much longer and attention flags[^baylor2012introduction-e21].
- **Review yourself afterward:** Listen to your own recording and ask: were my questions open-ended? Did I lead? Did I follow up well[^baylor2012introduction-e38]?

### Step 7: Handle trauma and sensitive topics

- **For the narrator:** The Baylor guide says that when a difficult topic comes up, give the narrator time to collect their thoughts[^baylor2012introduction-e18]. The UK OHS says to respect a request to stop or to continue another day; agreeing on an end time or breaks in advance often helps[^ohs2019legal-e9].
- **Flag risky content:** The UK OHS recommends recording passages that might be defamatory or might disclose sensitive information about other people, with time codes, on an "Interview Sensitivity Review Form," and then weighing with the narrator and the project leader whether to make them public[^ohs2019legal-e10]. Until the agreements are settled, everyone handling the material should keep its content confidential[^ohs2019legal-e11].
- **For vulnerable groups:** The OHA says that when interviewing communities worried about surveillance, you may collect only the most basic background information[^oha2018principles-e28]; when depositing in a repository could create legal or physical danger (for example, for undocumented immigrants or survivors of domestic violence), the archiving plan may bypass traditional repositories[^oha2019archiving-e1].
- **For yourself:** The UK OHS page "Looking after yourself" recommends doing no more than one interview a day if you can[^ohs2026wellbeing-e1]; before you go, tell someone whom you are meeting, where, and when you'll be back[^ohs2026wellbeing-e2]; when you expect the topic to be distressing, talk to someone experienced beforehand, and have someone to talk to afterward as well[^ohs2026wellbeing-e3][^ohs2026wellbeing-e4]. People who transcribe and catalog also listen closely to the recordings and need breaks too[^ohs2026wellbeing-e5].
- **When a narrator becomes distressed:** As an example, here is the "distress protocol" template published in 2025 by Charles Darwin University in Australia. It is just one university's template for research involving people of all kinds, adapted from a 2009 article by Draucker and colleagues, not an agreed standard of the oral history community[^cdu2025distress-e6]. It asks you to watch for signs such as crying, tearfulness, or the person going from relaxed to uncomfortable[^cdu2025distress-e1]. When you notice them, first say that you've noticed the person seems uncomfortable and ask whether they can continue; if they confirm they are upset, offer a pause and remind them they can also end the interview; if they want to continue, offer to leave the topic that caused the distress[^cdu2025distress-e2]. If needed, refer them to community support services, mental health professionals, or a crisis line[^cdu2025distress-e3]; at the end of the interview, ask how they are feeling, and contact them again later that day or the next morning[^cdu2025distress-e4]. Each time this happens, make a confidential note of the date, time, signs of distress, and how you responded[^cdu2025distress-e5].
- **Our sources do not cover** how to respond professionally when a narrator is in serious psychological crisis (for example, at risk of self-harm). Discuss this in advance with your institution's ethics committee and your advisor, write a response plan for your own project, and have professional resources ready to refer people to.

### Step 8: Transcribe, index, and ask the narrator to review

- **Estimate the workload first.** The Baylor guide's 2012 estimate: transcribing one hour of recording takes about 10 to 12 hours, depending on recording quality and how complex the interview is[^baylor2012introduction-e30].
- **Change as little as possible.** The Baylor guide asks you to keep the narrator's word choice, grammar, and speech patterns accurately[^baylor2012introduction-e32]. The OHA's 2019 archiving guidelines say both human and automated transcription make errors, and every draft must be checked; before release, check especially that proper names and dates match the recording[^oha2019archiving-e8].
- **A transcript can't replace the recording.** The Baylor guide says transcripts inevitably distort, for example you can't hear sarcasm in them; many oral historians believe researchers should listen to the recordings themselves[^baylor2012introduction-e31].
- **If you can't do a full transcript, make an index.** Mark where each topic occurs, to the minute and second[^baylor2012introduction-e34]; the OHA's 2019 guidelines say an index can stand in when transcription isn't feasible[^oha2019archiving-e9].
- **Ask the narrator to review.** The Baylor guide says narrators can supply the spelling of proper names and clarify passages that were misheard[^baylor2012introduction-e33].

> **Where sources disagree: must the narrator review the transcript?**
> - The US OHA (2018 statement of ethics): oral history ethics **strongly recommends** giving narrators the chance to review the recording or transcript and approve it for release[^oha2018principles-e32].
> - The UK OHS (2019): you can ask narrators to check a summary or transcript, but this is **not a universal or necessary** practice[^ohs2019legal-e14].
> - **Our suggestion**: in the US, or for projects you plan to publish, arrange a review as the OHA does; wherever you are, follow your repository's requirements and whatever you agreed with the narrator.

### Step 9: Back up, create metadata, and archive

- **Back up right away.** For the OHA's requirement, see the [history and archaeology field overview](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md#oral-history-the-narrators-consent-and-control): export the original recordings as soon as possible, make several copies, and store them in separate places[^oha2018principles-e11]. The Baylor guide adds that what you export should be the original, unedited recording[^baylor2012introduction-e41]. The OHA's 2019 guidelines warn that having backups is not the same as archiving[^oha2019archiving-e4].
- **Name things consistently.** The OHA's 2019 guidelines ask you to give each interview and each of its components a unique identifier or name[^oha2019archiving-e13]; the Baylor guide asks for consistent file and folder naming[^baylor2012introduction-e35].
- **Create metadata** (information describing the recording). The OHA asks that, at a minimum, future users can tell who was interviewed, and when and where[^oha2018principles-e29]. The OHA's archiving guidelines divide metadata into five kinds: administrative, descriptive, technical, preservation, and rights and access[^oha2019archiving-e2].
- **Materials to hand over with the recording:** The OHA's 2019 guidelines list the release forms signed by the narrator, the interviewer, and anyone else present[^oha2019archiving-e5]; the Baylor guide lists a list of correct spellings of names and terms, notes on noise and equipment problems, research notes, the outline, and correspondence[^baylor2012introduction-e36].
- **Things can change after release.** The OHA's 2019 guidelines say narrators' views about public access may change: they may ask to loosen or tighten access, or even withdraw the interview[^oha2019archiving-e12]. The UK OHS holds that before you digitize interviews and put them online, you should try to contact the narrators again to confirm[^ohs2019legal-e16]; interviews online should come with terms of use[^ohs2019legal-e17].

### Step 10: Use memory as evidence

What follows draws mainly on an introductory article Shopes wrote in 2002 for "History Matters," a history teaching website at George Mason University. It is one scholar's view, not a rule of any professional association.
- **Assess the narrator and the account first.** Shopes says the first step in assessing an interview is to consider whether the narrator is reliable and whether the account can be verified[^shopes2002making-e2]. Look at the narrator's relationship to the events, what they have at stake in telling a particular version, their physical and mental state then and now, and whether their account is consistent over time[^shopes2002making-e3].
- **Check against other interviews and documents.** You can judge whether a statement is accurate by comparing it with other interviews on the same topic and with relevant documents[^shopes2002making-e4]. The OHA also asks that statements presented as fact be verified as far as possible[^oha2018principles-e8].
- **When they conflict, explain the differences.** When an interview conflicts with other evidence, Shopes says historians need to explain the discrepancies, for example by asking whether later shifts in thinking or popular versions of events have changed people's memories[^shopes2002making-e5]. Shopes's example: Kennedy won only 49.7% of the vote in 1960, but after his assassination nearly two-thirds of Americans remembered voting for him[^shopes2002making-e6].
- **The limits of memory:** Narrators often get names and dates wrong, and merge several events into one[^shopes2002making-e7].
- **The "errors" are evidence too.** Shopes describes Portelli's research on accounts of the death of the Italian worker Trastulli: the narrators changed the facts to make his death less senseless and more comprehensible; in the article's words, errors, inventions, and myths lead us through and beyond facts to their meanings[^shopes2002making-e8]. An interview tells a story about the past in the present, and is shaped by the moment of telling[^shopes2002making-e9].
- **The conclusions are yours.** The OHA statement of ethics says your conclusions can differ from the narrator's, but they must come from properly cited evidence[^oha2018principles-e31].

### Step 11: Cite, publish, and watch for legal risks

- **Citing.** The Baylor guide says oral histories are usually treated as unpublished primary sources; a citation should give enough information for readers to find where the original is held[^baylor2012introduction-e39], for example the narrator, the interviewer, the interview date, and the repository. For the requirement to stay faithful to the narrator's meaning when quoting, see the [history and archaeology field overview](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md#oral-history-the-narrators-consent-and-control); the OHA also asks you to avoid stereotyping and misrepresenting narrators' words[^oha2018principles-e38].
- **Publishing and public projects:** The Baylor guide says you need a signed release that transfers copyright, and you should tell narrators in advance that the recording may be used in productions or publications[^baylor2012introduction-e40].
- **Keep your promises:** The American Historical Association (AHA) Statement on Standards of Professional Conduct says that promises made in oral history interviews must be kept[^aha2023standards-e8]; for its other requirements, such as making interview records available, see the [history and archaeology field overview](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md#history-the-aha-statement-on-standards-of-professional-conduct).

**Legal risks (US).** This part covers US law only and draws mainly on a 2014 essay by Neuenschwander (author of *A Guide to Oral History and the Law*). It is one author's analysis and is not legal advice. He lists four areas: release agreements, protecting restricted interviews, privacy torts, and copyright[^neuenschwander2014legal-e1].

- **A subpoena (a court order to hand over material) can open up "confidential" interviews.** An oral history project at Boston College had promised that interviews would stay confidential until the narrators died[^neuenschwander2014legal-e4], but the US Court of Appeals for the First Circuit held in this case that "the choice to investigate criminal activity belongs to the government and is not subject to veto by academic researchers"[^neuenschwander2014legal-e5][^neuenschwander2014legal-e8]. The project directors had been told to state in every release form that confidentiality applied "to the extent American law allows," but they did not[^neuenschwander2014legal-e6]. According to Neuenschwander, researchers have had some success getting subpoenas quashed in civil cases; in criminal cases, US state and federal courts have taken positions similar to this one[^neuenschwander2014legal-e8]. The OHA also warns that subpoenas and open-records requests can make narrators' restrictions unenforceable[^oha2018principles-e10]. So make sure everyone involved understands the **limits** of confidentiality[^neuenschwander2014legal-e7].
- **Defamation and privacy.** The main privacy torts he lists are defamation, false light, public disclosure of embarrassing private facts, and misappropriation of name or likeness[^neuenschwander2014legal-e9]. To judge defamation risk, ask: is the person being talked about still alive and active? Would readers think less of them? Is there a factual basis? Is there other evidence that corroborates it[^neuenschwander2014legal-e10]? He also argues that most negative statements are not defamatory and the historical record should not be "sanitized"; try to verify first, and only then consider editing, sealing, or deleting[^neuenschwander2014legal-e11]. The OHA statement of ethics asks oral historians to understand legal issues such as defamation and invasion of privacy[^oha2018principles-e30].

**Legal risks (UK).** The UK OHS guidance states explicitly that it is not formal legal advice and that you should consult a lawyer when needed[^ohs2019legal-e1]. It notes that publicly funded organizations and repositories must also bear in mind the UK's Freedom of Information Act[^ohs2019legal-e18]. Our sources do not cover the law in other countries; follow local law and your institution's rules.

## 3. Checklist

**Must do**

- [ ] Choose a repository before the project starts, and find out its formats, agreements, and required accompanying materials[^oha2018principles-e34][^baylor2012introduction-e6]
- [ ] Formally record what both sides have agreed before recording starts[^oha2018principles-e27]; get release forms signed by the narrator and the interviewer, stating which rights are transferred and for which uses[^oha2019archiving-e5][^oha2019archiving-e14], with restrictions marked by time code and release date[^ohs2019legal-e13]
- [ ] Don't promise confidentiality you can't deliver; explain that subpoenas and similar demands can override restrictions[^oha2018principles-e10][^neuenschwander2014legal-e7]
- [ ] Export the original recordings as soon as possible after the interview, and keep several backups in separate places[^oha2018principles-e11]
- [ ] Record metadata such as who was interviewed, when, and where[^oha2018principles-e29]
- [ ] Verify statements treated as fact against other interviews and documents as far as possible[^oha2018principles-e8][^shopes2002making-e4]
- [ ] When citing, give enough information to find the original, and stay faithful to what the narrator meant[^baylor2012introduction-e39][^oha2018principles-e38]

**Better practice**

- [ ] Visit narrators before the formal recording[^baylor2012introduction-e10]
- [ ] Flag risky passages on a sensitivity review form[^ohs2019legal-e10]
- [ ] Make a time-coded index or transcript of each recording[^baylor2012introduction-e34][^oha2018principles-e35]
- [ ] After each interview, listen to the recording and review your questioning[^baylor2012introduction-e38]
- [ ] Arrange breaks and someone to talk to for interviewers and transcribers[^ohs2026wellbeing-e4][^ohs2026wellbeing-e5]

**Fine to skip; not a flaw**

- No full transcript, as long as there is a detailed index[^oha2019archiving-e9]
- Narrators named, not anonymized: as the OHA puts it, the point of oral history is for narrators' accounts to enter the public record[^oha2018principles-e13]
- Collecting only the most basic background information in interviews with vulnerable groups[^oha2018principles-e28], or not depositing them in a traditional repository[^oha2019archiving-e1]

**Common reviewer criticisms that don't hold up**

- "The narrator got details wrong, so this interview is worthless." (This criticism is our own example.) Shopes points out that interviews routinely contain errors[^shopes2002making-e7], and that errors and alterations can themselves reveal how people make sense of events[^shopes2002making-e8]. The right question is whether the author checked against other evidence and explained the differences[^shopes2002making-e5].

## 4. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's a problem |
|---|---|
| Looking for a repository only after recording | Repositories tend not to accept interviews lacking provenance and copyright documentation[^ohs2019legal-e15] |
| No signed release, or one that only says "for scholarly purposes" | It is hard to find people later to sign[^ohs2019legal-e7]; vague use clauses are too ambiguous in the digital age[^neuenschwander2014legal-e2] |
| Promising "absolute confidentiality" or "nothing released until you die" | A subpoena can break the promise[^neuenschwander2014legal-e5]; lifetime restrictions are also hard to enforce[^baylor2012introduction-e29] |
| Scripted questions read off a list, or leading questions | Gets in the way of candid answers and follow-up[^baylor2012introduction-e12], and reveals your biases[^baylor2012introduction-e13] |
| Constant "mm-hm" and "right," or rushing to the next question | Your voice drowns out the narrator's[^baylor2012introduction-e16]; silence often draws out more[^baylor2012introduction-e15] |
| Reading only the transcript, never listening to the recording | Transcripts inevitably distort; you can't hear sarcasm, for example[^baylor2012introduction-e31] |
| Keeping only one copy, or assuming backups count as archiving | The OHA asks for several copies stored separately[^oha2018principles-e11]; backup is not archiving[^oha2019archiving-e4] |
| Taking what narrators say as fact | Narrators often get names and dates wrong[^shopes2002making-e7]; you need to check against other evidence[^shopes2002making-e4] |

## 5. Further reading

- **US OHA, *Principles and Best Practices* (2018)**: the statement of ethics and best practices, covering everything from preparation and interviewing to preservation and use; read it through before you start.
- **US OHA, *Archives Principles and Best Practices* (2019)**: covers metadata, preservation, access, copyright, and ownership; read it when you prepare to archive.
- **Baylor University Institute for Oral History, *Introduction to Oral History* (2012)**: a university's online teaching guide, one topic per page; most useful for its example questions and its tips for the interview itself. The equipment section dates from 2012 and is out of date.
- **UK OHS, *Is your oral history legal and ethical?* (2019)**: covers UK legal and ethical requirements stage by stage: preparation, first approach, during the interview, after the interview, and archiving.
- **Neuenschwander, "Major Legal Challenges Facing Oral History in the Digital Age" (2014)**: an essay on US release forms, subpoenas, defamation, and copyright, including the Boston College case.
- **Shopes, "Making Sense of Oral History" (2002)**: an introductory article on reading interviews as evidence, the reliability of memory, and how to cross-check.
