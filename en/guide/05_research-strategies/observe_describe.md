# Description

> The reasoning on this page applies to every field; the examples and concrete steps come from computer science. The standards it cites, such as the software engineering review standards (the Empirical Standards of ACM SIGSOFT, the Association for Computing Machinery's Special Interest Group on Software Engineering), the reporting guidelines of medicine and veterinary science, and the planning guidance of the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, describe how those particular fields work and are marked with their source. They are not universal rules for all research.

What you want to find out is "what is this phenomenon actually like?": whether it exists, what kinds there are, how common it is, and how it differs from something else. You don't plan to build a new tool, and you are not yet trying to explain why it happens. In that case you are following the **description** strategy.

For what a research strategy is, see [Key concepts](../00_start-here/key-concepts.md#research-strategy). For where description ends and causal explanation, measurement, and synthesis begin, see the section "How to pick your main strategy" in [Research strategies](README.md#how-to-pick-your-main-strategy). For how to pick your own strategy from among them, see [Choosing a method](../02_do-the-project/02_choose-method.md).

## 1. What kind of knowledge this research produces

Description answers questions about "what the world is like right now". By the way the question is asked, there are roughly five kinds[^easterbrook2008selecting-e44][^easterbrook2008selecting-e46][^easterbrook2008selecting-e47]:

| Type of question | How it is asked | Example: large language models and smart-contract security | What the claim looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Existence | Does X exist? | Do contracts generated by large language models contain vulnerable code patterns that are rare in human-written code? | "There is a class of vulnerable patterns that …; we found N real examples" |
| Description and classification | What is X like? What kinds of X are there? | What categories do the vulnerabilities in generated contracts fall into? | "They fall into five categories, two of which have not been described before" |
| Frequency and distribution | How common is X? Where is it more or less common? | What share of generated contracts have reentrancy vulnerabilities? | "Forty percent of contracts generated by large language models have reentrancy vulnerabilities" |
| Descriptive comparison | How does X differ from Y? | How does the distribution of vulnerabilities differ between generated and human-written contracts? | "Generated contracts have a clearly higher rate of reentrancy vulnerabilities than human-written ones" |
| Descriptive process | How does X normally happen? | How do developers actually use large language models to write contracts, and how do they check the generated code? | "Developers usually first …, then …, and rarely …" |

The examples and claims in the table are our own illustrations. The last kind of question is usually answered with interviews or observation, and the result is a process or a set of categories, not necessarily a number.

**Why it is worth doing.** If you don't know how common something normally is, you can't tell whether what you are looking at is normal or unusual[^easterbrook2008selecting-e34]. Design questions ("how can we do this better?") presuppose that the related knowledge questions have already been answered, so that we understand the problem well enough[^easterbrook2008selecting-e48]. When a field does not yet have established results, "observations" that faithfully report what actually happens can guide practice and lay the groundwork for establishing results later[^shaw2002makes-e6].

**What it does not produce.** It doesn't answer "why", and it doesn't answer "which is better". Mistaking correlation for causation is a common error, and showing causation is much harder than showing that two variables are related[^easterbrook2008selecting-e36]. To claim "some factor causes vulnerabilities", see [Causal explanation](explain_causality.md); to claim "tool A beats tool B", see [Comparative evaluation](compare_evaluate.md); to claim "this benchmark doesn't measure accurately", see [Measurement](measure.md).

**Write it down:** For each of the five question types in the table, write one question from your research area. Pick the one whose answer you most want to know, and write what that answer might look like.

## 2. What evidence is enough

Take the claim "forty percent of contracts generated by large language models have reentrancy vulnerabilities". A reviewer will press you on at least the following. Most of the standards cited in the table and in this section come from the ACM SIGSOFT Empirical Standards, the review standards for software engineering research; they apply to software engineering. Sources from other fields are marked separately.

| What the reviewer will ask | What you need to show |
|---|---|
| What do you mean by "contracts generated by large language models"? | Which models, which versions, what prompts, and when the contracts were generated. At the start of the study, state the target population you want to describe and generalize to[^wagner2020challenges-e3][^baltes2022sampling-e11] |
| Where did the sample come from? Whom does it represent? | The purpose and steps of your sampling. Representativeness is a relationship between a sample and a population, not a property that comes with a sampling technique[^baltes2022sampling-e3]; if the sampling frame itself is biased, no sample size can make up for it[^baltes2022sampling-e5] |
| How did you decide that a contract "has a reentrancy vulnerability"? | The decision rules, and the tools used or the manual labeling process. First define exactly what the term means, and make sure you can reliably recognize it[^easterbrook2008selecting-e43] |
| Are those decisions accurate? | The software engineering standard for repository mining requires that you either use validated metrics or check construct validity (whether you are measuring what you mean to measure) yourself[^ralph2020empirical-e112]; for manual labeling, use several raters and report how many there were and their inter-rater reliability[^ralph2020empirical-e111] |
| How certain is "forty percent"? | The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's data planning guidance points out that probability sampling (drawing units at random with known probabilities) lets you draw quantitative conclusions about the population and express the uncertainty properly; a sample picked by judgment can't do this[^epa2006dqo-e13][^epa2006dqo-e14] |
| So what? Compared with what? | A point of reference: the rate in human-written contracts, or the rate for older model versions. Without one, readers can't tell whether forty percent is a lot or a little |

**The standard to meet** comes down to four points:

1. **The object of study is clearly defined.** A reader can use your definition to recognize the same thing. Simply asking "how common is it?" often presupposes that you know what you are studying and can reliably recognize it[^easterbrook2008selecting-e43]. Unvalidated single measures, such as GitHub stars standing in for "popularity", are an antipattern in the software engineering standard for repository mining[^ralph2020empirical-e119].
2. **The sample matches the scope of the conclusion.** State whether your sampling aims to represent a population or serves some other goal (such as finding unusual cases), and explain why your approach suits that goal[^ralph2020empirical-e36]. Sampling at random from a biased sampling frame and then claiming the sample is representative is an antipattern[^ralph2020empirical-e37].
3. **Judgments are double-checked.** The more disputable a judgment, the more it needs several raters[^ralph2020empirical-e128]. "Is this a reentrancy vulnerability or not?" is often disputable.
4. **The process can be repeated.** State where, when, and how you collected what data[^ralph2020empirical-e125].

**Don't apply the wrong standard.** No quality criteria fit all research; every study has to be judged by the standards that fit its method[^ralph2020empirical-e24]. When representativeness was never a goal of the study, complaining that the sample is not representative is an invalid criticism[^ralph2020empirical-e39]. A case study, for example, aims to understand one site in depth, not to generalize from a sample to a population[^baltes2022sampling-e10]. In the other direction, the software engineering standard for questionnaire surveys also lists complaining that the results are "hardly surprising" as an invalid criticism[^ralph2020empirical-e249].

> **Where sources disagree: is description alone enough to publish?**
>
> - **Yes, if it is interesting.** In Shaw's classification of the results of software engineering research, "observations" that faithfully report what actually happens are one kind of result, and they are judged by whether they are interesting[^shaw2002makes-e5]; she cites Brooks's view that observations can be good preliminary results[^shaw2002makes-e4].
> - **No, it has to connect to theory.** The software engineering standard for repository mining lists "limiting a study to quantitative description; failing to test, build or extend theory" as an antipattern[^ralph2020empirical-e122]. Easterbrook and colleagues also point out that many empirical studies don't connect their data to an underlying theory, which makes the results hard to interpret and the studies hard to compare[^easterbrook2008selecting-e54].
>
> In practice: when you submit to an empirical computer science conference or journal, add at least one more layer beyond "what it is like": "what this tells us". Which existing view does it support or challenge, and what does it mean for whose practice? A paper that is just a pile of percentages is the one most likely to get "so what?"

**Write it down:** Write your descriptive claim as one sentence, then go down the table above and write your answer to each row. Any row you can't answer is something your design has to fill in.

## 3. Common research methods

| Method | When to use it | What to watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| [Mining software repositories](../07_fields/computer-science/practices/repository_mining.md) | You want the distribution of something across large amounts of code, commits, or bug reports. Data from platforms like GitHub can be used directly to report properties of projects[^kalliamvakou2014promises-e11] | Be especially careful when using it to draw conclusions about abstract concepts[^kalliamvakou2014promises-e11]; define the unit of analysis first[^ralph2020empirical-e108] |
| [Questionnaire surveys](../06_methods/questionnaire_survey.md) | You want the distribution of a practice, tool, or opinion across a population. Surveys are typically used to answer base-rate questions[^easterbrook2008selecting-e49]; a descriptive survey cares about what the distribution is, not why it exists[^wagner2020challenges-e1] | What people say they do may bear no relation to what they actually do[^easterbrook2008selecting-e52]; you can cross-check against data such as work repositories[^molleri2020empirically-e7] |
| [Interview studies](../06_methods/interview_study.md), [Grounded theory](../06_methods/grounded_theory.md) | You don't yet know what kinds of the phenomenon exist or what to ask. Early in research, methods that yield rich qualitative data suit the job and help build an initial theory[^easterbrook2008selecting-e45] | The results are categories and processes, not proportions; don't use these methods to answer "how many people?" |
| [Case studies](../06_methods/case_study.md) | You want to describe in depth what actually happens in a few real settings | You trade representativeness for depth[^easterbrook2008selecting-e19]; don't pretend one or two sites are representative[^baltes2022sampling-e10] |
| [Longitudinal studies](../06_methods/longitudinal_study.md) | You want to describe how a phenomenon changes over time[^ralph2020empirical-e257] | If you only want to know "how widely is it used now?", a single cross-sectional "snapshot" is enough; you need to follow the same units only if you want to see change[^easterbrook2008selecting-e53] |
| [Data science](../06_methods/data_science.md) | Exploratory analysis of an existing dataset | For analyses of a single point in time, the SIGSOFT standards suggest following the standard for exploratory data science[^ralph2020empirical-e258] |
| [Action research](../06_methods/action_research.md) | You want to see how people in a real organization react when a change is introduced. The point of action research is to introduce a change and observe how participants react[^ralph2020empirical-e342] | The researcher has to carry out an intervention in a real setting; if you only observe without intervening, follow the standard for case studies[^ralph2020empirical-e331] |
| [Spatial analysis](../07_fields/geography/practices/spatial_analysis.md) (a geography practice) | You want to know how a phenomenon is distributed in space and whether it clusters. Spatial autocorrelation describes how far the similarity between observed values is related to how close their locations are[^rey2023geographic-e11] | People tend to see patterns in randomness[^rey2023geographic-e13], so "does it cluster?" needs a test: only a small enough p-value lets you reject "the pattern on this map is random"[^rey2023geographic-e15]; conclusions from areal data change with the scale and the zoning[^kwan2018limits-e2] |
| [Reviews](../06_methods/review_study.md) | The answer may already be in the literature | You still ask the exploratory question, but you can answer it with a literature survey instead of a new empirical study[^easterbrook2008selecting-e41]; see [Synthesis](synthesize.md) |

The uses and cautions in the table without a footnote are our own summary. For the corresponding approaches in other fields, see the cross-field methods [Field studies: observation and sampling](../06_methods/field_study.md) (mostly used in earth and environmental sciences) and the cross-sectional study in [Observational epidemiology](../06_methods/observational_epidemiology.md) (mostly used in the health sciences), and the languages and literature practice [Language documentation](../07_fields/languages-literature/practices/language_documentation.md).

**Why action research is listed here.** The software engineering review standards define action research as empirical research into how an intervention (for example, introducing a method or tool) affects a real-world setting[^ralph2020empirical-e330]. It serves two strategies: introducing an improvement into a real organization belongs to [Building and improving](build_improve.md); seeing how people in the real setting react belongs to description. The same standard also states that action research is not an experiment[^ralph2020empirical-e343], so this handbook does not file it under [Causal explanation](explain_causality.md).

**Why spatial analysis is listed here**: part of its work is describing how a phenomenon is distributed in space and testing whether it clusters, which is description; the part that studies how the environment affects individuals belongs to [Causal explanation](explain_causality.md).

**A common combination**: first use interviews or a close manual reading of a small sample to work out the categories; then use repository mining or a questionnaire to count how common each category is. For example, read 50 generated contracts by hand to work out the vulnerability types, then use a tool to count their distribution across a few thousand contracts, and finally check a sample of the tool's decisions by hand.

## 4. How it shapes the whole project

Description does not only shape your design; it matters at every stage, from reading the literature to writing the paper.

**When reading the literature**: first check whether anyone has described the same phenomenon; the answer may already be in the literature[^easterbrook2008selecting-e41]. Pay special attention to the definitions and categories others use: results from different studies can be compared only when key terms are clearly defined[^easterbrook2008selecting-e55]. If you can reuse someone else's definition, don't invent your own. For how to search the literature, see [Entering a field and exploring the literature](../01_find-a-problem/01_enter-a-field.md).

**When choosing a topic**: descriptive questions are most valuable when a research area is just starting. Large language models writing contracts is still a new phenomenon, so "what usually goes wrong?" is worth answering in its own right. If you meant to do building and improving but find that nobody knows how widespread the problem is, doing a descriptive study first is often the more solid route (see Section 1 for why[^easterbrook2008selecting-e48]). When you narrow down your topic, apply the "so what?" test: once you have the numbers, who will change what they do because of them? See [Converging on a project](../01_find-a-problem/05_converge.md).

**When designing the study**: write down the target population and the unit of analysis first. The unit of analysis determines how you should sample: if your question is about companies but you sample developers, several people from the same company will over-represent that company[^easterbrook2008selecting-e50]. In our example, whether the question is about "contracts" or "projects" changes how you count. State the purpose of your sampling[^baltes2022sampling-e6] and fix your decision rules in advance. A corpus can go out of date quickly; there is an inherent tension between keeping it stable and keeping it current[^baltes2022sampling-e15]. Large language models are updated often, so record the model version and generation date for every piece of data. For how to judge how far your conclusions generalize, see [Validity and limitations](../02_do-the-project/04_validity-and-limits.md).

**When running the study**: a low response rate raises the risk of bias; for example, perhaps only people unhappy with something answered the questionnaire[^easterbrook2008selecting-e51]. If the sampling frame may be biased, you can repeat the study with a different sampling frame, for example switching from GitHub to SourceForge[^baltes2022sampling-e13]. For manual labeling, first label a small trial batch, compute agreement, revise the rules, and only then scale up.

**When analyzing**: a confounder is a third factor that affects two variables at once and makes them look related. The epidemiology methods literature points out that when an analysis only aims to describe an association, there is no need to adjust for confounding; adjustment is needed only when the goal is causal[^hernan2018cword-e7]. But don't quietly turn description into causation: questionnaire data is often treated as if it were experimental data, revealing causal relationships that aren't there[^torchiano2017lessons-e2]. For how to interpret results, see [Analysis and interpretation](../02_do-the-project/07_analyze-and-interpret.md).

**When writing**: state the scope in the claim, for example "for these three models, under this kind of prompt". Very few software engineering studies can truly claim a representative sample, and overstating representativeness is both unnecessary and unscientific[^baltes2022sampling-e8]. Don't admit sampling bias in the limitations section and then write the conclusions as if they held universally[^baltes2022sampling-e9][^ralph2020empirical-e66]. For how a paper should make clear "what this tells us", see [Telling the story and writing the paper](../03_write-and-publish/01_story-and-paper.md).

## 5. Which fields use it

This lists only what our sources support; our sources do not cover whether other fields use it often.

- **Computer science (especially software engineering)**: the software engineering standard for questionnaire surveys lists providing "a detailed account of the properties of a phenomenon or population" as one use of questionnaires[^ralph2020empirical-e234]. Findings from mining software repositories are often descriptive in themselves, for example that most GitHub projects are personal and inactive[^kalliamvakou2014promises-e1], and that 67% of projects have only one committer[^kalliamvakou2014promises-e5]. See the [Computer science field overview](../07_fields/computer-science/README.md).
- **Medicine and public health**: according to the explanation and elaboration document of STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology, the reporting guideline for observational studies in medicine), a cross-sectional study surveys a sample at one point in time and is often used to look at the prevalence of exposures, risk factors, or disease[^vandenbroucke2007strobe-e3]. See [Health sciences](../07_fields/health-sciences/README.md) and [Clinical medicine](../07_fields/clinical-medicine/README.md).
- **Veterinary science**: according to STROBE-Vet, the reporting guideline for observational studies in veterinary research, observational studies can be used to estimate the prevalence or incidence of a condition and to study its distribution in time and space[^sargeant2016strobevet-e2]. See [Animal and veterinary science](../07_fields/animal-veterinary-science/README.md).
- **Environmental engineering and earth sciences**: the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's planning guidance lists "estimation" (for example, determining the mean concentration of a pollutant) as one use of data[^epa2006dqo-e2]. See [Environmental engineering](../07_fields/environmental-engineering/README.md) and [Earth and environmental sciences](../07_fields/earth-environmental-sciences/README.md).
- **Political science**: the case study methods literature in political science points out that case studies are generally more useful when the inference is descriptive rather than causal[^gerring2004case-e7]. See [Political science](../07_fields/political-science/README.md).
- **Linguistics**: in linguistics, language "description" and language "documentation" are treated as two kinds of work. Himmelmann called for keeping them separate, but some scholars think certain readings of that call have gone too far[^riesberg2018adequacy-e2]. See [Languages and literature](../07_fields/languages-literature/README.md).

## 6. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Starting to count before the thing is clearly defined | Others can't recognize what you counted, and your results can't be compared with other studies[^easterbrook2008selecting-e43][^easterbrook2008selecting-e55] |
| Letting a ready-made metric stand in for an abstract concept | Using stars to represent popularity, for example, is an unvalidated single measure[^ralph2020empirical-e119] |
| Sampling at random from a biased sampling frame and calling the sample representative | Bias in the sampling frame does not go away with randomization or a large sample[^ralph2020empirical-e37][^baltes2022sampling-e5] |
| Treating results from a convenience sample (whatever is easy to reach) as a general rate | For example, in a study that used snowball sampling (asking respondents to refer further respondents) to survey field researchers' experiences, the authors themselves said they could not tell from it how common those experiences were[^clancy2014survey-e7] |
| Asking "how do you do it?" in a questionnaire and taking the answers as what people actually do | What people say and what they do may bear no relation to each other[^easterbrook2008selecting-e52] |
| Having only one person label highly disputable judgments | The more disputable the judgment, the more it needs several raters[^ralph2020empirical-e128] |
| Reporting percentages without saying what they mean | Without a reference point you can't tell normal from unusual[^easterbrook2008selecting-e34]; the software engineering standard for repository mining lists purely quantitative description as an antipattern[^ralph2020empirical-e122] |
| "Seeing" causation in descriptive data | Correlation is not causation[^easterbrook2008selecting-e36][^torchiano2017lessons-e2] |
| Admitting bias in the limitations and then ignoring it in the conclusions | Overstated conclusions are an antipattern listed by SIGSOFT[^ralph2020empirical-e66] |

## 7. Further reading

- **Easterbrook et al. 2008, *Selecting Empirical Methods for Software Engineering Research*.** Section 2 sorts research questions into types such as existence, description and classification, base rate, relationship, and causality, and explains which methods fit each. The classification on this page comes mainly from here. [Evidence notes](../../sources/notes/easterbrook2008selecting.md)
- **Baltes & Ralph 2022, *Sampling in Software Engineering Research*.** Explains representativeness, sampling frames, and convenience sampling clearly, and ends with a set of writing recommendations. Worth reading before any "how widespread is it?" study. [Evidence notes](../../sources/notes/baltes2022sampling.md)
- **The ACM SIGSOFT Empirical Standards (Ralph et al. 2020).** There is a checklist each for repository mining, questionnaire surveys, longitudinal studies, sampling, and inter-rater reliability, listing essential attributes, desirable attributes, antipatterns, and invalid criticisms. [Evidence notes](../../sources/notes/ralph2020empirical.md)
- **Wagner et al. 2020, *Challenges in Survey Research*.** How descriptive surveys relate to theory and sampling. [Evidence notes](../../sources/notes/wagner2020challenges.md)
- **Kalliamvakou et al. 2014, *The Promises and Perils of Mining GitHub*.** Lists a set of pitfalls in doing research with GitHub data. [Evidence notes](../../sources/notes/kalliamvakou2014promises.md)
- **The STROBE explanation and elaboration (Vandenbroucke et al. 2007).** The reporting guideline for observational studies in medicine; the parts on cross-sectional studies are a useful reference. [Evidence notes](../../sources/notes/vandenbroucke2007strobe.md)
