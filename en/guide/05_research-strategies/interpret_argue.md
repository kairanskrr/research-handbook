# Interpretation and argument

> This page covers research whose main product is an interpretation or an argument, with examples from history, literature, philosophy, law, and art history. The sources it cites are mainly the guidelines of American scholarly societies (the American Historical Association, the American Philosophical Association, the Modern Language Association, and others), handouts that writing centers at American and British universities give their students (Harvard, North Carolina, and the University of York in the UK), and one methodology article on legal research in Australia and the UK. Each point reflects only the practice of the institution or author in its footnote, not a rule for all of the humanities; for the specific conventions, go by your own field, country, and institution.

You have a body of texts in front of you: a stack of archival documents, a novel, a set of court decisions, or a series of papers in which several philosophers answer one another. As you read, you see an understanding that nobody has put forward before, and you want your peers to accept it. You don't need an experiment or a measurement for this. What you need to do is **argue** for that understanding.

This is **interpretation and argument**: producing new understanding by interpreting and reasoning about texts, historical sources, legal and ethical norms, works, and concepts. For an overview of research strategies, see [Key concepts](../00_start-here/key-concepts.md#research-strategy). For how a particular field does it, this page gives only one sentence and a link; the details are in [Archival research](../06_methods/archival_research.md), [Oral history](../06_methods/oral_history.md), and the practice pages of each field.

## 1. What kind of knowledge this research produces

**The central claim looks like this: "X should be understood this way (or: this argument holds up, or doesn't), because …"** By the kind of knowledge produced, there are roughly four types. The categories and examples in the table below are this handbook's own summary; the examples are hypothetical, not real research findings `[Our analysis]`:

| Type of knowledge | Question it answers | What the claim looks like (hypothetical example) | Typical fields |
|---|---|---|---|
| Explanation | How past events came about, and how people at the time understood them | "A city's sanitation campaign in the 1950s was mainly about rebuilding neighborhood organizations, not about preventing disease" | History |
| Interpretation | What a text or work means | "The recurring image of windows in the novel expresses the protagonist's sense of being trapped" | Literature, art history, history of philosophy |
| Conceptual clarification | How a concept should be understood, and whether a definition holds | "This definition of 'knowledge' fails, because there is a class of cases that satisfy it but are not cases of knowing" | Philosophy |
| Normative claim | What the law is and what it ought to be; whether a practice is right | "Current law on the liability of online platforms is internally inconsistent and should be amended as follows" | Law, ethics |

Here is how several sources describe this kind of research in their own fields:

- **History** (a primer on historical research by Cronon and colleagues at the University of Wisconsin): the most basic form of argument is a claim supported by evidence[^cronon2009learning-e18]; the real goal of research is to build an argument and tell a story[^cronon2009learning-e3].
- **Literary studies** (the Harvard College Writing Center's handout on close reading): first observe facts and details in the text[^kain1998closereading-e1], then move from those observations to an interpretation[^kain1998closereading-e2].
- **Philosophy** (a 1996 statement by the American Philosophical Association, describing the situation in the US): philosophical research often consists in improving an analysis, proposing or criticizing an interpretation, or exploring new ways of thinking; in short, in advancing understanding[^apaphil1996research-e1].
- **Law** (a 2012 methodology article by Hutchinson and Duncan, mainly about Australia and the UK): a definition quoted in the article describes doctrinal research (research into "what the law is") as giving a systematic exposition of the rules governing a particular area of law, analyzing the relationships between them, and explaining areas of difficulty[^hutchinson2012defining-e1]; reform-oriented research instead evaluates whether existing rules are adequate and recommends changes to those that fall short[^hutchinson2012defining-e2].
- **Research on the arts**: the *Frascati Manual*, which the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) uses to measure research and development, says that research on the arts, such as musicology, art history, theater studies, and literature, mostly involves basic or applied research[^oecd2015frascati-e12].

**There is no single right answer, but some answers are better than others.** Having no single right answer does not mean that anything you say about a text goes[^uncwritinglit-e2]. Interpretations also change as scholarship accumulates: according to the American Historical Association's standards, historians reanalyze primary documents in the light of a growing body of secondary scholarship, so interpretations of the same record keep changing[^aha2023standards-e16]. In philosophy, a debate is usually not settled by a single paper: the best philosophical work often provokes debate rather than resolving it[^apaphil1996research-e4]; the premises of an argument are seldom completely beyond question, so an argument is usually not conclusive[^pryor2025terms-e12].

**How it differs from neighboring strategies** `[Our analysis]`:

| | Interpretation and argument | [Description](observe_describe.md) | [Causal explanation](explain_causality.md) | [Proof](prove.md) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Claim | This is the right, or a better, way to understand it | What the phenomenon is like, how common it is | Factor X causes Y | Under the assumptions, the conclusion must hold |
| Main evidence | Texts, sources, norms, works, plus reasoning | Systematically collected observations | A design that rules out alternative explanations | A proof |
| How others check it | Retrace your argument and check every citation | Repeat the observations | Repeat the experiment or analysis | Check every step of the derivation |

Some boundaries `[Our analysis]`:

- **Historical research is often mixed**: first you use archives to reconstruct what happened (description), then you argue for an interpretation. Your central claim decides the main strategy; do the reconstruction to the standards of description.
- **When you ask "why did it happen?"**, your claim becomes causal. Process tracing in political science stresses spelling out hypotheses about causal mechanisms explicitly so they can be rigorously tested[^bennett2012process-e7]. If your central claim is causal, also check it against the standards of [Causal explanation](explain_causality.md).
- **Philosophical arguments are mostly deductive**, and mathematical proofs are the paradigm of this kind of argument[^pryor2025terms-e3]. The difference is that the premises are disputable, so most of the work goes into defending premises and answering objections. That puts it in this strategy.
- **If you only survey earlier interpretations** (historiography, a review of the literature), your central claim is "what the existing research adds up to", which belongs to [Synthesis](synthesize.md).

**Write it down:** Write your central claim as "X should be understood as …, because …". Then write one sentence on how a thoughtful reader would argue against it. A good thesis is arguable: a thoughtful reader could disagree[^harvardwcthesis-e1]. If it only summarizes what readers can see for themselves, there is no one to persuade[^harvardwcthesis-e2].

## 2. What evidence is enough

Ask yourself: **where would a colleague who knows the same material but reads it differently find fault with you?** You can check the five areas below one by one `[Our analysis]`.

### Use primary sources, and scrutinize them first

- **Primary sources are the core of the evidence.** In history, primary sources are the original documents through which we know the past, and secondary sources are the interpretations scholars write from primary sources[^cronon2009learning-e26]; whether a document counts as primary or secondary depends on the question you ask of it[^aha2023standards-e2]. In doctrinal legal research, the core is locating and analyzing primary legal materials to establish what the law is[^hutchinson2012defining-e9]; it is more than a literature review[^hutchinson2012defining-e8]. Close reading in literature means slowing down and paying attention to a text's language, imagery, allusions, syntax, and form[^yorkclosereading-e1]. Primary sources are harder to find and harder to read, and beginners tend to avoid them[^cronon2009learning-e20].
- **Scrutinize before you use.** Ask who the author was and what biases they may have had[^cronon2009learning-e27], whether the document really dates from that period[^cronon2009learning-e24], and whom it was written for[^cronon2009learning-e19]. A record being authentic does not make its content reliable[^saaacrl2018primary-e20]. Conversely, even a document that is plainly lying can, once interpreted, tell you important things about the past[^cronon2009learning-e6]. Oral history interviews also need checking first: is the narrator reliable, and can the account be verified[^shopes2002making-e2]? For how to scrutinize sources, see [Archival research, step 5](../06_methods/archival_research.md#step-5-scrutinize-every-source) and [Oral history, step 10](../06_methods/oral_history.md#step-10-use-memory-as-evidence).
- **Say exactly which text and which law you are using.** The basic task of a scholarly edition is to provide a reliable text[^mlacseguidelines-e1]; quotations must be copied exactly from the original, down to the last comma[^yorkclosereading-e11]. In American legal research, checking that cases and statutes are still good law is always the last step[^northwesterngoodlaw-e2].

### Let others check your work

You can't "run the experiment again" with interpretation and argument. The results of doctrinal analysis vary with the scholar's expertise, so others cannot replicate them exactly[^hutchinson2012defining-e14]; but the step of locating the sources of law is easy for others to replicate[^hutchinson2012defining-e30]. So "checkable" here means others can follow your citations to the same materials and retrace your reasoning `[Our analysis]`.

- **Write notes that let others retrace your steps.** According to the American Historical Association's standards, notes and bibliographies let other historians retrace every step of an argument[^aha2023standards-e3]; the sloppier your notes, the harder it is for colleagues to trust you[^aha2023standards-e4]. For how to record locations while working in an archive, see [Archival research, step 6](../06_methods/archival_research.md#step-6-organize-and-cite).
- **Be ready to share your materials.** The American Historical Association requires historians to be prepared to make available the sources, evidence, and data they used[^aha2023standards-e15]; the College Art Association's *Standards and Guidelines for the Practice of Art History* requires the same, and requires credit for the sources of photographs[^caa2014standards-e1][^caa2014standards-e3].
- **Don't borrow someone else's citations.** The American Historical Association considers it improper to copy citations to primary sources that you have not checked yourself from a secondary work without acknowledging that work[^aha2023standards-e11].
- **Write a sentence about your method.** In law, doctrinal method is often left implicit, and many lawyers think it doesn't need saying[^hutchinson2012defining-e15]; in a thesis, write at least one sentence. For how, see [Doctrinal legal research, step 8](../07_fields/law/practices/doctrinal_research.md#step-8-write-the-methodology-section).

### Face contrary evidence and counterexamples

- **Don't leave out contrary evidence**[^aha2023standards-e6]. If you look only for evidence that fits your expectations, you easily fall into confirmation bias: noticing only information that confirms what you expected[^cronon2009learning-e7].
- **Raise the counterargument yourself**, that is, the argument a reader could reasonably make to challenge your thesis[^harvardwccounterargument-e1]. One kind of counterargument is a different interpretation of the same evidence[^harvardwccounterargument-e2]. Raising objections yourself is better than hoping readers won't think of them[^pryor2012writing-e16]. A writing handout from an American law school likewise asks you to anticipate and rebut "counterexamples" that cut against you[^georgetown2017comparison-e5].
- **Criticize the strongest version of the other side.** Before criticizing a position, state accurately what it says[^pryor2012writing-e15]; reading an opponent's argument in its strongest form is what philosophers call the principle of charity[^pryor2025terms-e17]. For which objections to answer, see [Philosophical argument, step 8](../07_fields/philosophy-ethics-religion/practices/philosophical_argument.md#step-8-find-the-strongest-objections-and-answer-them).
- **If you can't rebut it, revise your thesis.** A counterargument you can't answer may mean your thesis needs to change[^harvardwccounterargument-e3]; when strong evidence pulls you away from your initial hypothesis, change the hypothesis[^cronon2009learning-e8].

### Make the structure of the argument clear

- **Show how the evidence supports the thesis.** Use reasoning to show the link between evidence and thesis[^uncwritinglit-e3]; quoting without analyzing amounts to not showing your reasoning[^uncwritinglit-e8]. Organize your findings into an argument, not a list[^yorkclosereading-e6].
- **Break the reasoning into visible steps.** Evaluating a deductive argument involves two separate questions: are the premises worth accepting, and does the conclusion really follow from them[^pryor2025terms-e4]? In a persuasive argument, the premises are at least less controversial than the conclusion[^pryor2025terms-e9].
- **Make your key concepts clear and use them consistently.** Even if a key concept is an everyday word, say how you understand it[^pryor2012writing-e10]; if a keyword is read in different senses in different places, the argument only looks valid[^pryor2025terms-e19].
- **Legal reasoning has a few set shapes**: deduction from rules laid down by legislation[^hutchinson2012defining-e45], induction of a common principle from several cases[^hutchinson2012defining-e46], and analogy, arguing that similar cases fall under the same principle[^hutchinson2012defining-e47]. For how to read judgments and distinguish precedents in common law countries, see [Doctrinal legal research, step 4](../07_fields/law/practices/doctrinal_research.md#step-4-read-and-analyze-judgments-common-law-jurisdictions).

### Position your argument

Working out how your view relates to the views of existing scholars is called "positioning your argument", and it can greatly increase the significance of your work[^cronon2009learning-e23]. Your thesis is often itself a counterargument to someone else's argument[^harvardwccounterargument-e7].

- **Avoid the obvious conclusion**[^uncwritinglit-e4]; your thesis should be able to answer "so what?"[^uncwritinglit-e9] Ask yourself: can you say what this paper adds to our understanding of the question[^harvardwcthesis-e6]?
- **Say which step forward you are taking.** A law article can distinguish settled law from law that is still emerging[^hutchinson2012defining-e22], or bring several lines of cases together to expose an unjustified inconsistency[^hutchinson2012defining-e23]. In philosophy, moving a debate one step forward is a contribution in itself `[Our analysis]`.
- **State your limitations.** The record of the past is fragmentary, so absolute historical knowledge is impossible[^aha2023standards-e14].

**Write it down:** List the three to five pieces of evidence your claim depends on most. Next to each, write one sentence: where it comes from, how others can find it, and whether there is material that points the other way.

## 3. Common research methods

The table below is this handbook's own classification `[Our analysis]`; statements that have a source are footnoted separately.

| Method | When to use it | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| [Archival research](../06_methods/archival_research.md) (a cross-field method) | Using unpublished original records to answer questions about the past | According to the primary source literacy guidelines of American archivists and librarians, you examine and synthesize multiple sources to build, support, or refute an argument[^saaacrl2018primary-e16] |
| [Oral history](../06_methods/oral_history.md) (a cross-field method) | People who lived through events are still alive, and the written record is missing or one-sided | When an interview conflicts with other evidence, the historian has to explain the discrepancy[^shopes2002making-e5]; according to the Oral History Association's statement on ethics, your conclusions may differ from the narrator's, but they must come from properly cited evidence[^oha2018principles-e31] |
| [Close reading and literary argument](../07_fields/languages-literature/practices/close_reading.md) | Proposing an interpretation of one or a few texts | Also useful when interpreting a particular philosopher in the history of philosophy |
| [Philosophical argument](../07_fields/philosophy-ethics-religion/practices/philosophical_argument.md) | Defending a thesis, clarifying a concept, testing a principle | Reflective equilibrium means adjusting general principles and particular judgments against each other[^knight2023reflective-e1]; the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry calls it the dominant method in moral and political philosophy[^knight2023reflective-e3] |
| [Doctrinal legal research](../07_fields/law/practices/doctrinal_research.md) | Establishing what the law is, evaluating current law, proposing reform | Usually two steps: first locate the sources of law, then interpret and analyze the texts[^hutchinson2012defining-e6]. The tools for working with case law apply only to common law countries; see the scope note at the top of that page |
| [Process tracing and comparative case studies](../06_methods/process_tracing.md) | Your claim is "why did it happen", and you need to test several rival explanations one by one | Mainly serves causal explanation; you need to test explanations that historians and others have offered for the same case[^bennett2012process-e26] |

**Why archival research and oral history are listed here.** Both methods yield primary materials, and the materials are not conclusions in themselves: the sources cited in those two rows both require you to examine the materials alongside other evidence before using them to build or refute an argument. That is why this handbook files them under interpretation and argument. If you use archives only to reconstruct what happened, follow the standards of [Description](observe_describe.md); if you use them to test a causal explanation, also see [Causal explanation](explain_causality.md) `[Our analysis]`.

Religious studies can draw on many methods, including literary criticism, anthropology, sociology, and history[^aar2019literacy-e2]. Methods and strategies are two separate layers, and one method can serve different strategies; see [Choosing a method](../02_do-the-project/02_choose-method.md).

## 4. How it shapes the whole project

Most chapters in Part 1 and Part 2 use experimental research as their example. With interpretation and argument, "experiments" become working in archives, close reading, finding sources of law, and reconstructing arguments; "data" becomes the texts and sources you cite. The table below lists what to watch for at each stage `[Our analysis]`; statements that have a source are footnoted separately:

| Stage | What to watch for in interpretation and argument |
|---|---|
| [Entering a field and exploring the literature](../01_find-a-problem/01_enter-a-field.md) | When reading secondary literature, check whether the author's claims are supported by the evidence they give[^cronon2009learning-e21]. When reading a philosophy paper, first find the main conclusion, then map out the "parts" of the argument[^pryor2006reading-e1][^pryor2006reading-e5]. As you read, note "who holds which interpretation", so you can position your argument later |
| [Finding problems](../01_find-a-problem/02_generate-problems.md), [Converging on a project](../01_find-a-problem/05_converge.md) | Look for topics in the places in a text that puzzle you or don't make sense[^uncwritinglit-e5]; once you have a question, ask right away "what sources are there?"[^cronon2009learning-e2] A thesis that is too narrow has nothing to say; one that is too broad can't be supported by your material[^harvardwcthesis-e3]. A good philosophy paper makes one small point[^pryor2012writing-e5] |
| [From research questions to an evidence plan](../02_do-the-project/01_question-to-evidence.md) | The evidence plan becomes a list of materials: which archives, which edition of the text, which sources of law, which opposing interpretations. You can start with a list of "ideal sources" and keep updating it as you meet the real documents[^cronon2009learning-e41]. Law theses often state a "main argument" or "guiding proposition" that plays a role similar to a hypothesis[^hutchinson2012defining-e40] |
| [Validity and limitations](../02_do-the-project/04_validity-and-limits.md) | There is no sampling error, but there are gaps in the material: records may never have existed, may not have survived, or may never have been collected[^saaacrl2018primary-e9]. There is also your own standpoint: scholarly integrity requires awareness of personal and cultural bias[^caa2014standards-e2]; a researcher's philosophical position often shapes the research question and the conclusions, yet is often left unstated[^hutchinson2012defining-e39] |
| [The smallest version that runs end to end](../02_do-the-project/05_pilot-and-derisk.md) | Close-read a short passage or check one collection first, to make sure the material can carry the question. Contact an archive before you go, to confirm the material is there[^cronon2009learning-e32]. In philosophy: once you can explain it to someone out loud, write the outline[^pryor2012writing-e36] |
| [Running and iterating](../02_do-the-project/06_run-and-iterate.md) | Questions and sources shape each other[^cronon2009learning-e9]; choosing evidence and revising your thesis are best done together[^uncwritinglit-e19]; write while you collect[^cronon2009learning-e28]. In your notes, strictly separate verbatim quotation from paraphrase[^aha2023standards-e12], and mark clearly which ideas are your own[^cronon2009learning-e11] |
| [Analysis and interpretation](../02_do-the-project/07_analyze-and-interpret.md) | "Analysis" becomes the move from observations to a thesis: look for patterns, contradictions, and similarities among the things you noticed[^kain1998closereading-e5], then ask "how" and "why"[^kain1998closereading-e6]. If you can't fix an argument, change your thesis to one you can defend[^pryor2012writing-e19] |
| [Telling the story and writing the paper](../03_write-and-publish/01_story-and-paper.md) | You don't have to follow the "methods–results–discussion" structure; first look at how model papers in your field organize chapters around the argument. Make clear where you are giving your own view and where you are reporting someone else's[^pryor2012writing-e40]. The notes-and-bibliography system is widely used in the humanities, including literature, history, and the arts[^chicago2024citation-e2]; check the target journal's style before you start writing |
| [Choosing a venue and submitting](../03_write-and-publish/02_venue-and-submission.md) | Fields differ a lot: a 2013 statement by the American Historical Association calls history a "book-based" discipline[^aha2013embargo-e1]; the American Philosophical Association said in 1996 that far more philosophers write only articles than write only books[^apaphil1996research-e8]. Check the Fields section first |

## 5. Which fields use it

This lists only fields our sources give evidence for; our sources do not cover interpretive research in sociology and anthropology.

- **History**: aims at argument and narrative[^cronon2009learning-e3]. For evidence and archival work, see [History and archaeology](../07_fields/history-archaeology/README.md).
- **Literary studies**: proposes interpretations starting from close reading[^kain1998closereading-e2]. See [Languages and literature](../07_fields/languages-literature/README.md).
- **Philosophy and ethics**: according to the American Philosophical Association's statement, philosophy papers are rarely reports of empirical research[^apaphil1996research-e12], and what matters most is the quality of the reasoning[^apaphil1996research-e9]. See [Philosophy, ethics, and religion](../07_fields/philosophy-ethics-religion/README.md).
- **Theology and religious studies**: philosophers and theologians both interpret authoritative texts, make arguments, and marshal evidence[^wood2021philosophy-e1]; on one view presented in a *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry, theologians may treat revelation as evidence, while philosophers may appeal only to premises and evidence available to any rational inquirer[^wood2021philosophy-e4].
- **Law**: the several reports on legal research that Hutchinson and Duncan review all regard doctrinal research as the most accepted research method in law[^hutchinson2012defining-e5]. See [Law](../07_fields/law/README.md).
- **Art history, musicology, theater studies**[^oecd2015frascati-e12]: source criticism and archival work are much as in history `[Our analysis]`. See [Arts](../07_fields/arts/README.md).

## 6. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Restating the text or other people's views without arguing | A philosophy paper must offer an argument[^pryor2012writing-e2]; if you quote without analyzing, readers can't see your reasoning[^uncwritinglit-e8] |
| A thesis nobody would disagree with | If it only summarizes what readers can see for themselves, there is no one to persuade[^harvardwcthesis-e2] |
| Picking only material that supports you | The American Historical Association requires that you not leave out contrary evidence[^aha2023standards-e6]; otherwise you easily fall into confirmation bias[^cronon2009learning-e7] |
| Coming to the topic with your conclusion already decided | The University of North Carolina Writing Center's handout warns against this[^uncwritinglit-e11]; when strong evidence pulls you away from your initial hypothesis, change the hypothesis[^cronon2009learning-e8] |
| Treating a primary source as the facts themselves | A record being authentic does not make its content reliable[^saaacrl2018primary-e20] |
| Reading only secondary scholarship and avoiding primary sources | Primary sources are hard to find and hard to read, and beginners tend to avoid them[^cronon2009learning-e20]; doctrinal research is more than a literature review[^hutchinson2012defining-e8] |
| Copying citations to primary sources from someone else's book | The American Historical Association considers this improper[^aha2023standards-e11] |
| Attacking a straw man | Students often attack a view that sounds similar but is actually different[^pryor2012writing-e12] |
| A keyword that changes meaning partway through | The argument only looks valid[^pryor2025terms-e19] |
| Reading the past through present-day eyes | This is called presentism[^hunt2002against-e1]; the American Historical Association's standards ask historians to see the world, as far as possible, through the eyes of the people of the time[^aha2023standards-e5] |
| Not documenting your process because "it can't be replicated anyway" | The step of finding materials is easy for others to replicate[^hutchinson2012defining-e30]; your notes should let others retrace the argument[^aha2023standards-e3] |

## 7. Further reading

- **Cronon et al., *Learning to Do Historical Research: A Primer***: a free introductory website written by environmental historians and students at the University of Wisconsin, covering asking questions, finding sources, working with manuscripts, and writing an argument.
- **American Historical Association, *Statement on Standards of Professional Conduct***: the code of professional conduct for American historians, covering primary and secondary sources, notes, and not cherry-picking evidence.
- **University of North Carolina Writing Center, *Literature*; Harvard College Writing Center, *How to Do a Close Reading* and *Counterargument***: handouts for undergraduates on how to get from close reading to a thesis and how to handle counterarguments.
- **Jim Pryor, *Guidelines on Writing a Philosophy Paper* and his handout on philosophical terms and methods**: written for undergraduates in American philosophy courses, on how to write and test a philosophical argument.
- **Hutchinson & Duncan 2012, *Defining and Describing What We Do: Doctrinal Legal Research***: mainly about Australia and the UK, on what doctrinal legal research is and how to write a methods statement.
- **How-to pages in this handbook**: [Archival research](../06_methods/archival_research.md), [Oral history](../06_methods/oral_history.md), [Close reading and literary argument](../07_fields/languages-literature/practices/close_reading.md), [Philosophical argument](../07_fields/philosophy-ethics-religion/practices/philosophical_argument.md), [Doctrinal legal research](../07_fields/law/practices/doctrinal_research.md).
