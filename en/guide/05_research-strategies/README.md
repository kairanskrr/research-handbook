# Research strategies

> The categories here are designed to work across fields; the examples and concrete steps come from computer science. "Interpretation and argument" is found mostly in the humanities and social sciences.

You have a research area, and maybe a few candidate conclusions written down. Now you want to know: what kind of research am I actually doing? What kind of evidence do I need to produce?

This page helps you place yourself. First see what question each of the eight strategies answers, then use your central claim to pick your main strategy, and finally see how the strategy helps you read the literature, choose problems, design the study, and write the paper.

A **research strategy** is the main logic by which a project produces new knowledge. For how it differs from a claim and from a method, and why the distinction matters, see [Key concepts](../00_start-here/key-concepts.md#research-strategy); we won't repeat it here.

---

## The eight strategies at a glance

Each strategy has its own page explaining what knowledge it produces, what evidence it needs, and which methods it commonly uses.

| Strategy | The question it answers | Typical claim (computer science example) |
|---|---|---|
| [Description](observe_describe.md) | What is X like? How common is it? | "40% of contracts generated by large language models have reentrancy vulnerabilities" |
| [Measurement](measure.md) | How should X be measured to be accurate? Are existing measures reliable? | "Existing benchmarks suffer from data leakage; the new benchmark is more reliable" |
| [Comparative evaluation](compare_evaluate.md) | Of the existing A and B, which is better? | "On real-world contracts, tool A has a higher detection rate than B" |
| [Causal explanation](explain_causality.md) | Why does this happen? Does X cause Y? | "Prompting with vulnerability patterns raises the detection rate" |
| [Building and improving](build_improve.md) | How can X be done better? | "The new method finds 30% more vulnerabilities" |
| [Synthesis](synthesize.md) | What do existing studies, taken together, show? | "Research in this area falls into four groups, with three gaps" |
| [Proof](prove.md) | Under what conditions must X hold? | "Under condition C, the algorithm always terminates" |
| [Interpretation and argument](interpret_argue.md) | How should this text, institution, or idea be understood? | Mainly the humanities and social sciences |

**This is not an accepted standard classification.** This handbook put it together to help newcomers find their bearings, and we will keep testing and adjusting it as we write the chapters of Part 1 and Part 2 and the computer science pages. The papers you read won't call themselves "comparative evaluation"; other books may split these categories more finely or merge them more coarsely. For where it lines up with existing classifications and where it is this handbook's own addition, see [Where this grouping comes from](#where-this-grouping-comes-from). Treat it as a map that helps you think, not as rules you must follow.

## How to pick your main strategy

**Decide by your central claim, not by your topic and not by the methods you know.** There are three steps.

**Step 1: write down your central claim.** That is, the single most important conclusion of your paper. If you can't write it yet, go back to Section 1 of [Converging on a project](../01_find-a-problem/05_converge.md#1-write-down-the-sentence-you-want-to-prove).

**Step 2: see which kind of statement it is.** Check it against these:

| Your claim says that… | Main strategy |
|---|---|
| A phenomenon exists, what it looks like, how common it is, how it is distributed | Description |
| Something was measured wrongly before, or a new way of measuring is more accurate | Measurement |
| Among several **existing** approaches, which is better, under what conditions | Comparative evaluation |
| A factor **causes** an outcome | Causal explanation |
| Something you **newly built** is better than what exists | Building and improving |
| What a body of existing studies shows when taken together | Synthesis |
| A conclusion necessarily holds under given conditions | Proof |
| A new interpretation of a text, an institution, or an idea | Interpretation and argument |

**Step 3: if you have several kinds of contribution, decide which is primary.** Say you propose a new detection method and, to evaluate it, also assemble a new dataset. Ask yourself: if readers could remember only one sentence, which would it be? If it is "the new method finds 30% more vulnerabilities", your main strategy is building and improving, and the dataset is a secondary contribution. If it is "existing benchmarks seriously overestimate results", your main strategy is measurement, and the new method is just an illustration along the way.

**Pairs that are easy to confuse:**

- **Comparative evaluation vs. building and improving**: both run comparison experiments. The difference is whether the thing being compared is something you newly built. Comparing only existing tools is comparative evaluation; comparing a new method against existing tools is building and improving.
- **Comparative evaluation vs. causal explanation**: "A is better than B" is not the same as "some feature of A makes it better". To say "causes", you have to rule out alternative explanations.
- **Description vs. causal explanation**: "X and Y occur together" is description; "X causes Y" is causal. Mistaking correlation for causation is a common error, and showing causation is usually much harder than showing correlation[^easterbrook2008selecting-e36].
- **Description vs. measurement**: description **uses** measurement to reach conclusions about a phenomenon; in measurement, the contribution is **the way of measuring itself**. "40% of contracts have vulnerabilities" is description; "the old counting method overcounted vulnerabilities" is measurement.
- **Description vs. synthesis**: description studies the phenomenon itself (contracts, code, developers); synthesis studies existing research.

The three steps and the lookup table above are this handbook's own synthesis.

**Write it down:** Write your central claim and use the table above to decide your main strategy. If you have two kinds of contribution, write down "the one sentence I'd keep if I could keep only one", and use it to decide which is primary.

## Where this grouping comes from

The eight strategies are this handbook's own grouping. The closest existing classifications are two sources on "what types of research questions there are", **both from software engineering**:

- **Easterbrook et al. 2008**: on how to choose methods for **empirical research** in software engineering. It analyzes which kinds of questions each method is best suited to answer[^easterbrook2008selecting-e30], and treats getting clear about the research question as one of the first steps in choosing a method[^easterbrook2008selecting-e31]. It covers only empirical research: it says empirical research answers knowledge questions about the way the world is, whereas most non-empirical research in software engineering focuses on a different kind of question, namely how to design better ways of doing things[^easterbrook2008selecting-e38]; it calls these design questions[^easterbrook2008selecting-e39].
- **Shaw 2003**: divides the questions software engineering research answers into four kinds: methods of development or analysis; the design or evaluation of a particular instance; generalizations over whole classes of systems or techniques; and exploration of existence or feasibility[^shaw2003writing-e38].

The table below is our own mapping of the eight strategies onto these two sources. "No counterpart" means that source has no corresponding question type:

| This handbook's strategy | Easterbrook et al. 2008 | Shaw 2003 |
|---|---|---|
| Description | Existence questions and description and classification questions ("Does X exist?", "What is X like?", "How can it be categorized?")[^easterbrook2008selecting-e32]; base-rate questions (how common a phenomenon normally is)[^easterbrook2008selecting-e34]; relationship questions ("Are X and Y related?")[^easterbrook2008selecting-e35] | Feasibility or exploration ("Does X even exist, and if so what is it like?")[^shaw2003writing-e8]; particular instance ("What is the current state of X?")[^shaw2003writing-e39]; generalization ("What are the varieties of X?")[^shaw2003writing-e40] |
| Measurement | Only partly: the "how can we measure it" part of description and classification questions[^easterbrook2008selecting-e32] | Only partly: methods of analysis ("How can I evaluate the quality or correctness of X?")[^shaw2003writing-e25] |
| Comparative evaluation | Descriptive-comparative questions ("How does X differ from Y?")[^easterbrook2008selecting-e33]; causality-comparative questions ("Does X cause more Y than does Z?")[^easterbrook2008selecting-e37] | Particular instance ("How good is Y?", "How does X compare to Y?")[^shaw2003writing-e39]; "How do I choose between X and Y?"[^shaw2003writing-e25] |
| Causal explanation | Causality questions ("Does X cause Y?")[^easterbrook2008selecting-e37] | **No counterpart** |
| Building and improving | Design questions ("What's an effective way to achieve X?")[^easterbrook2008selecting-e39] | Methods of development ("How can we do X?", "What is a better way to do X?")[^shaw2003writing-e25]; a better design for a particular instance[^shaw2003writing-e39] |
| Synthesis | **No counterpart**; it only notes that exploratory questions may already be answered in the literature, and a literature survey can answer them[^easterbrook2008selecting-e41] | **No counterpart** |
| Proof | **No counterpart** (outside the scope of empirical research) | Generalization ("Given X, what will Y necessarily be?")[^shaw2003writing-e40]; a formal model must be validated by rigorous derivation and proof[^shaw2003writing-e9] |
| Interpretation and argument | **No counterpart** | **No counterpart** |

As you can see, synthesis and interpretation and argument have no counterpart in either source; causal explanation has none in Shaw, and proof has none in Easterbrook; measurement is only part of one question type in each. These are the categories this handbook separated out or split off to cover more fields and help newcomers find their bearings. Both sources come from software engineering, so the mapping fits computer science best; our sources do not cover how other fields classify research questions.

**Two other classifications you may have seen.** They sort along different dimensions and don't conflict with the strategies; any strategy can fit into them:

- **By aim (OECD, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development)**: basic research seeks to understand the underlying foundations of phenomena, without any particular application in view; applied research is directed toward a specific practical aim[^oecd2015frascati-e8]; experimental development draws on existing knowledge to produce new products or processes, or to improve existing ones[^oecd2015frascati-e9].
- **By the nature of the conclusion (Brooks, as reported by Shaw)**: findings (judged by truthfulness and rigor), observations (judged by interestingness), and rules of thumb (judged by usefulness)[^shaw2002makes-e5].

## How strategies differ from methods

**Settle the claim and the strategy first, then pick methods.** One method can serve several strategies, and one project often combines several methods. Examples from computer science:

| Research type | Strategies it can serve |
|---|---|
| [Controlled experiments](../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md) | Causal explanation, comparative evaluation, building and improving |
| [Benchmarking](../07_fields/computer-science/practices/benchmarking.md) | Comparative evaluation, building and improving, measurement |
| [Mining software repositories](../07_fields/computer-science/practices/repository_mining.md) | Description, causal explanation |
| [Questionnaire surveys](../06_methods/questionnaire_survey.md), [Interview studies](../06_methods/interview_study.md) | Description, causal explanation (as supporting evidence) |
| [Review studies](../06_methods/review_study.md) | Synthesis |
| [Theoretical research](../07_fields/computer-science/practices/theoretical_research.md) | Proof |

Why combine methods: every method has known weaknesses and can only provide limited evidence; but the weaknesses differ between methods, so you can choose several that compensate for each other[^easterbrook2008selecting-e1]. There is no fixed recipe for which ones; it depends on your resources, whether you can access the people or things you want to study, whether you can control the variables of interest, and what you yourself know how to do[^easterbrook2008selecting-e42].

Why you shouldn't work the other way round, starting from "I know how to run experiments" or "everyone else does it this way": borrowing a research paradigm someone used for a completely different question leads to serious mismatches[^shaw2003writing-e41]. For how to get from a strategy to concrete methods, see [Choosing a method](../02_do-the-project/02_choose-method.md).

## Why to keep your strategy in mind from start to finish

The strategy isn't only useful when you design experiments; at every stage it helps you ask the right questions. The stage-by-stage advice below is this handbook's own synthesis; most of the sources cited come from software engineering, and in other fields your own field's conventions take precedence.

**When reading the literature.** First ask which strategy a paper's central claim belongs to, then judge it by that strategy's standards. Don't apply quality standards that don't fit, for example demanding internal validity (whether the causal conclusion really holds) of a study that makes no causal claim[^ralph2020empirical-e24].

**When choosing a problem.** Some strategies often need to come first. Early in a line of research, you usually need to ask exploratory questions to understand the phenomenon[^easterbrook2008selecting-e32]; design questions assume the related knowledge questions have already been answered, so that you understand the problem well enough[^easterbrook2008selecting-e40]. In the running example: if no one even knows whether existing evaluation results can be trusted, a measurement project may be worth more than rushing into a new detection method. Conversely, an exploratory question may already be answered in the literature; then reading the literature answers it, and you don't need to do the study again yourself[^easterbrook2008selecting-e41].

**When designing the study.** Questions, results, and validation can't be mixed and matched freely. Using a field study to validate whether a formal model is correct is a mismatch[^shaw2003writing-e14]; a formal model must be supported by rigorous derivation and proof, not by one or two simple examples[^shaw2003writing-e9]. For a thoughtful researcher, choosing the right type of result for the question is usually not hard; choosing the validation well is the hard part, and it is often why papers fail[^shaw2003writing-e41][^shaw2003writing-e42]. Each strategy page lists the evidence that strategy needs; use it as a checklist.

**When writing the paper.** Readers must be able to see at a glance what kind of knowledge you are delivering. If reviewers can't tell whether you are proposing a new evaluation technique, applying an old technique to a new example, or evaluating a mature technique, you haven't made it clear[^shaw2003writing-e31]. The validation must also match the claim: if you claim a performance improvement, analyze performance, not ease of use or generality[^shaw2003writing-e12].

**Write it down:** Take a paper you read recently and write down its central claim and main strategy. Then one sentence: by that strategy's standards, what evidence does it most need, and does it provide it?
