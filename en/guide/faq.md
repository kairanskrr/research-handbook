# FAQ: quick answers

This page covers only situations that the chapters of Part 1, Part 2, and Writing and publishing don't: ones that don't belong to any single stage and can come up at any time; ones where you have to make the call yourself; ones you can't easily raise with your advisor, or where the problem is between you and your advisor; and ones that are costly to get wrong. The step-by-step approach is in those chapters. If this is your first research project, start with [The research journey at a glance: from interest to publication](00_start-here/research-journey.md).

Each entry has three parts: **First, work out** which situation you are in, **What to do now**, and where to **Read more**.

## Is this project still worth doing?

### You worry someone has already had your idea, or halfway through you find a paper that just came out

**First, work out: have you started yet, or are you already some way in?**

- **Not started yet**: the worst outcome is finishing the whole project and only then finding that someone published the same work before you began. That is why you check for prior work at the very start[^noble2023ten-e5]. If you find a similar paper, don't give up straight away. Compare it with yours as described below; often only the question is the same, and the angle or the evidence differs. For how to search and compare, see [Converging on a project: Has someone already done it?](01_find-a-problem/05_converge.md#3-has-someone-already-done-it).
- **Already some way in**: put their paper next to your work and compare three things: the claim, the data or subjects, and the method. If all three are the same, you have been scooped. If any one differs, you hold a part their paper doesn't cover. If the results are the same but you reached them independently, your work can serve as an independent confirmation. The three steps below are for this case.

**What to do now:**
1. Adjust your claim to match the differences you found. If the overlap is only partial, aim your claim at the part they don't cover; if the results are the same, write it up as an independent confirmation.
2. Talk to your advisor about where to submit. A 2018 editorial from the biology journal PLOS Biology notes that many journals reject scooped manuscripts for "lack of novelty"[^plosbiology2018importance-e2]. That journal takes the view that two groups independently finding the same phenomenon increases confidence in the result[^plosbiology2018importance-e3], and it formally considers manuscripts that confirm or extend a recently published study[^plosbiology2018importance-e1]. The window is six months from when the other paper is published or posted as a preprint[^plosbiology2018importance-e4], and the manuscript must still meet the journal's requirements for depth of study[^plosbiology2018importance-e5]. This is one journal's policy. Check the rules of the place you plan to submit to before you submit.
3. Cite their work openly in your paper and say clearly how yours differs. Don't avoid it.

**Read more:** [Replication studies: how it differs from related work](06_methods/replication.md#how-it-differs-from-related-work); [When the evidence doesn't match the claim, change the claim](02_do-the-project/07_analyze-and-interpret.md#7-when-the-evidence-doesnt-match-the-claim-change-the-claim)

### Your advisor gave you a topic you aren't interested in, or don't think is worth doing

**First, work out**: try writing down concrete reasons why "this topic isn't worth doing". If you can write reasons that can be checked (someone has already done it, the data can't be obtained, even a finished result wouldn't answer any real question), you have a value or feasibility problem. If all you can come up with is "it's boring", you have an interest problem.

**What to do now:**
1. Interest problem: don't change direction yet. Look inside the topic for an angle that excites you[^ruder2020tips-e17]. Early in your career it is normal to depend heavily on the directions your advisor gives you[^nanda2025taste-e9]. There is no shame in working on someone else's idea; the skill lies in which ones you pick and how well you execute them[^schulman2020opinionated-e4].
2. Value or feasibility problem: write your reasons down, bring a small-scale check, and discuss it with your advisor. Don't change direction on your own.
3. If your advisor still insists after you have talked: work on it their way for a while, and agree on when, and on what results, you will reassess together. One researcher recalls ignoring his advisor; the advisor turned out to be right, and he wasted several months[^karpathy2016survival-e7].

**Read more:** [The project is yours: propose the next steps](08_throughout/collaboration_and_advisors.md#4-the-project-is-yours-propose-the-next-steps); [Is it worth doing?](01_find-a-problem/05_converge.md#2-is-it-worth-doing)

## You did the work, but can't say what it amounts to

### You ran experiments and got results first, and only afterwards asked "what am I actually trying to show?"

**First, work out:** can you write down a specific hypothesis and say what evidence would tell you whether it is right or wrong[^nanda2025process-e5]? If you can't, you are still exploring, not testing. When new researchers get stuck early in a project, it is often because they think they are testing when they are still exploring[^nanda2025process-e2].

**Where the danger is:** using the same data both to generate a hypothesis and to test it is circular reasoning[^nosek2018preregistration-e10]. If you then present your after-the-fact explanation as something you predicted in advance, readers can no longer tell which parts are discoveries and which are tests[^nosek2018preregistration-e11]. Exploration itself is fine; what matters is making clear which is which[^nosek2018preregistration-e4].

**What to do now:**
1. Distill what you have found so far into a one-sentence claim.
2. Test it on new data. A methods article on preregistration gives two ways to do this. If data are easy to collect, treat all the initial experiments as exploratory; once you see something interesting, freeze the original design and analysis script and run the experiment again[^nosek2018preregistration-e13]. If data are hard to collect, split the dataset in two: use one part for exploration and keep the other sealed until exploration is complete[^nosek2018preregistration-e12].
3. In the paper, say which conclusions came from exploration and which were tested on new data.

**Read more:** [Exploring hands-on](01_find-a-problem/03_explore.md); [Do the planned analyses first; label unplanned ones as exploratory](02_do-the-project/07_analyze-and-interpret.md#2-run-the-planned-analyses-first-label-unplanned-ones-as-exploratory)

### You built a system or tool, and don't know whether it counts as research or what the contribution is

**First, work out**: try filling in this sentence: "We built X; it does better than existing approaches on Y; the evidence is Z." If you can't fill in Z, what you have is a thing, not yet a research result. One exception: if the question itself was "can this be done at all?", a working system may be enough to answer it[^shaw2003writing-e10].

**What to do now** (using software engineering as the example)**:**
1. Add a comparison with existing approaches. The empirical standards for software engineering research list as antipatterns both "evaluation consists only of users' subjective opinions" and "only performance numbers, not compared with established benchmarks or alternative solutions"[^ralph2020empirical-e92].
2. Show evidence that it helps other people, not just you. An article on writing software engineering papers says this is exactly what program committees look for[^shaw2003writing-e35].
3. Check the evaluation conventions of your field with your advisor before you settle on an evaluation plan.

**Read more:** [Building and improving: what kind of knowledge this research produces](05_research-strategies/build_improve.md#1-what-kind-of-knowledge-this-research-produces); [Building and improving: what evidence is enough](05_research-strategies/build_improve.md#2-what-evidence-is-enough)

### Your advisor or a reviewer says "not enough contribution" or "lacks novelty". What do they mean?

**First, work out**: the comment can mean four different things: you haven't made clear what is new; the work overlaps too much with existing work; it's new but not important; or the evidence doesn't support what you claim. If you can't tell which, ask directly: "Is it that it's too close to a particular piece of work, or that the evidence isn't enough?"

**What to do now:**
1. Not made clear: rewrite your contributions as statements of outcomes. A contribution is something the world did not know before this paper and now knows thanks to it[^vandeursen2013writing-e1]; it is an outcome, not the effort you put in[^vandeursen2013writing-e2]. Add a table comparing your work with the closest prior work.
2. Too much overlap: first look at how much overlap there is. Work that overlaps with prior work can still have some novelty if it offers new ideas and useful data[^hicks2014reviewing-e8]; changing just one thing that nobody had thought to change also counts as novel[^black2022novelty-e2]. Don't make your method more complicated to look new: a guide written for reviewers warns that reviewers often mistake complexity for novelty[^black2022novelty-e1].
3. Not important, or not enough evidence: go back to the convergence stage and narrow your claim, or add evidence, or weaken the claim.

**Read more:** [Introduction: five questions plus a list of contributions](03_write-and-publish/01_story-and-paper.md#4-introduction-five-questions-plus-a-list-of-contributions); [Has someone already done it?](01_find-a-problem/05_converge.md#3-has-someone-already-done-it); [Decide what to change and what to argue](03_write-and-publish/03_review-and-revision.md#3-decide-what-to-change-and-what-to-argue)

## Something went wrong, or time is running out

### You found an error in an earlier experiment or analysis, and the results are already out

**First, work out**: ask two things. How far have the results gone (a group meeting, a manuscript under review, a published paper)? And does the conclusion change once the error is fixed? The further the results have traveled and the more the conclusion changes, the more you need a formal correction. An error is not misconduct in itself: the American Geophysical Union's 2023 ethics policy states that honest errors in recording, selecting, or analyzing data are not research misconduct[^agu2023ethics-e11].

**What to do now:**
1. Stop, and write down what went wrong and which figures, tables, and conclusions it affects.
2. Tell your advisor and co-authors as soon as possible. Don't wait to "fix it quietly in the next version".
3. Correct it in proportion to how far it has gone. Internal only: send out the corrected results. Under review: discuss with your advisor whether to contact the editor or program chair (our sources do not cover the specific procedures at each venue). Already published: the 2023 *European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity* from ALLEA (All European Academies), called "the European Code" below, requires timely correction and, where necessary, retraction[^allea2023european-e11], and says that authors who issue corrections should be given credit for it[^allea2023european-e20].

**Read more:** [When you find an error, correct it yourself](08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#7-when-you-find-an-error-correct-it-yourself); [Report progress early, including bad news](08_throughout/collaboration_and_advisors.md#3-report-progress-early-including-bad-news)

### The scope keeps growing and you don't know when it's enough

**First, work out**: for each new piece of work you add, ask: if I dropped it, would the central claim be missing evidence? If yes, you are finishing this project. If no, you are starting a new one.

**What to do now:**
1. Write down what "done" means: which figures and which tables the paper must have. The open handbook The Turing Way, in its section on project design, lists lessons learned from mistakes; one of them is not pushing for clear goals and success criteria from the beginning[^turingwaydesign-e1].
2. Check against your paper outline: the figures and tables still missing tell you how far you are from submission.
3. Put new questions on your ideas list for the next project. People judge you by the projects you finish, not the ones you start[^patterson2001bad-e11].

**Read more:** [Break the claim into milestones: each milestone is a piece of evidence](02_do-the-project/03_research-plan.md#2-break-the-claim-into-milestones-each-milestone-is-a-piece-of-evidence); [For each milestone, write down what counts as done](02_do-the-project/03_research-plan.md#3-for-each-milestone-write-down-what-counts-as-done)

### The deadline is close and the evidence isn't there yet

**First, work out**: draw two columns. On the left, list each conclusion; on the right, the figure, table, or experiment that supports it. Where the right side is empty, is that the central claim or a supporting conclusion? Can you fill the gap in the time left?

**What to do now:**
1. A supporting conclusion lacks evidence: cut it, or present it as a "preliminary observation".
2. The central claim lacks evidence: narrow the claim to what the evidence supports, or aim for a later deadline. Do not quietly drop unfavorable data or results to keep the claim: the European Code defines omitting or suppressing data or results without justification as falsification[^allea2023european-e3].
3. Decide with your advisor whether to submit now or wait. An opinion piece in computing criticized the habit of submitting when a conference deadline comes around rather than when the research is ready[^fortnow2009time-e2].

**Read more:** [When the evidence doesn't match the claim, change the claim](02_do-the-project/07_analyze-and-interpret.md#7-when-the-evidence-doesnt-match-the-claim-change-the-claim); [How the plan changes](02_do-the-project/03_research-plan.md#7-how-the-plan-changes)

## Between you and your advisor or collaborators

### You disagree with collaborators about authorship

**First, work out:** is the dispute about who should be an author, or about author order? For who should be an author, go through the European Code's four criteria one by one: a significant contribution; drafting or critically reviewing the paper; approving the final version; and agreeing to be responsible for the content[^allea2023european-e7]. People who contributed but don't meet the criteria go in the acknowledgments[^allea2023european-e8]. Order is agreed among the authors[^allea2023european-e8][^icmje2026recommendations-e27], and conventions differ by field: economics lists authors alphabetically by surname[^einav2006surname-e1], psychology by contribution[^einav2006surname-e3].

**What to do now:**
1. Bring out the record of who did what and check each person against the criteria above. If there is no record, make one now and send it to everyone to confirm.
2. Ask your advisor or the project lead to chair a face-to-face discussion, and write down what you agree. Next time, raise it when the work starts: the 2026 recommendations of the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) advise deciding who qualifies as an author ideally at the planning stage, and adjusting as the work progresses[^icmje2026recommendations-e4].
3. If you still can't agree: a 2003 guide on authorship disputes for new researchers from the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE), by Albert and Wager, advises negotiating with the people involved and backing your view with evidence such as lab notebooks, manuscripts, and authorship guidelines. If you remain unhappy with your advisor's decision, you can appeal to the department head or dean, but only in exceptional circumstances, and make sure your advisor knows[^albert2003authorship-e1].
4. Don't expect the journal to settle it for you. The ICMJE recommendations state that the institution where the work was done should investigate, not the journal editor[^icmje2026recommendations-e5]. COPE's 2019 *Authorship discussion document* (version 2) also says editors are not in a position to fairly adjudicate authorship disputes; a manuscript under review can be put on hold until you resolve it[^cope2019authorship-e1][^cope2019authorship-e2]. Changing the order or adding or removing authors on a published paper requires the agreement of all authors[^cope2019authorship-e3]. If you have been left off, you can contact the journal, but the 2003 guide warns that an editor is unlikely to add your name without the other authors' agreement[^albert2003authorship-e2]. Our sources do not cover the specific procedures at individual institutions.

**Read more:** [Authorship: discuss it at the start and adjust as you go](08_throughout/collaboration_and_advisors.md#6-authorship-discuss-it-at-the-start-and-adjust-as-you-go); for field conventions, see [Economics](07_fields/economics/README.md#alphabetical-authorship) and [Mathematics](07_fields/mathematics/README.md#one-person-or-a-few-listed-alphabetically)

### Communication with your advisor isn't working, or your advisor has no time for you

**First, work out**: think back over the last month. If you ask for meetings and your advisor keeps postponing or not replying, your advisor is too busy. If you rarely ask, and don't want to show your results, you are avoiding them. If you meet but the two of you disagree on how far along you should be, expectations haven't been made clear.

**What to do now:**
1. Expectations unclear: ask your advisor directly how they supervise students and what they expect of you[^dredze2012successful-e7]. Write down how often you will meet and how you will report in a few lines, and send it to your advisor to confirm.
2. You are avoiding them: send results as you go, rather than saving them up so your advisor sees them for the first time in a meeting[^huang2023advisor-e1]. If you are falling behind, say so early; your advisor would rather know sooner[^dredze2012successful-e20].
3. Advisor too busy: pair up with a senior member of the group, communicate with them regularly day to day, and report larger milestones to your advisor[^herremans2020successful-e2].

**Read more:** [Ask how your advisor supervises, and tell them about yourself](08_throughout/collaboration_and_advisors.md#1-ask-how-your-advisor-supervises-and-tell-them-about-yourself); [Make every meeting count](08_throughout/collaboration_and_advisors.md#2-make-every-meeting-count)

### You and your advisor disagree (for example, your advisor wants to submit and you think it isn't ready, or the other way round)

**First, work out**: is the disagreement about facts (is the evidence enough, are the results trustworthy) or about judgment (direction, where to submit, when to submit)? Factual disagreements can usually be settled with one more experiment or one table. Judgment calls have no standard answer, and your advisor usually has more experience than you.

Experienced people see this differently. One piece of advice for graduate students says your advisor isn't always right, but has been doing research longer than you, so do what your advisor asks[^dredze2012successful-e8]. Another researcher argues that when you are sure your advisor is wrong, you need the courage to sometimes not listen[^karpathy2016survival-e6].

**What to do now**:
1. Factual disagreement: bring your reasons and a small-scale check to a meeting and discuss them. Don't change direction on your own.
2. Disagreement about whether to submit: lay out the "conclusion / evidence" two-column table together, and ask whether reviewers will ask about the missing evidence.
3. A judgment call you still want to stand by: first agree with your advisor how long you will try, and what result will make you stop.

**Read more:** [The project is yours: propose the next steps](08_throughout/collaboration_and_advisors.md#4-the-project-is-yours-propose-the-next-steps); [Cultivate research taste on purpose](08_throughout/mindset_and_growth.md#1-cultivate-research-taste-on-purpose) (includes "Follow your advisor, or trust your own judgment?")

### Your advisor says something different from this handbook. Whom do you follow?

**First, work out**: is the difference about the rules of your field and venue (format, author order, whether to include a contribution list), or about the bottom line of research integrity (fabrication, falsification, plagiarism)?

**What to do now:**
1. On rules, generally go with your advisor. These rules really do vary by field and venue: author order differs between economics and psychology[^einav2006surname-e1][^einav2006surname-e3], and the ICMJE recommendations themselves say they should be used together with each journal's instructions for authors[^icmje2026recommendations-e1]. Many of this handbook's examples come from computer science, and your advisor knows your target venues better.
2. If you want to understand why, show your advisor the source the handbook cites and ask, "Why do we do it this way here?".
3. The integrity bottom line is the exception: fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism[^allea2023european-e3] are off limits no matter who asks.

**Read more:** [Research integrity and ethics](08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md); [Choosing a venue and submitting](03_write-and-publish/02_venue-and-submission.md)

### You suspect a collaborator may have crossed a line

**First, work out:** is it an honest error or a difference in interpretation, or is it fabrication, falsification, or plagiarism? The American Geophysical Union's policy states that errors of judgment, honest errors in data, and differences of opinion about interpreting data are not misconduct[^agu2023ethics-e11]; the European Code defines misconduct as fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism[^allea2023european-e3]. One test we suggest: can you state what you saw in one sentence, such as "they changed X to Y", "they made up Z", or "they copied W without credit"? If you can't, treat it as a question to clear up first.

**What to do now:**
1. Write down the specific facts you saw (which data, which figure, when). Don't guess at motives.
2. Ask about it privately first, or talk it over with your advisor.
3. If you still suspect misconduct, make a formal report. Taking the US as an example: the Office of Research Integrity (ORI) of the US Department of Health and Human Services, which covers research funded by the US Public Health Service (PHS), advises reading your institution's policy before making an allegation, to learn whom to report to and what protections you have as the person reporting[^oriwhistleblowers-e1], and submitting the allegation to the official the policy designates[^oriwhistleblowers-e2]. An allegation in good faith is one made with an honest belief that misconduct may have occurred[^oriwhistleblowers-e3]. Under PHS rules, institutions must protect good-faith complainants from retaliation[^oriwhistleblowers-e4], and making sure the matter is properly investigated is the responsibility of the investigating body and ORI, not yours[^oriwhistleblowers-e5]. In other countries and institutions, follow your own institution's rules.

**Read more:** [Know the red lines: fabrication, falsification, plagiarism](08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#1-know-the-red-lines-fabrication-falsification-plagiarism)

## You and yourself

### You feel you aren't cut out for research, the pressure is high, and you can't keep going

**First, work out**: ask three things. Are you stuck on this project, or have you lost interest in research itself? Has it been a hard month, or have you had no energy for more than a year? Are your sleep, eating, and daily life affected? If you are doing a PhD, also know that two to three years in is when the most people drop out[^might2010successful-e2]; if it feels hard at that point, the problem isn't only you.

**What to do now:**
1. Stuck on the project: treat it as a project problem. Work through the troubleshooting steps, or decide whether to change direction.
2. Going through a hard patch: protect your sleep and routine first, and talk to your advisor or peers. Don't carry it alone.
3. No energy for a long time, or no interest in research itself: have a serious conversation with your advisor or someone you trust about whether to stay. Leaving is a legitimate choice too.
4. It is already affecting your life: go to your university's counseling or mental health service.

**Read more:** [When you're stuck, troubleshoot in fixed steps](02_do-the-project/06_run-and-iterate.md#5-when-youre-stuck-troubleshoot-in-fixed-steps); [Persist or change direction](02_do-the-project/06_run-and-iterate.md#6-persist-or-change-direction); [Treat failure and rejection as normal](08_throughout/mindset_and_growth.md#2-treat-failure-and-rejection-as-normal) (includes "Persist, or let go?"); [Keep a pace you can sustain](08_throughout/mindset_and_growth.md#6-keep-a-pace-you-can-sustain)

## Is this crossing the line?

### You aren't sure whether a practice crosses the line (for example, removing outliers or reporting only your best run)

**First, work out:** the red lines are clear: fabrication, falsification, plagiarism. Omitting or suppressing data or results without justification counts as falsification[^allea2023european-e3]. The hard part is the gray zone: running many times and reporting only the best run[^zeller2021devil-e1]; or, when the first analysis isn't significant, checking whether outliers were interfering with the effect, looking only at a subset of participants, or coding the variable differently[^nelson2018psychology-e6]. Psychologists' simulations show that stacking several such flexible choices can raise the false-positive rate (finding an effect that isn't there) from 5% to 61%[^nelson2018psychology-e7].

**A self-check**: if readers knew exactly what you did, would your conclusion still stand? Feynman put it as reporting everything you think might make your result invalid[^feynman1974cargo-e3], giving all the information so others can judge the value of your contribution for themselves[^feynman1974cargo-e5]. The same goes for image processing: editors at a cell biology journal suggest asking, for every adjustment, whether the adjusted image is still an accurate representation of the original data[^rossner2004what-e11].

**What to do now**:
1. Set the rules before you see the results, for example the criteria for excluding outliers. Report honestly which rules you used and how much you excluded.
2. Label unplanned processing as exploratory, and explain why you did it.
3. If you aren't sure, ask your advisor, and record your decision and reasoning in your log.

**Read more:** [Be honest with the evidence: don't fool yourself, report all results](08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#2-be-honest-with-the-evidence-dont-fool-yourself-report-all-results); [Do the planned analyses first; label unplanned ones as exploratory](02_do-the-project/07_analyze-and-interpret.md#2-run-the-planned-analyses-first-label-unplanned-ones-as-exploratory)

### Can you get help from a large language model?

**First, work out**: are you asking it for work you can check item by item, such as editing text or writing code, or to generate content you haven't verified (references, data, analytical conclusions)? For the first, check whether your venue requires a disclosure. For the second, if something is wrong, you are still responsible.

**What to do now:**
1. Check everything it generates, item by item, especially references. Take NeurIPS 2025 (Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems, a machine learning conference) as an example: authors are responsible for the entire content of the paper, whatever tools they used[^neurips2025llm-e6], and using references generated by a large language model without verifying them is a violation there[^neurips2025llm-e7].
2. Before submitting, read your target conference's or journal's own policy and disclose as it requires.
3. If you aren't sure whether to disclose, state what you used and what for, and tell your advisor.

**Read more:** [When you use large language models and other tools, you are still responsible](08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#6-when-you-use-large-language-models-and-other-tools-you-are-still-responsible) (includes how the rules differ across venues)
