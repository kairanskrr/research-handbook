# How to use this handbook

## Who this handbook is for

You might be a student doing research for the first time. Your advisor has given you a research area, or you are curious about a topic yourself, but you don't know where to start or how far each step needs to go. Or you have done a project or two already, you are stuck at one stage, and you want a method you can follow.

This handbook is written for you. It does not teach the subject knowledge of any one field. It teaches **how to take a study from a vague idea all the way to publication**: what you need to figure out at each stage, what you should produce, and how to tell whether you are ready to move on.

Before you start, spend ten minutes on two pages:

- [The research journey at a glance](research-journey.md): the stages a study passes through from start to finish, and where you will loop back;
- [Key concepts](key-concepts.md): research area, problem, research question, claim, contribution, evidence, research strategy, research methods, and field conventions. The whole handbook uses these words in the sense defined on that page.

---

## The handbook has two layers: the Parts and the Reference

**The Parts** follow the order in which research happens and tell you **what you need to decide now**. There are four of them, one per stage; read them in order:

| Stage | Where you need to get to |
|---|---|
| 1. [From interest to project](../01_find-a-problem/01_enter-a-field.md) | Start from a research area, read the literature, find problems, converge on a project that holds up, then write a research plan |
| 2. [From project to study design](../02_do-the-project/01_question-to-evidence.md) | Turn what you want to show into the evidence you need, choose a research strategy and methods, work out how far your conclusions can reach, and run a pilot study first |
| 3. [Running the study and analyzing results](../02_do-the-project/06_run-and-iterate.md) | Carry out the study, adjust as you go, then analyze the results and interpret what they show |
| 4. [Writing and publishing](../03_write-and-publish/01_story-and-paper.md) | Write the study up as a paper, choose where to submit, and handle reviews and revisions |

**The Reference** is not read in order. The Parts link to it when you need it, and you can also look things up directly:

| Reference | What it covers | When to look here |
|---|---|---|
| [Research strategies](../05_research-strategies/README.md) | What knowledge each of the eight kinds of research produces, what evidence it needs, and which methods it usually uses | You have written your central claim and want to know what kind of research you are doing and what evidence reviewers will expect |
| [Research types](../06_methods/README.md) | How to do a specific method well, such as a [Controlled experiments](../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md), an [interview study](../06_methods/interview_study.md), or a [questionnaire survey](../06_methods/questionnaire_survey.md); for methods used only in one field, see [field-specific practices](../07_fields/README.md#field-specific-practices) and [Research practices specific to computer science](../07_fields/computer-science/practices/README.md) | You have chosen a strategy and are about to collect and analyze evidence |
| [Fields](../07_fields/README.md) | The conventions of a particular field or research community, such as its publishing culture and ethics approval | You are entering a field, or want to know "what evidence counts in this community" |
| [Throughout the project](../08_throughout/README.md) | Things that don't belong to one stage but come up all the time: planning and daily routines, organizing your materials, working with your advisor and collaborators, integrity and ethics, mindset and growth, being a reviewer | When these things come up |
| [FAQ: quick answers](../faq.md) | Advice organized by "what has happened to you", with links to the detailed sections | You have a specific problem and want to go straight to a fix |

**How the Parts and the Reference fit together:** the Parts tell you which decision to make now; the Reference gives you the details you need to make it. For example, in [Converging on a project](../01_find-a-problem/05_converge.md) you write your claim and decide that it belongs to the "Building and improving" strategy. At that point you read [Building and improving](../05_research-strategies/build_improve.md) to see what evidence this kind of research needs; later, when you design the study, you look up how to carry out the specific methods. You don't need to read the Reference before you start. `[Our analysis]`

**Write it down:** In one sentence, describe where you are now, for example "My advisor gave me a research area and I haven't read any papers yet" or "I'm halfway through my experiments and the results aren't what I expected." The next section uses that sentence to pick a reading path for you.

## Pick a reading path for your situation

**This is your first research project and you are starting from scratch.**

1. Read [The research journey at a glance](research-journey.md) and [Key concepts](key-concepts.md) first;
2. Start Part 1 at [Entering a field and exploring the literature](../01_find-a-problem/01_enter-a-field.md) and read on in order, doing each chapter's exercises as you go;
3. When you hit a link to a research strategy, a method, or a field's conventions, follow it, read the parts relevant to you, then come back to the Part.

**You are already working on a project and are stuck at one stage.**

1. Skim [The research journey at a glance](research-journey.md) and find the stage you are in;
2. Go straight to that chapter and read its "Where you are" section first, to confirm it describes your situation;
3. Then look at that chapter's "Checklist before moving on": did you really meet the checklist of the stage before? Many times when you feel stuck, the real problem is one step back. For example, when an experiment won't come together, it is often because the sentence you set out to prove was never clear in the first place, and you need to go back to [Converging on a project](../01_find-a-problem/05_converge.md). `[Our analysis]`

**You need a specific method.**

1. If you already know which method to use, go straight to its page;
2. If you are not sure which method fits, first read the matching [Research strategies](../05_research-strategies/README.md). Each strategy page has a section on common research methods that tells you which methods this kind of research usually uses;
3. If you are still not sure which strategy you are in, read the section "How to choose your main strategy" in [Research strategies](../05_research-strategies/README.md#how-to-pick-your-main-strategy).

**You are moving into a new field or research area.**

1. First read [Entering a field and exploring the literature](../01_find-a-problem/01_enter-a-field.md) in Part 1: how to find the core papers, the key people, and the main publication venues;
2. Then read that field's overview in [Fields](../07_fields/README.md) to learn how it publishes, what ethics requirements apply, and what the common practices are;
3. If you are coming from another field, pay particular attention to how the new field's idea of "enough evidence" differs from your old one.

**You have a specific problem** (your advisor has no time, the experiment keeps failing, your paper was rejected...): check [FAQ: quick answers](../faq.md) first.

## Every chapter in the Parts has the same structure

Each chapter in the four Parts is laid out in this order:

| Section | What it does for you |
|---|---|
| Where you are | Confirms that the chapter is about your situation |
| What this stage is for | Tells you what you should have figured out by the end of the chapter |
| How to do it | Numbered sections on how to do the work, each ending with a short "**Write it down:**" exercise |
| Judgment calls, Common mistakes | How to choose at a fork in the road, and which traps other people often fall into |
| What you should have at the end | What you should be holding when this stage is done |
| Checklist before moving on | Which conditions you need to meet before you move to the next stage |
| When to go back | Which signs mean you should return to an earlier chapter, and which one |
| Next | What to read next |

**Do the "Write it down" exercises.** If you only read and never write, every chapter will feel clear, and you will still be stuck when your advisor asks, "So what exactly are you trying to show?" The exercises build up, step by step, into your project materials: a one-page project summary, a comparison table of related work, a research plan... These are the very things you and your advisor will discuss. `[Our analysis]`

**Treat the checklist as a self-check, not an exam.** You don't need to get every item perfect. But if there is an item you clearly can't meet, don't move on yet: look at the "When to go back" table, which tells you where to go to fill the gap.

**Going back and forth is normal.** Research is not a straight line you walk once. What you find later often sends you back to an earlier stage. The "When to go back" tables are for exactly those moments: going back is not a sign of failure; the table tells you where returning will cost you the least. `[Our analysis]`

## How to read the sources

Statements in this handbook come in two kinds, marked differently.

**Statements backed by a source carry a footnote.** A specific piece of advice from an author, a finding from a study, a requirement from a standard: each has a footnote at the end of the sentence. For example: when you write up your contribution, use verbs that show results, not verbs like "studied" or "explored" that only describe activity[^shaw2003writing-e18]. Open the footnote to see which source it comes from and where in the original; click "Evidence" to see the sentence or two from the original, so you can check it without finding the full text. For the format of the evidence notes, see [About the evidence notes](../../sources/notes/README.md).

**Advice we synthesized ourselves is tagged `[Our analysis]`.** For example, how-to tables, rules of thumb, and examples that this handbook has drawn together from several sources, where no single source says it in those words. This tag appears once per paragraph (or per subsection or table), not on every sentence; but if a paragraph also contains a fact, such as a number or a finding from a study, that sentence still gets its own footnote. Treat tagged content as reasonable advice, but know that it is not a source's own words; where it conflicts with your advisor's view, go with the specific situation of your own group.

Two more things you may come across:

- **"Where sources disagree"**: when sources disagree and the disagreement affects what you should do, the handbook sets out the options side by side with the reasons for each, and you choose based on your own situation.
- **"Our sources do not cover this"**: where the handbook could not find a reliable basis, it says so plainly instead of offering a claim from memory.

## Examples come from computer science for now

The handbook's structure is designed to work for every field: the four stages, the eight research strategies, and the relationship between claims and evidence are, in our view, applicable in every discipline `[Our analysis]`. But for now the examples and concrete steps come from computer science, and the opening of each chapter in the Parts and each strategy page says so.

Every chapter in the Parts and most strategy pages keep coming back to one running example: "**large language models and smart contract security**". You will see how the same research area moves forward at each stage: how to find problems in it, how to write the sentence you want to prove, how to pick a research strategy, and how to design the evidence. These examples are there to illustrate the steps; they are not the full story of a real project.

If you are not in computer science, most of the steps in the Parts still apply; you just need to swap in examples from your own field `[Our analysis]`. For specific methods and field conventions, go by what [Fields](../07_fields/README.md) says about your field. Examples and practices from other fields will be added over time.

**Write it down:** Replace "large language models and smart contract security" with your own research area, in one sentence. As you read the Parts, each time you meet an example, try to redo it with your own research area.
