# Key concepts

This handbook keeps coming back to a handful of words. In everyday speech people use them loosely and interchangeably, but when you do research, keeping them apart saves you a lot of wasted effort. This page explains them once, and the whole handbook uses them in exactly this sense.

All the examples come from one research area: "large language models and smart contract security".

---

## A chain: from research area to evidence

A research project usually comes into focus step by step along this chain:

```
research area → problem → research question → claim → evidence
```

Once the claim is clear, the contribution you need to deliver, the research strategy you should follow, and the methods you should use become clear along with it.

This is not a straight line you walk once. Later links often send you back to earlier ones: if you find you can't get the evidence, you change the problem; if you find you can only support a very weak claim, you go back and come at it from a different angle. For how to move back and forth along this chain, see [Converging on a project](../01_from-interest-to-project/03_converge.md).

## Research area

**A field you are interested in, or one your advisor has handed you.** For example, "using large language models for smart contract security".

You can't start work on a research area directly. It doesn't tell you what to solve or when you are done. The most common situation for a newcomer is to have a research area and nothing more.

## Problem

**Something in that field that is wrong, not good enough, or not yet known.** For example:

- Existing vulnerability detection tools raise too many false alarms, so developers don't want to use them;
- Everyone says large language models are very accurate at detecting vulnerabilities, but nobody has checked whether the models have already "seen" the benchmark data.

A problem answers "why is this worth doing?" If you have a research area but can't name a problem, your research has nothing to push against.

## Research question

**A problem narrowed into a question that can be answered.** For example: "How much of the reported performance of large-language-model vulnerability detection comes from data leakage from the benchmarks?"

A good research question has clear boundaries: what you study, under what conditions, and how complete an answer you are after. A question like "Can large language models be used for smart contract security?" is too broad; the only possible answer is "in some cases, yes", which answers nothing.

## Claim

**The sentence you want readers to believe after reading your paper.** It is the answer to your research question and what the whole paper sets out to show. For example: "About half of the accuracy in existing evaluation results comes from data leakage."

State your claim in terms of **results**, not **activities**[^shaw2003writing-e18]. "We studied X" describes what you did; "X achieves Z under condition Y" describes what you found. With the first, readers don't know what they are supposed to believe; only the second is a claim.

A paper usually has one central claim plus a few supporting claims.

## Contribution

**What new thing the study adds to the field.** It might be a new method, a new dataset, a new finding, a new benchmark, a proof...

Contribution and claim are closely related but not the same: the contribution is **what you deliver**; the claim is **what you say about it**. For example, the contribution is "a new benchmark with leaked samples removed", and the claim is "on the new benchmark, the accuracy of existing tools drops by half".

## Evidence

**What it takes to convince a skeptical reader of your claim.** It might be experimental results, measurement data, interview material, a proof, a systematic analysis of the literature...

The questions to ask are: why should the reader believe you? What standard should your claim be judged by? What concrete evidence shows that your result satisfies your claim[^shaw2003writing-e6]?

The claim determines the evidence, not the other way round. First decide "what do I want to show", then decide "what do I need to produce".

## Research strategy

**The main logic by which the project produces new knowledge.** The same research area can be approached with different strategies, and different strategies mean completely different work:

| Strategy | Knowledge it produces | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Description | What a phenomenon is like | "40% of contracts generated by large language models contain reentrancy vulnerabilities" |
| Measurement | How to measure something accurately | "Existing benchmarks suffer from data leakage; the new benchmark is more reliable" |
| Comparative evaluation | Which existing approach is better | "On real-world contracts, tool A has a higher detection rate than tool B" |
| Causal explanation | Why something happens | "Providing vulnerability-pattern hints in the prompt raises the detection rate" |
| Building and improving | Something better | "The new method finds 30% more vulnerabilities" |
| Synthesis | An overall picture of existing knowledge | "Research in this area falls into four groups, with three gaps" |
| Proof | Conclusions that hold by formal necessity | "Under condition C, the algorithm always terminates" |
| Interpretation and argument | A new understanding of texts, institutions, or ideas | Mostly in the humanities and social sciences |

A project often makes several kinds of contribution at once, for example a new method and a released dataset. In that case, the main strategy is the one your **central claim** belongs to; the others are secondary.

Why tell the strategies apart: the problem, the result, and the way you validate it have to match[^shaw2002makes-e9], and there are no universal quality criteria: each study has to be judged against the criteria that suit it[^ralph2020empirical-e24]. Holding a Description study to the standards of Building and improving (for instance, demanding that it compare against a baseline) is applying the wrong standard.

## Research methods

**The specific ways you collect and analyze evidence.** For example, controlled experiments, questionnaire surveys, interviews, mining software repositories, benchmarking, and formal proof.

Strategy and method are two separate layers. The strategy says "what knowledge to produce"; the method says "how to get the evidence". One method can serve different strategies: a controlled experiment can compare two tools (Comparative evaluation), or test whether some factor causes some outcome (Causal explanation).

## Field conventions

**The customs and requirements specific to a research community.** For example, computer science publishes mainly in conferences, and many conferences ask authors to submit reproducible research artifacts; psychology studies have to go through ethics review and are often expected to be preregistered.

These conventions don't change the basic logic of research, but they decide "what evidence will be accepted in this community".

## One example: the same research area, four different studies

The research area is always "large language models and smart contract security". Change the central claim, and the study becomes a completely different one:

| Central claim | Strategy | What you have to do |
|---|---|---|
| "The new method finds 30% more vulnerabilities than existing tools, with fewer false positives" | Building and improving | Implement the method, choose benchmarks and baselines, run comparisons and ablation studies |
| "40% of contracts generated by large language models contain reentrancy vulnerabilities" | Description | Collect a large number of generated contracts and systematically analyze how the vulnerabilities are distributed |
| "Providing vulnerability-pattern hints in the prompt raises the detection rate" | Causal explanation | Change only the way the prompt is given, keep everything else the same, and run a controlled experiment |
| "Existing benchmarks have serious leakage, so results are inflated" | Measurement | Show that the leakage exists, and show how the conclusions change once it is corrected |

So when you are handed a research area, the first question to settle is not "which method should I use" but "**which sentence do I ultimately want to prove**".
