# Philosophy, ethics, and religion: field overview

This page explains how research in philosophy actually works: the main output is argument; how to read and write a philosophy paper; how to handle objections; where articles and books fit; anonymous review; and how to find out a journal's review times and acceptance rate. It also briefly introduces experimental philosophy, which uses empirical methods to do philosophy, and the difference between religious studies and theology. Practices shared by every field (choosing a topic, reading the literature, writing, peer review, research ethics) are covered in the Parts listed in [The research journey at a glance](../../00_start-here/research-journey.md) and in [Throughout the project](../../08_throughout/README.md), and are not repeated here. For which research strategies philosophy uses and what evidence each one needs, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence); for how-to pages, see [Research methods common in this field](#research-methods-common-in-this-field).

**About our sources**: our sources come mainly from the US: three documents from the American Philosophical Association (1996, 2016, and 2022), a 2019 teaching guide from the American Academy of Religion, writing and reading guides for undergraduates by a philosophy teacher at New York University, and several entries from the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (a free online encyclopedia of philosophy). Note that the American Philosophical Association is also abbreviated APA, but it is not the American Psychological Association that appears on the [Psychology page](../psychology/README.md). When the page describes what a society or a journal does, it means that society or journal, not a worldwide rule; in other countries and institutions, follow the local rules `[Our analysis]`. We found very few sources on research methods in religious studies and theology, so the page covers only the basic difference between the two.

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- say what a philosophy paper has to do: give an argument for a small, clear claim, and respond to the strongest objections;
- read philosophy by first finding the conclusion, then taking the argument apart, then evaluating it;
- know how philosophy is published (mainly articles, with anonymous review) and what to find out before you submit;
- know which research strategies philosophy commonly uses, what evidence each relies on, and how it is judged;
- know what experimental philosophy is and where to learn methods for its empirical side;
- tell religious studies (which takes religion as its object of study) apart from theology (which works from inside a faith tradition).

## How research works in this field

### The main output is argument, not new data

The American Philosophical Association's 1996 *Statement on Research* (which describes the US situation at the time) puts it this way: in some disciplines, research means discovering new facts, gathering new data, and testing hypotheses by experiment, and some philosophical research is of this kind too[^apaphil1996research-e2]. But philosophical research often means refining analyses, proposing or criticizing interpretations, and exploring new ways of thinking: in short, advancing understanding[^apaphil1996research-e1]. Philosophy papers are rarely reports of empirical research[^apaphil1996research-e12]; what counts most is the quality of the reasoning and the deeper understanding it brings[^apaphil1996research-e9].

Philosophy varies a great deal internally. The same statement says some areas resemble mathematics and some of the sciences, while others resemble the study of language, literature, and the arts[^apaphil1996research-e5]. Logic, for example, is close to mathematics, and the history of philosophy is close to intellectual history and textual study `[Our analysis]`.

Another feature is that **disagreement is normal**. The same statement says disagreement and criticism are hallmarks of philosophical life, and it is hard to find two philosophers in the same area who agree completely[^apaphil1996research-e3]; the best philosophical work often provokes controversy rather than settling it[^apaphil1996research-e4]. So in philosophy, moving a debate forward is itself a contribution; you do not have to deliver a final answer `[Our analysis]`.

### Common tools of thought: cases, thought experiments, reflective equilibrium

- **Thought experiments** are essentially devices of the imagination[^brown2023thought-e1]. Few people object to using them to illustrate complicated situations; using imagined scenarios to investigate reality is controversial[^brown2023thought-e2].
- **The method of cases**: imagine a concrete situation (for example, whether someone "knows" something), see how people judge it, and use those judgments to test a definition or theory `[Our analysis]`. It is often seen as a central part of analytic philosophy; the vignette questionnaires of experimental philosophy can be seen as the systematic counterpart for studying such judgments (see below)[^sytsma2026experimental-e18].
- **Reflective equilibrium**: in light of the relevant arguments and theories, you adjust general principles and judgments about particular cases against each other until they fit together[^knight2023reflective-e1][^knight2023reflective-e2]. Knight, in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, cites other scholars who regard it as the dominant method in moral and political philosophy[^knight2023reflective-e3]; any philosopher who proposes principles using arguments and examples is very likely using it[^knight2023reflective-e4].

### What a philosophy paper has to do

What follows draws mainly on the writing guidelines of Jim Pryor (a philosophy teacher at New York University). These are one teacher's personal advice on undergraduate course papers, not a society's standard; but they cover the basic skills of philosophical writing, which apply to research papers too. The differences are noted at the end of this section `[Our analysis]`.

1. **Argue for a claim.** A philosophy paper is a reasoned defense of a claim[^pryor2012writing-e1]; reporting your own opinions, or restating what philosophers have said, is not enough[^pryor2012writing-e2].
2. **The usual moves.** You typically set out a thesis or argument and then do one or two things with it[^pryor2012writing-e3], for example criticize the argument, or show that certain arguments for the thesis fail[^pryor2012writing-e4].
3. **Make one small point, and make it well.** A good philosophy paper is modest: it makes one small point, but makes it clearly and with good reasons[^pryor2012writing-e5]. Trying to do too much usually produces a paper that is hard to follow and full of claims that are not properly defended[^pryor2012writing-e6].
4. **Outline first.** Pryor thinks the outline is at least 80% of the work of writing a good philosophy paper[^pryor2012writing-e7].
5. **Keep the prose simple and the structure obvious.** Don't aim for literary style; keep sentences and paragraphs short[^pryor2012writing-e8]. Use words like "so" and "therefore" correctly: writing "P, so Q" claims that P is a good reason to accept Q[^pryor2012writing-e9].
6. **Define your key terms and use them consistently.** Even if a central term is an everyday word, say how you understand it[^pryor2012writing-e10]; call the same thing by the same name throughout[^pryor2012writing-e11].
7. **Raise objections yourself and answer them.** It is better to raise them yourself than to hope the reader won't think of them[^pryor2012writing-e16]; focus on the strongest and most pressing ones[^pryor2012writing-e17].
8. **You don't need a definitive conclusion.** A paper does not have to offer a clear-cut solution[^pryor2012writing-e18]; if you can't fix your argument, you can change your thesis to one you can defend[^pryor2012writing-e19].
9. **Write many drafts.** At least three or four[^pryor2012writing-e20].

How research papers differ: Pryor says his course papers do not need secondary literature beyond the assigned readings[^pryor2012writing-e24]; a research paper, by contrast, has to say how your view relates to the existing literature. For how to do that, see the Part 4 chapter section [Related work](../../03_write-and-publish/01_story-and-paper.md#5-related-work-show-where-you-stand) `[Our analysis]`. For general writing practice (core message, structure, revision), see the Part 4 chapter [Telling the story and writing the paper](../../03_write-and-publish/01_story-and-paper.md).

### Reading: take the argument apart first

Pryor's reading guidelines for undergraduates have three stages, which you can treat as three steps:

1. **Skim to find the main conclusion.** Skim first and find the author's main conclusion[^pryor2006reading-e1], taking care not to mistake a supporting argument (an argument that defends one of the premises) for the main argument[^pryor2006reading-e2]. Also get exactly what the author is claiming: an article that rebuts "a certain objection to skepticism" concludes that the objection fails, not that skepticism is true[^pryor2006reading-e3].
2. **Read closely and map the structure of the argument.** Pay special attention to assumptions that are not argued for[^pryor2006reading-e4]; in your notes, outline the "parts" of the argument and draw arrows between them[^pryor2006reading-e5]. You will need to read an article more than once; after more than ten years in philosophy, Pryor still does[^pryor2006reading-e6].
3. **Evaluate.** Are there false premises? Unstated false assumptions? Does it equivocate or beg the question[^pryor2006reading-e7]? Philosophers often say things like: "Even if we grant premise such-and-such for the sake of argument, X's argument still fails, because…"[^pryor2006reading-e9]

For general reading practice (reading in several passes, taking notes, following citations), see the Part 1 chapter [Entering a field and exploring the literature](../../01_find-a-problem/01_enter-a-field.md).

### Handling objections and opponents: state the view accurately, then criticize

- **Restate accurately before you criticize.** Before criticizing a position, you have to explain what it says[^pryor2012writing-e15]. Students often attack a view that sounds similar but is actually different[^pryor2012writing-e12].
- **Criticize the most reasonable reading.** Don't treat the philosopher you are discussing as stupid[^pryor2012writing-e13]; work out the reasonable position they might hold and argue against that[^pryor2012writing-e14]. This is often called the principle of charity `[Our analysis]`.
- **Respect people who hold different views.** The American Philosophical Association's 2016 Code of Conduct lists respecting others' philosophical views and traditions, and not disparaging those whose positions differ from your own, among philosophers' professional responsibilities[^apaphil2016conduct-e1].
- **You judge the argument, not the conclusion.** Pryor says that when he grades student papers, it doesn't matter whether he agrees with the conclusion[^pryor2012writing-e23].

### Publishing: mainly articles, but books too

According to the American Philosophical Association's 1996 statement (which reflects the US situation at the time): some philosophers write both articles and books, a few write only books, and more write only articles[^apaphil1996research-e8]. It is neither expected nor typical for dissertations to be published as books; it is more common to publish parts of them as articles[^apaphil1996research-e10]. Writing a solid paper good enough for a good journal takes a great deal of time at any career stage[^apaphil1996research-e11]. The statement also says that, apart from a few areas connected to science and public policy, philosophy has no research funding of the kind the sciences have[^apaphil1996research-e7]. This means much philosophical research is not organized around grant-funded projects and labs `[Our analysis]`. Our sources do not cover how things stand today, or in which areas monographs matter more; follow your advisor and your institution's requirements.

Posting papers before submission: the American Philosophical Association's 2022 statement on journals says that posting papers online before submission (for example, in a preprint archive) is increasingly common among philosophers; philosophy of science has PhilSci Archive at the University of Pittsburgh[^apaphil2022journal-e12]. It recommends that journal editors generally be willing to consider papers already posted in a preprint archive[^apaphil2022journal-e13]. Check your target journal's policy.

### Authorship: only one small-sample study

On authorship conventions in philosophy, we found only one study, and it does not cover the whole discipline: a poster by Chang and Yeh at the 2023 iConference (we read only the public abstract). They analyzed 3,773 articles by the 100 authors who published most in philosophy journals from 2011 to 2020, and 56.5% were single-authored[^chang2023prolific-e1]. Of these 100 people, 28 worked mainly in psychology, and they were more inclined to co-author than the other 72 philosophy authors[^chang2023prolific-e2]. The sample is only "the 100 most prolific", so it does not represent philosophers in general `[Our analysis]`.

Author order: a bibliometric study by Fernandes and Cortez in *Scientometrics* (2020; we read the submitted manuscript on the authors' homepage, with data taken from the Scopus database in May 2020) compared author ordering across 27 broad fields. They did not treat philosophy separately, but the samples for the "Arts and Humanities" field were all philosophy journals: one set was papers from a single journal, *Noûs*[^fernandes2020alphabetic-e1], and the other was papers from nine philosophy journals, including *Noûs*, *Mind*, and *Ethics*, from 2018 to 2020[^fernandes2020alphabetic-e3]. The field showed only a "moderate" tendency toward alphabetical order, not as strong as in economics or mathematics[^fernandes2020alphabetic-e2]; the authors also caution that samples drawn from a few journals may not represent the whole field[^fernandes2020alphabetic-e4]. So, at least in these journals, co-authored philosophy papers are not necessarily in alphabetical order; how your team orders names is up to your advisor and co-authors `[Our analysis]`. Our sources do not cover what author order signals about contribution in philosophy.

### Review: anonymous, and you need to find out the timelines and acceptance rates

**Anonymous review.** The American Philosophical Association's 2022 statement strongly recommends that journals use "triple-anonymous" review: neither the editor nor the referees know who the author is, and the author does not know who the referees are[^apaphil2022journal-e1]; editors should also have read a manuscript anonymously before desk-rejecting it[^apaphil2022journal-e2]. The statement notes that many philosophers post papers online before submitting, so referees can often find out the author; it therefore asks referees not to search[^apaphil2022journal-e8], and to tell the editor if they do know who the author is[^apaphil2022journal-e9]. It also recommends that journals not ask authors to suggest referees[^apaphil2022journal-e14]. These are one US society's recommendations; journals differ, so check your target journal.

**How long review takes and how high the acceptance rate is.** We found only two kinds of data, and you need to read the caveats on both:

- **Questionnaires self-reported by authors.** The American Philosophical Association's "Journal Surveys" project lets authors voluntarily report their review experiences[^andrews2024journal-e1]. On the association's blog, Andrews (2024) analyzed 1,568 survey responses from 2019 to 2023, spread over 97 journals[^andrews2024journal-e3][^andrews2024journal-e4]. In these responses, the first decision took 3.22 months on average, and 14.9% reported waiting more than 6 months[^andrews2024journal-e5]; 67% reported receiving at least one referee report[^andrews2024journal-e6]. Andrews also cautions that there is no way to know how accurate these data are, since most journals do not publish statistics to check them against, and the acceptance rates in the surveys are generally thought to be too high[^andrews2024journal-e2].
- **Figures published by journals themselves.** We checked only one: the *Journal of the History of Philosophy*. In 2025–2026 it received 356 submissions and accepted 15, an acceptance rate of 4.2%[^jhp2025statistics-e1]; for manuscripts sent out for review, the average time from submission to final decision was 76 days[^jhp2025statistics-e2], and from acceptance to publication 1.31 years on average[^jhp2025statistics-e3]. For other journals, you need to look up the figures yourself.

The American Philosophical Association says that four months after submission you can comfortably ask about the status, unless the journal has said it will take longer[^apaphil2022journal-e7]. Submitting a manuscript implies it is not under consideration anywhere else at the same time[^apaphil2022journal-e6]. For general practice in peer review and responding to reviews, see the Part 4 chapter [Choosing a venue and submitting](../../03_write-and-publish/02_venue-and-submission.md).

### Experimental philosophy: doing philosophy with empirical methods

Experimental philosophy (often written x-phi) uses empirical methods for philosophical ends[^sytsma2026experimental-e1]. What follows draws on the new 2026 entry in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* by Sytsma, Reuter, and Willemsen.

- **What it studies.** Most prominently, people's "intuitions" about philosophical concepts, usually by surveying the judgments of ordinary people[^sytsma2026experimental-e2].
- **What methods it uses.** The most common are questionnaires and corpus linguistics (statistical analysis of word use across large bodies of real text)[^sytsma2026experimental-e4]. The typical questionnaire uses "vignettes": a philosophical thought experiment written up as a short story, on which participants give a judgment[^sytsma2026experimental-e3].
- **The process.** Ideally you start from an argument that engages with the existing philosophical literature[^sytsma2026experimental-e5]; an empirical premise in that argument is the point of entry into empirical research, after which the process is the same as in experimental psychology[^sytsma2026experimental-e6]; at the end you connect the results back to the philosophical argument and state the study's limitations[^sytsma2026experimental-e7].
- **How it differs from informally "asking around".** Systematic methods exist to avoid drawing conclusions from a few or biased responses[^sytsma2026experimental-e10]. Even if you hand out a questionnaire in class, for example, students should answer independently rather than by a show of hands[^sytsma2026experimental-e11].
- **Standards are rising.** The entry says sample sizes have grown and statistical reporting has improved, and some early studies would not meet today's standards[^sytsma2026experimental-e8]. It also describes open science practices: preregistering the study design, hypotheses, and predictions, and posting raw data and analysis code online; these practices came partly out of psychology's replication crisis[^sytsma2026experimental-e9].
- **Controversy.** Some critics dismiss it as "amateur psychology"[^sytsma2026experimental-e12]; the entry's authors argue that experimental philosophers' own checks of statistical reporting and replicability in the field turned out better than critics claim[^sytsma2026experimental-e13]. On method, the "positive program" aims to put the use of intuitions as evidence on a firmer empirical footing[^sytsma2026experimental-e14], while the "negative program" aims to undermine that practice, often targeting the assumptions behind the method of cases[^sytsma2026experimental-e15]. Another challenge rests on a body of empirical results: some case judgments vary with factors that are philosophically irrelevant[^sytsma2026experimental-e16].

For the empirical side, the methodological requirements are the same as in psychology (preregistration, statistical power, complete reporting, and so on); see the [Psychology page](../psychology/README.md#what-changed-afterward). For ethics approval when participants are involved, see [When research involves people, protect them first](../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#4-when-research-involves-people-or-could-harm-others-protect-people-first) in Throughout the project `[Our analysis]`.

### Religious studies and theology: two different starting points

The religious studies part below draws on the American Academy of Religion's 2019 religious literacy guidelines. They were written for US college teaching and describe what college graduates should understand about religion; they are not a standard for research methods.

- **Religious studies** takes religion as its object of study. The guidelines say the academic study of religion includes many methods, such as literary criticism, anthropology, sociology, and history, and that the sociology, anthropology, and psychology of religion are consistent with it[^aar2019literacy-e2]. A shared premise of religious studies is that expressing a religious belief and studying religious expressions are different things[^aar2019literacy-e3]. The guidelines list "being able to distinguish confessional or prescriptive statements made by religions from descriptive or analytical statements" as one of the abilities college graduates should have[^aar2019literacy-e7].
- **Faith-based approaches**: the guidelines say faith-based religious teaching promotes a particular belief or practice[^aar2019literacy-e5], and may or may not use the tools of critical analysis[^aar2019literacy-e6]. The guidelines list it separately from the religious studies approach.
- **Theology and philosophy**: Wood's entry in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* discusses only Christian theology. He says both interpret authoritative texts, make arguments, and marshal evidence[^wood2021philosophy-e1]; the contrast "theology relies on faith, philosophy on reason" has something to it, but the line is less sharp than people imagine[^wood2021philosophy-e2]. On the "cooperation" model the entry describes (one view of how the two relate), theology presupposes that divine revelation is true and philosophy does not[^wood2021philosophy-e3]; theologians may take scripture and authoritative church tradition as evidence, while philosophers may use only premises and evidence available in principle to any rational inquirer[^wood2021philosophy-e4].

What this means for a newcomer: when you write, be clear about where you stand, whether you are describing and analyzing a tradition or arguing from within it, and make that visible to your readers `[Our analysis]`. Which methods to use in religious studies depends on the discipline you borrow from: for textual and historical research, see the [History and archaeology page](../history-archaeology/README.md); for interviews and fieldwork, see [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md) `[Our analysis]`. The guidelines also say that experiential approaches, such as visiting religious people and sites, can be compatible with the religious studies approach if organized as ethnographic study[^aar2019literacy-e8].

## Norms in this field

- **Submitting to and reviewing for journals** (the American Philosophical Association's 2022 recommendations): before you submit, check the journal's website for its acceptance rate, average review time, and average time from acceptance to publication[^apaphil2022journal-e4], and prepare the manuscript for anonymous review[^apaphil2022journal-e5]. When you referee, reply to an invitation within a week at most[^apaphil2022journal-e11], don't search for the author's identity[^apaphil2022journal-e8], and don't fuss over minor stylistic flaws, since the author may not be a native English speaker[^apaphil2022journal-e15]. For general practice, see [Being a reviewer](../../08_throughout/being_a_reviewer.md).
- **Professional conduct** (the American Philosophical Association's 2016 Code of Conduct): respect different philosophical views and traditions[^apaphil2016conduct-e1], act with integrity in all professional dealings[^apaphil2016conduct-e2], and treat academic freedom as a core value[^apaphil2016conduct-e3].
- **Citation**: the American Philosophical Association's 1996 statement says philosophers cite contemporary literature selectively, and a citation is as often critical as approving[^apaphil1996research-e6]. So being cited does not mean being endorsed; when you cite someone's view, make clear whether you agree with it or are arguing against it `[Our analysis]`. Follow your target journal's citation style; our sources do not cover a single standard format.

## Common strategies and standards of evidence

A **research strategy** is the main logic by which a project produces new knowledge, decided by your central claim; see [Key concepts](../../00_start-here/key-concepts.md#research-strategy) and [Research strategies](../../05_research-strategies/README.md). The table lists only the strategies our sources support. The grouping and the "What it looks like in philosophy" column are our own summary; the standards come mostly from US sources, one teacher's handouts, and *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entries, and represent only the sources in the footnotes `[Our analysis]`.

| Strategy | What it looks like in philosophy | Common evidence and how it is judged | Methods |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Interpretation and argument](../../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md) (the main strategy) | A reasoned defense of a claim[^pryor2012writing-e1]. Arguments are mostly deductive, but the premises are rarely beyond all doubt, so they are usually not conclusive[^pryor2025terms-e12] | The evidence is contestable premises, judgments about cases, and conceptual distinctions; the quality of reasoning counts most[^apaphil1996research-e9]; you must respond to the strongest and most pressing objections[^pryor2012writing-e17]; ethics and political philosophy often use reflective equilibrium to test principles[^knight2023reflective-e3] | [Philosophical argument](practices/philosophical_argument.md) |
| [Interpretation and argument](../../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md) (history of philosophy and textual interpretation) | Explaining a philosopher's views and arguments | Quote passages from the text to support your interpretation; say so explicitly when you discuss views for which the text gives no direct evidence[^pryor2012writing-e25] | [Close reading and literary argument](../languages-literature/practices/close_reading.md) (literature, for reference) |
| [Proof](../../05_research-strategies/prove.md) | Areas such as logic are close to mathematics[^apaphil1996research-e5]; mathematical proof is the paradigm of deductive argument[^pryor2025terms-e3]; formal epistemology uses tools such as probability theory and modal logic to study knowledge and reasoning[^weisberg2021formal-e1] | The proof must first be correct; formal results must also connect back to philosophical questions; details below the table | [Proof: what evidence is enough](../../05_research-strategies/prove.md#2-what-evidence-is-enough), [Theoretical research](../computer-science/practices/theoretical_research.md) (computer science, for reference) |
| [Description](../../05_research-strategies/observe_describe.md), [Causal explanation](../../05_research-strategies/explain_causality.md) (experimental philosophy) | Studying people's "intuitions" about philosophical concepts, usually by surveying ordinary people's judgments[^sytsma2026experimental-e2]; also testing whether some case judgments vary with philosophically irrelevant factors[^sytsma2026experimental-e16] | Use systematic methods; don't draw conclusions from a few or biased responses[^sytsma2026experimental-e10]; start from a philosophical argument, then connect the results back to it and state the limitations[^sytsma2026experimental-e5][^sytsma2026experimental-e7]; requirements for sample size and statistical reporting are rising[^sytsma2026experimental-e8] | [Questionnaire surveys](../../06_methods/questionnaire_survey.md), [Controlled experiments](../../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md), [Data science](../../06_methods/data_science.md) (corpus studies) |

Notes on the Proof row. This part draws on the American Mathematical Society's ethical guidelines (revised 2022), Weisberg's entry on formal epistemology in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (revised 2021), and the submission requirements on three logic journals' websites. The journal websites state only general requirements; our sources do not cover which specific criteria referees apply, so follow your target journal's instructions.

- **It must be new, and the result must be substantial.** The *Journal of Symbolic Logic* of the Association for Symbolic Logic requires submissions to be innovative, not minor variations on existing work, and of interest to a broad readership[^asljslabout-e1]. The logic journal *Studia Logica* says in its aims and scope that its key criterion for accepting papers is methodological: a paper must contain significant, original results about formal systems and their properties[^studialogicaaims-e1].
- **Explain why it matters.** The same society's *Bulletin of Symbolic Logic* requires that "communications" (submissions reporting important new results or ideas) give, beyond the results themselves, enough history, background, and explanation for a broad readership to see their significance[^aslbslabout-e1].
- **The proof must first be correct.** The American Mathematical Society's guidelines ask editors of mathematics journals to accept a paper only when reasonably confident it is correct[^ams2022ethical-e9]; formal papers in philosophy can take this as a reference point `[Our analysis]`.
- **Formal results must connect back to philosophical questions.** *Studia Logica* aims to strike a balance between mathematical technique and philosophical relevance[^studialogicaaims-e2]. Weisberg uses the problem of induction as an example: formalization can state precisely how induction proceeds, but it relies on assumptions such as the probability axioms and cannot by itself solve the kind of problem Hume raised; its use is to isolate and clarify those assumptions, and then argue for them[^weisberg2021formal-e2].
- **Watch out for idealization.** Models often make idealizing assumptions, for example that people believe everything their beliefs entail[^weisberg2021formal-e4]. Several authors the entry discusses argue that when a model is simple, idealized, and its results are not robust, you should doubt whether it applies to reality[^weisberg2021formal-e3].

How to use this table `[Our analysis]`:

- **Most philosophy papers fall under Interpretation and argument.** For what kind of knowledge this produces and what evidence is enough, see the [Interpretation and argument](../../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md) strategy page; for the concrete steps of a philosophy paper, from choosing a topic and constructing an argument to responding to objections, see [Philosophical argument](practices/philosophical_argument.md).
- **In formal areas, you have to pass on both fronts.** Check the proof itself against the standards of the [Proof](../../05_research-strategies/prove.md#2-what-evidence-is-enough) strategy; for the formal result, also explain why its assumptions hold up philosophically and whether the conclusion carries back to the original question.
- **Choose the strategy by the question.** If the question can be answered by collecting data ("how do people actually judge this?"), take an empirical strategy; if the question is "does this claim hold up on its merits?", it is Interpretation and argument. In religious studies, choose strategies and methods by the discipline you borrow from (textual study, history, sociology, anthropology)[^aar2019literacy-e2].

## Advice for newcomers

1. **Make one small point per paper, and write a detailed outline first.** Before you write, state your thesis and main reasons in one sentence[^pryor2012writing-e5]; the outline is most of the work of writing a good philosophy paper[^pryor2012writing-e7].
2. **Map the structure of arguments when you read.** What is the conclusion, what are the premises, and which premises are not argued for[^pryor2006reading-e4][^pryor2006reading-e5]? If you don't understand it, read it again; that is normal[^pryor2006reading-e6].
3. **Restate the other side's view accurately first, then find the strongest objections.** Criticize the most reasonable reading[^pryor2012writing-e14]; deal only with the strongest objections, and if you can't fix your argument, change your thesis[^pryor2012writing-e17][^pryor2012writing-e19].
4. **Check the journal's requirements before you submit, and plan for slowness.** Look up the acceptance rate, review times, and similar information[^apaphil2022journal-e4]. Reference figures: in self-reported (and unverifiable) responses to the American Philosophical Association's Journal Surveys covering 97 journals from 2019 to 2023, the first decision took 3.22 months on average[^andrews2024journal-e5]; at the *Journal of the History of Philosophy*, the average time from acceptance to publication is 1.31 years[^jhp2025statistics-e3]. Build these into your timeline `[Our analysis]`.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Only stating your own opinion, or only restating what philosophers have said | A philosophy paper has to give an argument[^pryor2012writing-e2] |
| Attacking a straw man: a view that sounds similar but is actually different | Pryor says students often waste time on this[^pryor2012writing-e12]; restate the view accurately before you criticize it[^pryor2012writing-e15] |
| Avoiding objections that hurt your case | It is better to raise them yourself than to hope the reader won't think of them[^pryor2012writing-e16] |
| Sprinkling "so" and "therefore" around to make the argument look rigorous | "P, so Q" claims that P is a good reason for Q, and that claim had better be true[^pryor2012writing-e9] |
| Not defining key terms, or switching between different words for the same concept | Readers can't tell whether you mean the same thing `[Our analysis]`; Pryor asks you to say how you understand your central terms and to call the same thing by the same name throughout[^pryor2012writing-e10][^pryor2012writing-e11] |
| Opening with "Since the dawn of time, humans have…" or a dictionary definition | You don't need a warm-up; the dictionary is not a good philosophical authority[^pryor2012writing-e21][^pryor2012writing-e22] |
| Mistaking a supporting argument for the main argument when reading, or misreading the author's conclusion | You will misunderstand the whole article[^pryor2006reading-e2][^pryor2006reading-e3] |
| Treating experimental philosophy as "sending out a questionnaire" | Systematic methods exist precisely to avoid drawing conclusions from biased responses[^sytsma2026experimental-e10]; some early studies do not meet today's standards for sample size and statistical reporting[^sytsma2026experimental-e8] |
| Mixing description and confession in the study of religion | The American Academy of Religion's teaching guidelines list distinguishing confessional statements from descriptive and analytical ones as an ability college graduates should have[^aar2019literacy-e7] |

## Further reading

- **Jim Pryor, *Guidelines on Writing a Philosophy Paper* / *Guidelines on Reading Philosophy*** (jimpryor.net): free writing and reading guides for undergraduates by a US philosophy teacher. Read them before you write your first philosophy paper.
- **American Philosophical Association, *Statement on Best Practices in Journal Publishing*** (2022): one US society's recommendations for journals, authors, and referees. Read it before you first submit or referee.
- **American Philosophical Association, *Statement on Research*** (1996): what philosophical research is and how it differs from other disciplines, as things stood in the US at the time. Useful when you explain philosophical research to people in other fields.
- ***Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*: "Experimental Philosophy"** (new 2026 entry), **"Reflective Equilibrium"**, and **"Thought Experiments"**: the methods and controversies of experimental philosophy, and two common philosophical methods.
- **American Academy of Religion, *AAR Religious Literacy Guidelines*** (2019; US college teaching guidelines, not a research methods standard) and ***Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*: "Philosophy and Christian Theology"** (Christianity only): the differences among religious studies, theology, and philosophy.
