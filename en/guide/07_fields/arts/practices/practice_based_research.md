# Practice-based research

**Scope.** This is a practice from the arts: research in which making creative work is the method, and the work is the basis of the contribution to knowledge. Everything all research shares (framing a research question, reading the literature, planning, and writing and publishing) is covered in the chapters of Part 1, Part 2, and Writing and publishing. The general logic of research that produces something new is on the [Building and improving](../../../05_research-strategies/build_improve.md) strategy page. Practice-based research usually does not compare against a baseline, though, and its standards of judgment are very different from those used in computer science, chemistry, and similar fields described there `[Our analysis]`.

**Strategy it usually serves**: [Building and improving](../../../05_research-strategies/build_improve.md) (as listed in that page's section on common research methods).

This page explains how to run a **practice-based research project**, such as a PhD built around creative work: the work is the basis of the contribution to knowledge, accompanied by documentation of the process and a written text. The [arts field overview](../README.md#kind-two-research-that-uses-practice-as-its-method) already covers the differences between the names (practice-based, practice-led, artistic research), the UK Research Excellence Framework (REF) definition and its 300-word statement, the debate around the *Vienna Declaration* and the *Frascati Manual*, and US fair-use guidelines. This page does not repeat them; it covers only **how to actually do it**.

**Where this page's sources come from.** Two are personal views of their authors, not rules of any institution: a guide Linda Candy wrote in 2006 for a research center at the University of Technology Sydney in Australia, and a 2018 article by Candy and Edmonds drawing on more than 35 years of supervising PhDs in art, design, and digital media[^candy2018practice-e1]. The others are the 2021 report of the UK Practice Research Advisory Group (PRAG-UK), based on consultation with 62 practice researchers, theorists, research support staff, and policymakers in England[^bulley2021practice-e8]; the *Characteristics Statement: Doctoral Degree* of the UK Quality Assurance Agency for Higher Education (QAA); the UK REF 2021 assessment documents; and the submission requirements and review form of the *Journal for Artistic Research* (JAR), which speak for that one journal only. So this page mainly reflects **practice in the UK, Australia, and this one journal**. For the form, length, and examination of your thesis, your own university's regulations take precedence `[Our analysis]`.

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- tell whether your project is practice-based research, and how it differs from action research and design-based research;
- frame a research question that your creative work can answer, and write a contextual review (one that places your work among what your peers are doing);
- design a make–look–revise cycle, and document the process from day one;
- say clearly what counts as your evidence, and write a text that connects the work to the contribution;
- know what examiners and reviewers look for, and avoid the common mistakes.

## 1. What this research is, and when to use it

**The one-line test** (Candy 2006's distinction): if the creative output is the basis of the contribution to knowledge, it is practice-based research; if the research mainly leads to new understanding about practice, it is practice-led research[^candy2006practice-e1]. A practice-led PhD can be written entirely in text, and it often falls within the broad area of action research[^candy2006practice-e3].

**What about design and architecture?** The *Vienna Declaration*, a policy document published jointly in 2020 by European organizations including the Association Européenne des Conservatoires (AEC, the European association of music schools), lists architecture and design among the disciplines in which artistic research is done[^aec2020vienna-e8]. In UK REF 2021, another main panel (Main Panel C) requires that outputs such as buildings, designs, or interventions come with a statement including photographs and diagrams, so that assessors can judge the research contribution without visiting the site[^ref2019panel-e13]. So, at least in these two documents, design and architectural outputs can count as research outputs `[Our analysis]`. But Candy and Edmonds argue that design research focuses on understanding design practice itself and how to improve it, rather than on creating and reflecting on new works[^candy2018practice-e25]; Candy 2006 also notes that design research is often done by specialist researchers rather than by the designers themselves[^candy2006practice-e34]. Check with your advisor whether the approach on this page applies directly to your design or architecture project `[Our analysis]`.

**How it differs from related approaches** (cells without footnotes are our own summary `[Our analysis]`; for the education approach of designing and researching at the same time, see [Design-based research](../../education/practices/design_based_research.md)):

| | Practice-based research | Practice-led research | [Action research](../../../06_methods/action_research.md) (research type) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core output | Creative work plus text[^candy2018practice-e17] | New understanding of practice; can be all text[^candy2006practice-e3] | A study of an organizational change |
| Whose practice | Usually the researcher's own creative work | The researcher's own practice or someone else's | A change the researcher drives together with a partner organization |
| How examiners see it | They need access to the work (or its documentation)[^candy2018practice-e7] | By reading the text | By reading the text |

**When to use it.** When your question **can only be answered by making the work**. Candy and Edmonds give two examples:

- Tebby studied the use of various formal procedures and schemata in making art. Without making the works, the implications of those procedures could not be grasped[^candy2018practice-e19].
- A video of a rotating object in a geometry thesis is just an aid to explanation; but if the research is about how people perceive the work, providing the video may be essential. Then the work is not an illustration; it is itself the object of study[^candy2018practice-e6].

If the work is only an illustration, and deleting it would not affect the argument, you are probably doing ordinary text-based research `[Our analysis]`. Watch out when searching the literature: in clinical fields such as psychotherapy, public health, and family medicine, "practice-based research" means research done in practice settings, which is not what it means in the arts[^bulley2021practice-e38].

## 2. How to do it

The steps below follow the order of a project, but in practice they overlap and loop back `[Our analysis]`. The PRAG-UK report notes that a shift in the focus of inquiry or in methods during the project is a common difficulty for practice research PhD students[^bulley2021practice-e16].

### Step 1: Frame a question that practice can answer

**How:** Candy 2006, paraphrasing the 2000 definition of research by the UK Arts and Humanities Research Board, says a research proposal should set out three things: the questions to be addressed and what understanding you hope to advance[^candy2006practice-e18]; the research context, that is, why the question matters, what others have done, and what your particular contribution will be[^candy2006practice-e19]; and the methods for answering the questions, and why they are the most appropriate[^candy2006practice-e20]. When you write the question, ask yourself: **once I have made this body of work, what will I know that I don't know now and nobody else knows either?** `[Our analysis]`

**Why:** Candy and Edmonds argue that in practice-based research, questions arise out of the process of practice, and the answers are used to inform and improve practice[^candy2018practice-e2]; but research and practice are different things and should not be confused[^candy2018practice-e4]. Candy 2006 notes that practitioners do "research" every day, mostly for their own immediate goals, not to add to shared knowledge[^candy2006practice-e4].

**Where people go wrong:**

- **The question is new only to you.** Candy and Edmonds say that knowledge that is new only to the practitioner is not part of any definition of practice-based PhD research[^candy2018practice-e15].
- **Staying inside the single case.** Research is usually set in a specific context, but to count as a contribution to knowledge it has to go beyond the individual case[^candy2018practice-e3].
- **Thinking the question must be fixed at the start.** The PRAG-UK report cites the artist-researcher Carole Gray: practitioners' questions often emerge gradually; the basic question may not change, but the final question is only settled when the project is complete[^bulley2021practice-e15]. Johnston's PhD aims were also discovered through practice[^candy2018practice-e22]. So start with a "working" question in your proposal, and record in your journal each time it changes and why `[Our analysis]`.

### Step 2: Write a contextual review to position the work

**How:** Both of Candy's sources say a practice-based PhD thesis must include a substantial contextualization of the creative work[^candy2006practice-e21], done as a form of "literature" review[^candy2018practice-e13]. Candy 2006 suggests this chapter answer: who is doing what? Who has done what? Who did or published it first? Sources include not only papers and books but also exhibition catalogs and the like[^candy2006practice-e24]. Go back to primary sources rather than relying on textbooks or other people's summaries; ideally this chapter offers your own new, structured view of the field[^candy2006practice-e25]. General methods for finding literature and taking notes are in [Entering a field and exploring the literature](../../../01_find-a-problem/01_enter-a-field.md).

**Why:** This part both explains the basis for your claim of originality and where the work stands, and gives the grounds for judging whether general scholarly requirements are met[^candy2006practice-e21]. An advisor to the REF arts and humanities panel (Main Panel D) said that if it is not clear where a work sits in relation to other work in the field, it is hard to say whether it is new[^bulley2021practice-e31].

**Where people go wrong:** Reviewing only theoretical literature and not **comparable works and practice**; "who is doing what" is a question about your peers' practice `[Our analysis]`. Also say which communities your outcomes matter to (artists, theorists, technologists, and so on)[^candy2006practice-e23].

### Step 3: Design a make–look–revise cycle

**How:** The PhD by Bell that Candy and Edmonds describe was a repeated cycle of making (writing code), looking (evaluating), and revising[^candy2018practice-e21]. Insights from making, reflecting, and evaluating can feed straight back into the work[^candy2018practice-e5]. When writing up your method, Candy suggests: first describe what you actually did (as if writing instructions for an assistant), then briefly say where the methods came from, and only then justify the parts that have not yet been justified[^candy2006practice-e27]; methods are usually chosen from known, tried, and tested methods[^candy2006practice-e26].

**Who does the looking?** Candy and Edmonds's cases show both approaches:

- **Mainly the researcher's own judgment.** Bell's evaluation was entirely subjective: he consulted others, but the core evaluator was the artist himself[^candy2018practice-e21].
- **Including other people's responses.** Johnston observed how performers played with the new forms and discussed it with them[^candy2018practice-e22]; Seevinck's work was meant to prompt "emergent" thinking in participating audiences, and to show that it did, she reported an evaluation of participants' responses[^candy2018practice-e23].

**How to choose** `[Our analysis]`: if your claims are about **the work and the making process**, relying mainly on your own judgment is fine, but write down your criteria (see Step 5). As soon as a claim involves **the audience or users** ("it makes the audience…"), you need to collect their responses as evidence. For interviews and questionnaires, see the Research types pages [Interview studies](../../../06_methods/interview_study.md) and [Questionnaire surveys](../../../06_methods/questionnaire_survey.md).

**Where people go wrong:**

- **Spending pages arguing that "practice can be a research method".** PRAG-UK respondents (journal editors, supervisors, assessors) said practice researchers often begin with long philosophical and ontological explanations and take a long time to get to the research itself[^bulley2021practice-e19]. The report considers this extra justification unnecessary unless it bears directly on describing the method[^bulley2021practice-e18].
- **Dismissing other methods in passing.** Candy warns: if you write "statistics are no use here", make sure you really understand statistics[^candy2006practice-e28].

### Step 4: Document the process from day one

**How:** Candy 2006's advice[^candy2006practice-e29][^candy2006practice-e30][^candy2006practice-e31]:

1. Keep an online diary, a blog, or a handwritten notebook, ideally one you carry with you, so you can note observations and events during making as they happen;
2. If you make audio recordings, you will probably need to transcribe them; build the transcription time into your plan;
3. Keep an **overview chart** so you can see at a glance what has been recorded and what you still plan to record;
4. Set aside time for reflection after events, and record your reactions and new ideas at agreed review points. **Don't reflect only when you happen to think of it; put it in your timetable.**

At a review point, you can ask yourself: what was proposed, discussed, decided, and carried out? What obstacles came up, and how were they resolved? Are the ideas feasible and interesting? Did the collaboration go smoothly, and were there disagreements with collaborators? Why did an approach succeed or fail, and what did you learn?[^candy2006practice-e32]

**What to record with.** The tools PRAG-UK respondents mentioned most were cameras, notebooks, file-sharing systems, audio recorders, post-production software, and code repositories[^bulley2021practice-e27]. Date every record and note which version of the work it relates to; never overwrite raw material `[Our analysis]`. For organizing material, see [Organizing your research materials](../../../08_throughout/organizing_materials.md#5-keep-three-kinds-of-notes-apart) in Throughout the project.

**Why:** Candy says the outcomes of practice must be accompanied by documentation of the research process[^candy2006practice-e5]. Performances and installations disappear when they end, and most readers can only know them through documentation (see the [field overview](../README.md#publishing-and-dissemination)). The PRAG-UK report quotes the scholar Biggs: practice does not become research because it is documented, but where documentation is possible, it is an efficient way to work[^bulley2021practice-e24]. Another artist-researcher reminds her PhD students to notice when to document something and start establishing ownership, especially when what they make may involve copyright[^bulley2021practice-e26].

**Where people go wrong:** Treating "documentation" and "output" as the same thing. The PRAG-UK report says you need to distinguish the complete research output from documentation within it that stands in for the practice[^bulley2021practice-e25]. When documentation is genuinely impossible (for example because of funding or permissions), you can describe the practice in writing[^bulley2021practice-e30].

### Step 5: Work out what counts as data and evidence

**How:** List the kinds of things your project will produce, and say which claim each kind supports `[Our analysis]`. The forms of knowledge Candy and Edmonds list include understanding of audience experience, strategies for designing engaging art systems, taxonomies, models of collaboration, and of course the works themselves[^candy2018practice-e16]. Some of this knowledge comes from empirical studies of audiences and art systems; some is more speculative and exploratory[^candy2018practice-e18]. **Also say what criteria you judge by:** they argue that when reporting research results you should state the norms and tests you used, and at least include a commentary explaining the context in which the work should be understood[^candy2018practice-e14]. For example: "when I judge one version better than the last, these are the points I look at" `[Our analysis]`.

**Why:** Candy argues that new knowledge at doctoral level has to be shareable and open to verification or challenge[^candy2006practice-e8]; in a PhD submission, the work cannot be expected to speak for itself[^candy2018practice-e10].

### Step 6: Use the text to connect the work to the contribution

**How:** In the UK QAA statement this text is called a "critical commentary": an artifact or composition is usually accompanied by a critical commentary on the work[^qaa2020doctoral-e3]. In the performing arts, for example, the written and practical parts complement each other; the written part may be shorter than a traditional thesis and includes reflection and contextualization[^qaa2020doctoral-e4]. The PRAG-UK report calls this kind of text a "research narrative" and gives an example structure, for cases where the research narrative is not embodied in the practice itself[^bulley2021practice-e22]: (abstract) → context / introduction → research inquiry / questions in the practice → statement of methods → the practice itself, a description of it, or its documentation → insights / discussion of what was learned → conclusion and references[^bulley2021practice-e21]. Candy and Edmonds stress that the written thesis is an essential part of the research output, not an optional add-on, and must state the aims, context, methods, and outcomes[^candy2018practice-e11]; it must also say what is new about the work and tell the reader **how to look at it**[^candy2018practice-e12].

**Why:** The work itself is not an explanation; you need text to link the development and nature of the work with an understanding of the creative process[^candy2006practice-e11]. Without the text, the work on its own cannot count as a research output[^candy2018practice-e17]. In the PRAG-UK report, a researcher discussing how institutions assess practice outputs said: assessors can guess, but guessing is not their job; making the innovation clear is the researcher's job[^bulley2021practice-e17].

**Where people go wrong:**

- **Writing an introduction to the work or an artist's statement.** Write what you asked, how you did it, and what you found. Drafting it once in the form of the REF 300-word statement is a good exercise (see the [field overview](../README.md#how-it-is-assessed-the-uks-ref-2021-as-an-example)) `[Our analysis]`.
- **Convoluted language.** One respondent said: the simpler the language, the stronger the claim; use small words for big ideas, not big words for small ideas[^bulley2021practice-e20].
- **Using a nonlinear form to look "novel".** Before you do, ask: will it create accessibility problems for some readers? Will it affect long-term preservation, or whether others can find it?[^bulley2021practice-e23]
- **Not knowing what the thesis should look like.** PRAG-UK suggests looking for examples in thesis repositories such as the British Library's EThOS and Open Access Theses and Dissertations (OATD), which hold hundreds of practice research PhD theses completed over the last twenty years[^bulley2021practice-e32].

### Step 7: Present the work and the text together, and prepare for the viva

**How:** According to the UK QAA statement, the final doctoral assessment covers a thesis, portfolio, artifact, or composition (usually with a critical commentary), plus an oral examination (viva); there are usually at least two examiners, typically one internal and at least one external[^qaa2020doctoral-e3]. Candy and Edmonds's advice on preparing the work:

- **Get examiners as close to the real experience as you can.** Examiners need access to the work in a form as close as possible to the real experience[^candy2018practice-e7], usually by visiting an exhibition or attending a live performance; when that is not possible, use audio recordings, film, photographs, and so on[^candy2018practice-e8]. Tebby's PhD examination was a thesis plus an exhibition, and the examiners spent a long time in the exhibition[^candy2018practice-e20].
- **Prepare a permanent record.** The work itself may not be storable in a library; then you must submit documentation good enough for the complete work to be understood[^candy2018practice-e9]. Tebby's exhibition was fully documented, and the slides were deposited in the library with the thesis[^candy2018practice-e20].

**What examiners look for.** QAA says that in the creative arts, most doctoral assessment looks for originality, a contribution to knowledge in the discipline, and work of a standard publishable after peer review[^qaa2020doctoral-e5]. Whatever form the submission takes, the material as a whole must present the research question and critically evaluate how far it has been answered[^qaa2020doctoral-e6]. The QAA statement is a reference point for UK institutions, not a mandatory requirement[^qaa2020doctoral-e1]; your own university's regulations take precedence.

**Publishing afterward.** Besides ordinary journal articles, consider journals dedicated to artistic research, such as JAR `[Our analysis]`. The following are the requirements of this one journal: its "expositions" combine text, images, film, and sound on the web[^jar2026submissions-e9], and readers should be able to see all the main points in about an hour[^jar2026submissions-e2]; it accepts material from PhD research[^jar2026submissions-e4], but submissions that reproduce large parts of a thesis without reframing them are not accepted[^jar2026submissions-e3]; media files must be uploaded rather than linked externally (links break), and you should test that they play on another computer[^jar2026submissions-e6][^jar2026submissions-e7]. JAR says that the time from submission to publication is currently usually eight months to a year[^jar2026submissions-e10].

### Step 8: Handle ethics and rights properly

General ethical principles are in [Research integrity and ethics](../../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md) in Throughout the project; image copyright and community collaboration are in the [field overview](../README.md#using-images-of-works-and-recordings-in-your-writing-check-copyright-first). Below are only the extra points to think about in a practice project:

- **Write the ethical issues into your proposal.** Candy 2006 says the first step in designing a project is writing a research proposal that lists the methods and ethical issues; informed consent must be obtained from participants before the research begins[^candy2006practice-e33]. Audiences who take part in an interactive work, and performers and collaborators you record, can all be "participants" `[Our analysis]`.
- **Borrow ethics protocols from the social sciences.** The PRAG-UK report notes that practice researchers sometimes fail to consider the ethics of human participants; one respondent found training in social science and qualitative research methods useful[^bulley2021practice-e33], and the report suggests learning from the protocols the social sciences already have[^bulley2021practice-e35]. Situations that deserve extra thought include working with vulnerable groups, sensitive themes such as loss and trauma, experiments involving people or animals, and physical and mental health issues[^bulley2021practice-e34].
- **List everyone involved in making the work**[^bulley2021practice-e12], and **decide what can be made public**. Professional creative work is often funded from outside the university, and putting it into a public research output may cost you financially and in intellectual property[^bulley2021practice-e36]. One option is to publish representative excerpts (a few pages of a score, film clips) under a suitable Creative Commons license[^bulley2021practice-e28].

## 3. Checklist

This checklist draws mainly on Section 3 of the JAR review form ("how the submission exposes practice as research", which speaks for this one journal only), the UK QAA statement on doctoral assessment, and Candy's two sources. JAR suggests authors also read the review form to learn how submissions will be reviewed[^jar2023peerreview-e2].

**Must do**

- [ ] State the research question clearly, and in what sense the work answers it[^qaa2020doctoral-e6][^jar2023peerreview-e3]
- [ ] Contextualize the work, with references[^candy2006practice-e21][^jar2023peerreview-e4]
- [ ] Describe the method, and why you used it[^candy2006practice-e20][^jar2023peerreview-e4]
- [ ] Say what is new about the work in content, form, or technique[^jar2023peerreview-e3], and what new understanding or experience it brings[^jar2023peerreview-e4]
- [ ] The work comes with documentation of the process and a textual analysis[^candy2006practice-e5]
- [ ] Examiners can access the work in a form close to the real experience; if it cannot be stored in a library, there is good enough documentation[^candy2018practice-e7][^candy2018practice-e9]
- [ ] If people are involved, informed consent is obtained before starting[^candy2006practice-e33]; everyone involved in making the work is listed[^bulley2021practice-e12]
- [ ] Copyright for other people's material you use has been cleared[^jar2026submissions-e8] (rules differ by country; see the [field overview](../README.md#using-images-of-works-and-recordings-in-your-writing-check-copyright-first))

**Better practice**

- [ ] Reflection is scheduled, with review points and an overview chart[^candy2006practice-e30][^candy2006practice-e31]
- [ ] The criteria for judging the work are stated[^candy2018practice-e14]
- [ ] Where claims involve the audience, there is evidence of audience responses[^candy2018practice-e23]
- [ ] Readers are told how to look at the work[^candy2018practice-e24]
- [ ] The text uses plain language[^bulley2021practice-e20]

**Fine to skip; not a flaw**

- A long philosophical argument that "practice can be a method"[^bulley2021practice-e18].
- The research question changed partway through: the final question can be settled when the project is complete[^bulley2021practice-e15][^bulley2021practice-e16]; just describe the changes honestly `[Our analysis]`.

**Criticisms the JAR review form says do not necessarily hold** (this one journal only)

- **"It doesn't meet conventional academic standards."** The JAR review form says a submission may succeed in exposing practice as research even if it does not meet conventional academic criteria; what reviewers should point out is **where departing from the standards actually damages the submission**[^jar2023peerreview-e5]. For each thing that is missing, the form asks reviewers to ask whether the omission matters[^jar2023peerreview-e3].
- **"The author's identity is visible, so it isn't double-blind."** JAR considers that works often carry their maker's "signature", so double-blind review does not work in artistic research, and it uses single-blind review instead[^jar2023peerreview-e1].

## 4. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Treating a body of work as the research, with the question "I want to make a series of works" | Knowledge that is new only to you is not knowledge in the sense of PhD research[^candy2018practice-e15]; JAR also requires going beyond documenting and describing the work, to address questions and claims about knowledge[^jar2026submissions-e1] |
| Reviewing only theory, without positioning the work among comparable practice | If you don't know where the work stands in the field, it is hard to judge whether it is new[^bulley2021practice-e31] |
| Catching up on documentation only near the end | Process documentation is a required part of the output[^candy2006practice-e5]; reflection should be scheduled, not done when you happen to remember[^candy2006practice-e31] |
| Submitting only the work and expecting it to speak for itself | In a PhD submission the work cannot be expected to speak for itself[^candy2018practice-e10]; making the innovation clear is the researcher's job[^bulley2021practice-e17] |
| Half the text argues that "practice is a method too" | Journal editors, supervisors, and assessors have all noticed that this delays getting to the research itself[^bulley2021practice-e19] |
| Claiming "the audience experienced X" with no evidence from the audience | To show her work affected audiences, Seevinck reported a dedicated evaluation of participants' responses[^candy2018practice-e23] |
| Documentation that is long and hard to open | One of the most frequent comments from PRAG-UK respondents as readers was that documentation should be of a reasonable format and length[^bulley2021practice-e29]; external links break[^jar2026submissions-e6] |
| Copying large parts of the PhD thesis into a journal submission | JAR does not accept unreframed copies[^jar2026submissions-e3] |
| Forgetting that performers, audiences, and collaborators are research participants too | Practice researchers sometimes fail to consider the ethics of human participants[^bulley2021practice-e33] |

## 5. Further reading

- **Candy 2006, Practice Based Research: A Guide**: a short, free guide. It is very concrete about what goes in each chapter of the thesis and how to keep a reflective record (the Q&A on p. 8). Good to read before your proposal.
- **Candy & Edmonds 2018, Leonardo, vol. 51, no. 1**: on the role of the work in research and how examiners access it, with several PhD cases that show how different projects handle evaluation. The full text is open access in the University of Technology Sydney institutional repository.
- **Bulley & Şahin 2021, PRAG-UK report**: Section 2.1 of the second report covers output structure and documentation; useful to check against while writing your thesis and preparing documentation.
- **QAA 2020, Characteristics Statement: Doctoral Degree**: the overall description of doctoral assessment in the UK; shows how practice-based doctorates are examined there.
- **JAR submission requirements and review form**: read them if you want to publish in this journal, or want to know "what will reviewers ask". Section 3 of the review form works as a self-check list.
- For the field background (names, REF, copyright, community ethics), see the [arts field overview](../README.md); for research that works with an organization to drive change, see the Research types page [Action research](../../../06_methods/action_research.md); for design-based research in education, see [Design-based research](../../education/practices/design_based_research.md).
