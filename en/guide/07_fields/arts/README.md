# Arts: field overview

This page explains how research actually works in the arts (art history, musicology, the performing arts, design, and practice-based arts research). The field contains **two very different kinds of research**: **scholarly research about art**, which studies works, archives, and artists; and **research that uses creative practice as its method**, where making the work is itself part of the research. The page treats the two separately. Practices shared by every field (choosing a topic, reading the literature, writing, research integrity) are covered in the Parts listed in [The research journey at a glance](../../00_start-here/research-journey.md) and in [Throughout the project](../../08_throughout/README.md), and are not repeated here. For which research strategies arts research uses and what evidence each one needs, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence); for how-to pages, see [Research methods common in this field](#research-methods-common-in-this-field) at the end.

Our sources come mainly from the UK, the US, and Europe: UK research assessment documents and a survey-based report from England, guidelines from two US societies, a declaration jointly issued by European organizations, and a report from a research center at an Australian university. When the page describes what an organization requires, it means **that organization's practice, in that country**. Degree systems, research assessment, and copyright law differ from country to country, so **follow the rules of your own country, your university, and your target publisher** `[Our analysis]`.

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- tell "research about art" apart from "research that uses practice as its method", and know which kind your project is;
- explain what a work has to do to count as research, and how to explain its contribution in writing;
- know how UK research assessment evaluates outputs such as exhibitions, performances, and compositions;
- know which research strategies arts research commonly uses, and what evidence each one usually relies on;
- know which copyright questions to check before you use images of works or recordings in your thesis or papers;
- know what extra responsibilities come with working with participants and communities.

## How research works in this field

### First, tell the two kinds of research apart

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) *Frascati Manual* is the manual countries use to measure research and development (R&D). It recommends separating three things: research for the arts, research about the arts, and artistic expression itself[^oecd2015frascati-e11]. For a newcomer, the most important distinction is between the last two (cells without a footnote are our own summary `[Our analysis]`):

| | Research about art | Research that uses practice as its method |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Art history, musicology, theater studies[^oecd2015frascati-e12] | Making creative work, where the work is the basis of the contribution to knowledge[^candy2006practice-e1] |
| Evidence | Works, documents, archives, and other sources | The work itself, plus documentation of the process and written analysis[^candy2006practice-e5] |
| Outputs | Mainly writing | The work plus a "research narrative" (see below)[^bulley2021practice-e5] |
| Closest existing field page | [History and archaeology](../history-archaeology/README.md) | The second kind on this page; among research methods, [Action research](../../06_methods/action_research.md) has things in common with it |

### Kind one: scholarly research about art

Art history and musicology study past works, documents, and archives, and **their methods of source criticism and archival work are shared with history** `[Our analysis]`. For the distinction between primary and secondary sources and for source criticism, see ["What 'evidence' means here"](../history-archaeology/README.md#what-evidence-means-here) on the History and archaeology page; for how to use archives and take notes, see [Working in the archive](../history-archaeology/README.md#working-in-an-archive) on the same page. Below are only the points specific to art history, based on the College Art Association's (CAA, the US professional association for art historians and artists) 2014 *Standards for the Practice of Art History*. These are a US society's professional standards and currently have no enforcement provisions[^caa2014standards-e12].

- **Record your findings and make your evidence available.** Art historians should record their findings carefully, make the sources, evidence, and data they used available to others, and neither misrepresent evidence nor plagiarize[^caa2014standards-e1]. Scholarly integrity also requires awareness of personal and cultural bias[^caa2014standards-e2].
- **Credit photographs and opinions "in the margins" too.** Credit the sources of photographs, not just of ideas and text[^caa2014standards-e3]. Opinions other scholars have written on mats of drawings, on the backs of photographs, or in museum files, and even opinions they told you in person, should be cited when relevant[^caa2014standards-e4].
- **Share primary sources you discover.** CAA says the person who discovers primary source material has an ethical obligation to share it with colleagues and should aim to publish it within three years[^caa2014standards-e7]. Using your influence to get an archive to reserve material for you is improper[^caa2014standards-e8].
- **Watch for conflicts of interest.** CAA recommends declining gifts of works from artists, as a way to keep the scholarly process impartial[^caa2014standards-e9].
- **Give students full credit when you collaborate.** When teachers and students publish together, the students' contributions should be fully acknowledged[^caa2014standards-e11].

**Publishing books.** CAA's standards mention that scholarly publications have to be evaluated in "a world in which art-history monograph publishing opportunities are diminished"[^caa2014standards-e14]. CAA also states that submitting a book manuscript to several publishers at once is not unethical, as long as each one is told[^caa2014standards-e13]. That is a US society's position on book manuscripts; journal articles follow other rules, so check the requirements of your target journal and publisher `[Our analysis]`. For turning a dissertation into a book, see the [History and archaeology page](../history-archaeology/README.md#monographs-and-turning-the-dissertation-into-a-book).

### Kind two: research that uses practice as its method

**The names.** In English, practice-based research and practice-led research are often used interchangeably[^candy2006practice-e16]. In 2006, the Creativity and Cognition Studios, a research center at the University of Technology Sydney in Australia, published a report for graduate students by Linda Candy. She draws the line this way: if the creative artifact is the basis of the contribution to knowledge, it is practice-based research; if the research leads primarily to new understanding about practice, it is practice-led research[^candy2006practice-e1]. A practice-led doctoral thesis can be written entirely in text, without a creative work attached[^candy2006practice-e3]. In the UK, people also often say practice research. The Practice Research Advisory Group UK (PRAG-UK) published a report in 2021 based on a survey of 62 practice researchers, theorists, research support staff, and policy makers in England[^bulley2021practice-e8]. Its definition: practice research is when practice is the significant method conveyed in a research output[^bulley2021practice-e1]. In Europe, the usual term is artistic research. In 2020, ten European organizations, including the Association Européenne des Conservatoires (AEC, the European association of music schools)[^aec2020vienna-e9], jointly issued the *Vienna Declaration on Artistic Research*, which defines artistic research as practice-based, practice-led research in the arts[^aec2020vienna-e1]. It covers all art forms, including architecture, design, film, photography, fine arts, media and digital arts, music, and the performing arts[^aec2020vienna-e8].

**Making work is not the same as doing research.** This is the point newcomers most need to think through:

- Candy points out that practitioners do some "research" in their everyday work, but mostly for their own immediate goals, not to add to shared knowledge[^candy2006practice-e4].
- The PRAG-UK report says that excellent practice is not necessarily excellent practice research, and vice versa; for practice to become research, **the research has to be articulated**[^bulley2021practice-e2]. A line the report quotes puts it more bluntly: doing good research is not about making successful works; it is about succeeding in generating new insights[^bulley2021practice-e3].
- Candy holds that new knowledge at doctoral level must be shared and must be open to verification or challenge[^candy2006practice-e8]. Tacit knowledge (a "feel" for the work that stays with you and is never shared) does not count as knowledge in this sense[^candy2006practice-e9].

**Research needs a question, a context, and a method.** Candy's report lists three conditions for research: define research questions; specify the research context; specify the methods for answering the questions[^candy2006practice-e7]. This matches what [Key concepts](../../00_start-here/key-concepts.md) and the Part 2 chapter [From research questions to an evidence plan](../../02_do-the-project/01_question-to-evidence.md) say; the difference is that here the "method" can itself be creative practice `[Our analysis]`.

**Document the process as you go.** Candy says creative outputs must be accompanied by documentation of the research process and by written analysis or explanation, to support your position and show critical reflection[^candy2006practice-e5]. Her concrete advice:

1. Keep a continuous record in an online diary, a blog, or a handwritten notebook[^candy2006practice-e10];
2. Schedule reflection rather than doing it only when you happen to think of it[^candy2006practice-e10]. For how to categorize and keep your records, see [Organizing research materials](../../08_throughout/organizing_materials.md#5-keep-three-kinds-of-notes-apart).

**Explain your contribution in writing.** A work is not its own explanation; you need writing that links the development and nature of the work to an understanding of the creative process[^candy2006practice-e11]. Candy says the significance and context of your claims are described in words, but full understanding requires direct reference to the work[^candy2006practice-e2]. PRAG-UK calls this text the "research narrative" and found that, in most cases, combining a research narrative with the practice is an effective way to share insights[^bulley2021practice-e5]. They also say you need not feel obliged to use complex non-linear or multimedia formats; sometimes a journal article or a book chapter is the best place for the research narrative[^bulley2021practice-e6].

**A doctoral thesis has to situate the work.** Candy writes that practice-based doctoral submissions must include a substantial contextualization of the creative work[^candy2006practice-e6]. If creative work is submitted for examination, the thesis structure she suggests has a chapter introducing the new research that at least explains what is significant and new in the work[^candy2006practice-e17]. In tracing how these degrees came about, she also mentions a regulation that the thesis deposited in the library must include a permanent record of the creative work submitted for examination[^candy2006practice-e15]. Universities differ in their rules on creative work, thesis length, and the form of the examination; follow your own university's rules `[Our analysis]`.

### How it is assessed: the UK's REF 2021 as an example

The UK's Research Excellence Framework (REF) is the system the UK higher education funding bodies use to assess universities' research periodically `[Our analysis]`. Its 2021 documents spell out in detail how practice outputs are assessed, so they work as a checklist for "how to explain the research in a work" `[Our analysis]`. This is the UK system; other countries do it differently.

- **The definition of research.** REF defines research as "a process of investigation leading to new insights, effectively shared"[^ref2019guidance-e1]; creating ideas, images, performances, and artifacts (including design) counts as research when it leads to new or substantially improved insights[^ref2019guidance-e2].
- **A wide range of output types.** The Art and Design unit of assessment anticipates texts, edited publications, creative practice, artifacts, events, and curatorial outputs[^ref2019panel-e6]; the unit covering music, drama, dance, film, and related subjects anticipates artifacts, creative practice, curatorial outputs, edited publications, recordings, and writings[^ref2019panel-e7]. One output can be several types at once, for example a composition that has been performed or recorded, or an exhibition whose research lies in the curation[^ref2019panel-e8].
- **The 300-word statement.** When the research is not evident in the output itself, a written statement must be attached[^ref2019guidance-e3]. The arts and humanities panel (Main Panel D) strongly encourages a statement of up to 300 words[^ref2019panel-e1] covering three things: the **research process** (the questions, the process of discovery, the methods, and the creative or intellectual context the work draws on)[^ref2019panel-e2], the **research insights** (the findings or creative outcomes)[^ref2019panel-e3], and **dissemination** (where and how the insights were effectively shared)[^ref2019panel-e4].
- **What is assessed and what is ignored.** The panels assess originality, significance, and rigor, and ignore material that comments on the perceived quality of the output[^ref2019panel-e5]. So writing "this was a widely acclaimed performance" in your statement does nothing; say what you asked, how you went about it, and what you found `[Our analysis]`.
> **Where sources disagree: is making art research in itself?**
>
> - **OECD *Frascati Manual* (2015, a manual for R&D statistics)**: artistic performance is usually not R&D, because it seeks new expression rather than new knowledge[^oecd2015frascati-e13]. Art is creative, but it also has to meet the other criteria (novel, uncertain, systematic, transferable and/or reproducible[^oecd2015frascati-e16]) to count[^oecd2015frascati-e15]. However, when a university awards a doctorate to an artist on the basis of artistic performance, this has to be judged case by case[^oecd2015frascati-e14].
> - **The *Vienna Declaration* (2020, a policy document from European organizations)**: excellent artistic research is epistemic inquiry carried out through high-level artistic practice and reflection[^aec2020vienna-e2]. It holds that artistic research should be recognized through peer review, with reviewers who together cover all the disciplinary competences the work involves[^aec2020vienna-e4]. The declaration also calls for artistic research to be a separate category in the Frascati Manual[^aec2020vienna-e5], and says artistic research in general does not get research funding on equal terms with other fields and is sometimes not even eligible to apply[^aec2020vienna-e6]. Note that it is an advocacy document addressed to policy makers and funders[^aec2020vienna-e7].
> - **UK REF 2021**: see the definition in the previous section.
>
> **In practice**: what these sources share is that making art counts as research only when it produces new understanding that can be shared and tested. So whatever definition your system uses, state your research question, method, and insights clearly `[Our analysis]`.

### Publishing and dissemination

- **Many forms.** Besides articles and monographs, there are exhibitions, performances, recordings, and curated projects[^ref2019panel-e6][^ref2019panel-e7]. The *Vienna Declaration* says artistic research includes many things that are not (or not only) text, such as works, movement, and sound[^aec2020vienna-e3]. For example, the *Journal for Artistic Research* uses a research database called the Research Catalogue and publishes work in the form of "expositions"[^bulley2021practice-e14].
- **Documentation is all your readers will see.** For the vast majority of practice research outputs, readers cannot experience the practice itself; they can only see the "documentation"[^bulley2021practice-e4]. Performances and installations end and are gone, so **plan the documentation while you are making the work** `[Our analysis]`.
- **Don't let your outputs scatter.** The report based on 62 people in England says the parts of a practice research output are often spread across commercial video and audio platforms, institutional repositories, blogs, personal websites, and elsewhere[^bulley2021practice-e7]. Where possible, deposit the documentation of the work together with the research narrative in your university's institutional repository `[Our analysis]`.
- **Make it accessible.** The same report reminds you to give multimedia content enough descriptive text and to provide transcripts wherever possible[^bulley2021practice-e9].

## Norms and ethics in this field

For general ethical principles, see [Research integrity and ethics](../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md) in Throughout the project. Below are only the parts specific to this field.

### Using images of works and recordings in your writing: check copyright first

Arts research often uses other people's works: plates in a paper, recorded excerpts in a lecture, material quoted in your own creative work. **Whether you can use something, and whether you have to pay, depends on the country where you publish and on the laws and contracts that govern the material** `[Our analysis]`. Below are the main points of CAA's 2015 *Code of Best Practices in Fair Use for the Visual Arts*. Fair use is part of US copyright law, and the Code applies only to uses in the US[^caa2015fairuse-e1]. Other countries have different copyright exceptions; we add only China below.

**China**: Article 24 of the Copyright Law of the People's Republic of China (as amended in 2020)[^npc2020copyright-e1] lists the cases in which a work may be used without permission and without paying the copyright owner. The two most directly relevant to writing papers and teaching are: appropriate quotation from another person's published work in your own work, to introduce or comment on a work or to illustrate a point[^npc2020copyright-e3]; and translation, adaptation, compilation, broadcasting, or reproduction in small quantities of a published work by teachers or researchers for classroom teaching or scientific research, provided it is not published or distributed[^npc2020copyright-e4]. Whichever case applies, you must give the name of the author and the title of the work, must not affect the normal use of the work, and must not unreasonably prejudice the copyright owner's legitimate rights and interests[^npc2020copyright-e2]. This is based on the English translation on the National People's Congress website; the official Chinese text prevails. The law does not say how much quotation counts as "appropriate"; to find out whether your use falls under one of these cases, ask your university's copyright office or your publisher `[Our analysis]`.

**When you write about art**, scholars (and their publishers) may invoke fair use to quote, excerpt, or reproduce works[^caa2015fairuse-e2], provided that:

- the use is justified by an analytic purpose, and you are prepared to explain that justification[^caa2015fairuse-e3];
- the analytic purpose outweighs "simply showing the work"[^caa2015fairuse-e4];
- the size and resolution of images are no greater than the analysis needs[^caa2015fairuse-e5];
- you credit the original work following the field's conventions[^caa2015fairuse-e6].

**Where fair use does not reach**:

- when a license agreement or other contract governs reproduction and use[^caa2015fairuse-e9];
- image fees charged by the holding collection: fair use cannot be invoked to avoid paying them[^caa2015fairuse-e8];
- works in the public domain, which do not need it in the first place[^caa2015fairuse-e14]. CAA also cautions that the US *Bridgeman* decision (which dealt with photographs of two-dimensional works `[Our analysis]`) does not automatically apply to photographs of three-dimensional works such as sculpture, architecture, or the performing arts[^caa2015fairuse-e13].

**When you make art**, using someone else's work should create new artistic meaning; a mere change of medium may not be enough[^caa2015fairuse-e10]. Credit the source unless you have an aesthetic reason you can articulate for not doing so[^caa2015fairuse-e11].

**Don't over-censor yourself.** Before drafting the Code, CAA surveyed nearly 12,000 members and conducted 100 interviews[^caa2015fairuse-e12]. It found that many US visual arts professionals believed they always needed permission, even when they could safely have relied on fair use[^caa2015fairuse-e7]. CAA's 2014 standards also note that understanding of fair use and intellectual property changes quickly, so look for the most current sources[^caa2014standards-e10].

**Music and recordings.** Our sources do not include a dedicated guide from musicology on quoting scores or recordings. The Society for Ethnomusicology (SEM) holds that its members need to understand copyright and the laws on intellectual and cultural property[^sem1998ethics-e5]; the PRAG-UK report also notes that practice research often relies on multimedia documentation, which makes copyright and licensing especially complicated[^bulley2021practice-e10]. Follow the advice of your target publisher and your university's copyright office.

### Working with participants and communities

Performances, participatory art, community projects, and field recordings all involve people `[Our analysis]`. Candy's report says that when research involves people, informed consent must be obtained before it begins[^candy2006practice-e13]. The PRAG-UK report notes that practice researchers sometimes fail to consider the ethics of human participants[^bulley2021practice-e11]; that for ethical reasons some projects (for example, ones about care or bereavement) are hard to share publicly[^bulley2021practice-e13]; and that everyone who took part in making the work should be listed and acknowledged[^bulley2021practice-e12].

When you study living musical and artistic traditions, the following are the expectations of two US societies (SEM and CAA). SEM itself acknowledges that these ethical values may not represent all practitioners everywhere[^sem1998ethics-e7]:

- **Build relationships on informed consent and respect.** SEM's 1998 ethics statement asks that field research rest on informed consent, the right to privacy and confidentiality, and mutual respect[^sem1998ethics-e1], and that researchers be sensitive to questions of who owns recordings and photographs[^sem1998ethics-e2].
- **The responsibility is long-term.** Responsibilities in field research extend beyond the field site and are often a long-term commitment to collaborators and their communities[^sem1998ethics-e3]. Field research can open the way to unforeseen, even exploitative, later uses of recordings[^sem1998ethics-e4].
- **Give results and materials back to the community.** CAA says art historians have often not considered the host community and have not left their results or field materials with it[^caa2014standards-e6]. It asks scholars who study living art traditions to deposit originals or copies of all field materials relevant to their publications, and to leave a copy of the published results in the community[^caa2014standards-e5].
- **Authorship and acknowledgment.** When you publish, give collaborators, colleagues, and students the credit they are due[^sem1998ethics-e6].

**How this differs from ordinary interview research.** SEM's 2008 position statement argues that the emphasis of US university Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) on anonymity, and their requirement to destroy records at the end of a project, are at odds with how ethnomusicology works[^sem2008irb-e1]. It holds that research records also matter for the historical record and should be archived[^sem2008irb-e3], and that how you obtain permission should depend on local culture[^sem2008irb-e2]. The statement responds to the US rules as they stood before the 2018 revision `[Our analysis]`. Whether you need ethics approval, and whether you may name your interviewees, is up to your institution's ethics committee. This point is close to oral history; see the [History and archaeology page](../history-archaeology/README.md#oral-history-the-narrators-consent-and-control).

## Common strategies and standards of evidence

A **research strategy** is the main logic by which a project produces new knowledge, decided by your central claim; see [Key concepts](../../00_start-here/key-concepts.md#research-strategy) and [Research strategies](../../05_research-strategies/README.md). The table lists only the five strategies our sources support.

The grouping and the "What it looks like in the arts" column are our own summary; each standard represents only the practice of the organization, country, or article cited in its footnote `[Our analysis]`. The Measurement row belongs to music cognition (also called music psychology), which overlaps with psychology; for the general conventions, see the [Psychology page](../psychology/README.md) `[Our analysis]`. Our sources do not cover the other strategies.

|---|---|---|---|
| [Building and improving](../../05_research-strategies/build_improve.md) | Practice-based research: the creative work produced is the basis of the contribution to knowledge[^candy2006practice-e1] | The work plus documentation of the process and written analysis, to show critical reflection[^candy2006practice-e5]; new knowledge must be shareable and open to verification or challenge[^candy2006practice-e8]; UK REF 2021 assesses originality, significance, and rigor, and ignores material that comments on the perceived quality of the output[^ref2019panel-e5]; the *Vienna Declaration* holds that recognition should come through peer review covering all the disciplinary competences the work involves[^aec2020vienna-e4] | [Practice-based research](practices/practice_based_research.md) |
| [Interpretation and argument](../../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md) | Research about art, such as art history, musicology, and theater studies[^oecd2015frascati-e12], studying works, documents, and archives | Record findings carefully, make the sources, evidence, and data you used available to others, and do not misrepresent evidence (US, CAA)[^caa2014standards-e1]; credit photograph sources and other people's spoken or handwritten opinions when relevant[^caa2014standards-e3][^caa2014standards-e4] | [Archival research](../../06_methods/archival_research.md); for source criticism, see the [History and archaeology page](../history-archaeology/README.md#what-evidence-means-here) |

| [Synthesis](../../05_research-strategies/synthesize.md) | Reviews of research on the arts and health: pooling studies from different disciplines that use different methods | This row comes from a single report: Fancourt and Finn's scoping review, published by the WHO Regional Office for Europe in 2019 (a scoping review first maps what evidence exists; see mapping studies under [Synthesis](../../05_research-strategies/synthesize.md)). Its aim is to present the existing evidence as broadly as possible, without filtering by research method[^fancourt2019evidence-e1]; the authors say they did not do a systematic search, and chose a scoping review over a systematic review so as not to be restricted by study design or outcome measures, which they consider important for such an interdisciplinary field[^fancourt2019evidence-e2]; the quality of the evidence is uneven, so the report compares findings across several studies with different strengths (triangulation) to offset the weaknesses and biases of individual studies[^fancourt2019evidence-e3]; it searched only English- and Russian-language literature[^fancourt2019evidence-e4]. Our sources do not cover standards for reviews in art history, musicology, and similar areas | [Review studies](../../06_methods/review_study.md) |

How to use this table `[Our analysis]`:

- **First decide which kind of research you are doing** (see [First, tell the two kinds of research apart](#first-tell-the-two-kinds-of-research-apart)), then find the strategy from your central claim. Practice-led research leads mainly to new understanding about practice[^candy2006practice-e1] and often falls within the broad scope of action research[^candy2006practice-e3]; the strategy pages do not yet discuss which strategy it belongs to, so decide from your central claim.
- **For the same strategy, "good enough" in the arts is very different from other fields.** Practice-based research also "makes something new", but it generally does not compare against a baseline (see [Building and improving](../../05_research-strategies/build_improve.md#5-which-fields-use-it)); what matters is articulating the research, since excellent practice is not necessarily excellent practice research[^bulley2021practice-e2].

**Submission requirements of empirical aesthetics and music science journals.** Below are the main points of two journals' submission guidelines (from 2024 web archives); they represent only these two journals:

- *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts* (the journal of Division 10 of the American Psychological Association) publishes original empirical research and integrative evaluations of existing research, and generally discourages qualitative research, case studies, and literature reviews[^apa2024aesthetics-e1]. It accepts Registered Reports: you first submit the introduction and methods (including hypotheses, a sample size set by statistical power analysis, and the analysis plan), and if this stage is approved, the paper is accepted in principle whatever the results[^apa2024aesthetics-e2] (for background, see [What changed afterward](../psychology/README.md#what-changed-afterward) on the Psychology page). The submission guidelines say the American Psychological Association expects authors to make their data available during review and for at least 5 years after publication[^apa2024aesthetics-e3]; the journal also recommends describing the participant sample and stating the limits on how far the findings generalize[^apa2024aesthetics-e4].
- *Music & Science* (published by Sage) asks you to submit the raw data with the article (for example, statistical data files, code for reproducing the analysis, audio, video) or to explain in detail in the article how to access it[^sage2024musicscience-e1], labeled clearly enough that reviewers or readers can rerun your analysis[^sage2024musicscience-e2]. The methods section must state whether participants gave informed consent and whether it was written or verbal[^sage2024musicscience-e4]. Review is double-blind, with at least two reviewers[^sage2024musicscience-e3].

## Advice for newcomers

1. **First settle which kind of research you are doing.** Research about art and research that uses practice as its method have different requirements for evidence and outputs. If yours is practice research, also find out which name and definition your university uses[^candy2006practice-e16].
2. **Write your research question in one sentence.** Research needs a question, a context, and a method[^candy2006practice-e7]. "I want to make a series of works" is not a research question `[Our analysis]`.
3. **Document the process from day one.** Keep a continuous diary or notebook and schedule your reflection[^candy2006practice-e10]; plan the documentation of performances and installations in advance `[Our analysis]`.
4. **Practice explaining your contribution in 300 words.** Write a statement covering REF's three items (research process, research insights, dissemination)[^ref2019panel-e2][^ref2019panel-e3][^ref2019panel-e4]. Even if you are not in the UK, this is a good test of whether your research is clearly articulated `[Our analysis]`.
5. **Before publishing, take stock of your rights in images and recordings.** List the source, the rights holder, and the basis you plan to rely on (license, fair use, or public domain) for every image and recording, and ask your publisher what it requires `[Our analysis]`. In the US, fair use needs an analytic purpose and a justification you can explain[^caa2015fairuse-e3].
6. **When people or communities are involved, get ethics approval first and decide where your materials will go.** Obtain informed consent before you begin[^candy2006practice-e13]; consider archiving your field materials and returning them to the community[^caa2014standards-e5][^sem2008irb-e3].
7. **When you study living artists, avoid conflicts of interest.** CAA recommends declining gifts of works from artists[^caa2014standards-e9].

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Treating a portfolio as research | Excellent practice is not necessarily excellent practice research, and the research has to be articulated[^bulley2021practice-e2]; everyday creative work mostly serves personal goals and does not add to shared knowledge[^candy2006practice-e4] |
| Submitting only the work, with no written explanation | A work is not its own explanation[^candy2006practice-e11]; the UK REF requires a written statement when the research is not evident in the output[^ref2019guidance-e3] |
| Writing in your statement how popular the work was, rather than what you asked and what you found | REF panels ignore material that comments on the perceived quality of the output[^ref2019panel-e5]; they assess originality, significance, and rigor |
| Thinking about documentation only after the performance is over | For the vast majority of practice research outputs, readers can only know the work through documentation[^bulley2021practice-e4] |
| Assuming "academic use" means you can use any image freely | Fair use is US law and applies only to uses in the US[^caa2015fairuse-e1]; it does not apply when a contract governs the use[^caa2015fairuse-e9], and it cannot be used to avoid a collection's fees[^caa2015fairuse-e8] |
| The opposite: believing you must buy a license for every image | CAA's survey found that many US professionals thought they needed permission even when they could have relied on fair use[^caa2015fairuse-e7] |
| Crediting the work but not the photograph source or someone's spoken tip | CAA requires crediting photograph sources and other people's spoken or handwritten opinions when relevant[^caa2014standards-e3][^caa2014standards-e4] |
| Taking field recordings away and never looking back | The responsibility is long-term[^sem1998ethics-e3]; CAA asks that materials and results be left with the host community[^caa2014standards-e5] |

## Research methods common in this field

How-to pages come in two groups: research practices used only in the arts live in the Arts section's `practices/`; research methods that other fields also use live in the [Research methods](../../06_methods/README.md) section. For which strategy each serves, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence) above. The differences to watch when you borrow a cross-field method are our own summary `[Our analysis]`:

- **Specific to the arts:** [Practice-based research](practices/practice_based_research.md). You make creative work, and the work is the basis of the contribution to knowledge[^candy2006practice-e1]; the output is the work, its documentation, and a research narrative.
- [Archival research](../../06_methods/archival_research.md): art history and musicology studying works, manuscripts, letters, and institutional archives.
- [Review studies](../../06_methods/review_study.md): when you write a literature review, you can borrow its approach to searching and selecting without copying the full systematic review procedure; do not cite secondhand, go back to the original[^candy2006practice-e12].
- [Controlled experiments](../../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md): music cognition experiments. For the differences, see the Measurement row in [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence) above: do not limit participants and materials to the West, and when you use scales across cultures, first confirm that participants understood them.
- [Case study and ethnography](../../06_methods/case_study.md) and [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md): field research in ethnomusicology and on living art traditions. For the differences, see above: participants are often not anonymized, and materials should be archived and returned to the community.
- [Action research](../../06_methods/action_research.md): practice-led research often falls within its broad scope[^candy2006practice-e3], and you can borrow its plan–act–reflect cycle; the difference is that here what is being improved is often the researcher's own creative practice rather than a partner organization.
- For design research, see [Design-based research](../education/practices/design_based_research.md) on the Education page. Candy points out that design research focuses more on how design practice works and how to improve it, and is often done by dedicated researchers rather than designers; the visual arts place more weight on the creative process and the works it produces[^candy2006practice-e14].

## Further reading

- **Candy 2006, *Practice Based Research: A Guide***: a report from an Australian research center on the difference between practice-based and practice-led research, how to structure a doctoral thesis, and how to keep a reflective record. A good first read if you are doing a creative doctorate.
- **Bulley & Şahin 2021, *Practice Research* (PRAG-UK report)**: two reports based on a survey of 62 people in England, on what practice research is, research narratives, and how to share and preserve outputs. Open access.
- **REF 2021, *Panel criteria and working methods* (REF 2019/02)**: paragraphs 264–267 explain how the arts and humanities panel reads statements accompanying practice outputs, and work as a checklist for writing your own statement; the definition of research is in Annex C of REF 2019/01.
- ***The Vienna Declaration on Artistic Research* (2020)**: a two-page European policy document, for understanding how artistic research is positioned in Europe and what it is asking for.
- **CAA, *Code of Best Practices in Fair Use for the Visual Arts* (2015)**: essential reading before you publish images of art in the US; readers in other countries can use it to understand why you should be able to justify a use.
- **CAA, *Standards for the Practice of Art History* (2014)**: research, publishing, conflicts of interest, and community responsibilities for US art historians.
- **SEM, *Position Statement on Ethical Considerations* (1998)**: the Society for Ethnomusicology's position statement on the ethics of field research.
