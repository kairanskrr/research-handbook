# Languages and literature: field overview

This page explains how research actually works in languages and literature. The field holds two very different traditions: **literary studies** (close reading of texts, building interpretations, archival and editorial work) and **linguistics** (fieldwork with speech communities, building corpora, running experiments). The page treats them separately; don't carry the practices of one straight over to the other. Practices shared by every field (choosing a topic, reading the literature, writing, research integrity) are covered in the chapters listed in [The research journey at a glance](../../00_start-here/research-journey.md) and in [Throughout the project](../../08_throughout/README.md), and are not repeated here. For which research strategies the field commonly uses and what evidence each one needs, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence); for how-to pages, see [Research methods common in this field](#research-methods-common-in-this-field).

Our sources come mainly from two US scholarly societies. The linguistics sections draw on the ethics statements and resolutions of the Linguistic Society of America (LSA), and on a position statement on data citation signed jointly by linguists. The literary studies sections draw on reports, guidelines, and the style website of the Modern Language Association (MLA, the US society for the study of language and literature), plus two free handouts, one from the Writing Center at the University of North Carolina in the US and one from the Department of English at the University of York in the UK. These two handouts are teaching materials the two universities give their students, not disciplinary rules. So when the page describes what a society or university requires, it means **that society's, that university's, and that country's practice**. Ethics review systems, research assessment, and publishing conventions differ between countries, so **follow the rules of your own country, your institution, and your target journal**.

In the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) classification, "6.5 Other humanities" has no page of its own. Depending on what you study, use this page, [History and archaeology](../history-archaeology/README.md), or [Philosophy, ethics, and religion](../philosophy-ethics-religion/README.md).

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- tell literary studies and linguistics apart, and know which side your project belongs to;
- start from close reading and build an evidence-based argument around a text;
- understand why US literary studies centers on the monograph, and how to cite in MLA style;
- know what responsibilities you have to speakers and communities when doing linguistic fieldwork;
- know why language data should be archived and cited, and how to make corpus and experimental work hold up to scrutiny;
- name the research strategies this field commonly uses, what evidence each relies on, and what standards it is judged by.

## How research works in this field

### First, tell the two traditions apart

The table below is our own summary; cells with a source carry a footnote. Some projects span both sides, for example using a corpus to study the style of literary texts: the interpretive part follows the standards of literary studies, the data part those of linguistics and data science.

| | Literary studies | Linguistics |
|---|---|---|
| What is studied | Literary texts (novels, poems, plays, etc.) and their historical and cultural context | Language itself: sounds, grammar, meaning, use, and change |
| Evidence | The language, imagery, syntax, and form of the text[^yorkclosereading-e1], and recurring patterns[^uncwritinglit-e12] | Audio and video recordings, corpus data[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e7], and native-speaker judgments[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e9] |
| Outputs (as seen in our sources) | US: monographs and journal articles[^mla2007evaluating-e3][^mla2007evaluating-e5], and scholarly editions[^mlacseguidelines-e1] | The LSA holds that besides articles, grammars, dictionaries, text collections, corpora, and the like also count as scholarship[^lsa2010documentation-e2][^lsa2010documentation-e3] |
| Main sources of norms (on this page) | MLA reports and style | LSA ethics statements; linguists' principles for data citation |

### Literary studies: from close reading to argument

**Close reading** is a basic method widely used in literary studies. The writing handout from the University of York's Department of English quotes the scholar Showalter: close reading is slow reading, deliberately resisting the spell of the story to notice language, imagery, allusion, intertextuality, syntax, and form[^yorkclosereading-e1]. It is not reading "between the lines" but reading more and more deeply into the words themselves[^yorkclosereading-e2]. In practice:

- **Quote accurately and note the page number.** Some treat accurate quotation as the first rule of close reading[^yorkclosereading-e3]; the handout from the Writing Center at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (UNC) advises noting page numbers when you take notes, so you don't have to hunt through the book again when you cite[^uncwritinglit-e7].
- **Work from the text.** Look at the words in front of you, not at the paraphrase in your head[^yorkclosereading-e8].
- **Look for patterns and problems.** The UNC handout suggests finding a topic in two kinds of places: images, words, forms, or events that recur (patterns)[^uncwritinglit-e12], and places that puzzle you or don't make sense (problems)[^uncwritinglit-e5].

**An interpretation has to be argued.** A literary work has no single right answer, but that doesn't mean anything goes; some interpretations are better than others[^uncwritinglit-e2]. The same handout says a good interpretation avoids obvious conclusions readers could reach on their own[^uncwritinglit-e4], and uses careful reasoning to show how the evidence supports the claim[^uncwritinglit-e3]. Your argument should answer "so what?"[^uncwritinglit-e9] Two practical rules:

- **Quoting without analysis is not argument.** If you quote a passage and don't explain it, you haven't shown your reasoning[^uncwritinglit-e8]. The York handout proposes a "rule of two": your analysis of a quotation should be twice as long as the quotation[^yorkclosereading-e7].
- **Your working thesis will change.** A changing thesis means you are starting to say something more interesting and complex[^uncwritinglit-e6]. This matches the Writing and publishing chapter [Telling the story and writing the paper](../../03_write-and-publish/01_story-and-paper.md#2-build-the-outline-before-writing-sentences), which treats writing as part of the research.

**Using theory and context.** Literary scholars often connect texts to larger issues such as economics, religion, law, politics, history, and the environment[^uncwritinglit-e10]. Literary theories offer angles from which to look at a text, not ready-made conclusions. The UNC handout warns against approaching a topic with a conclusion already in mind[^uncwritinglit-e11]. On the other hand, close reading has itself been criticized for detaching texts from context, away from their historical and political connections[^yorkclosereading-e4]. As the York handout puts it: what's wanted is close reading, not closed reading[^yorkclosereading-e5]; don't list what you find, organize it into an argument[^yorkclosereading-e6].

### Literary studies: archives and scholarly editions

If you study an author's manuscripts, letters, or different editions, you will work in archives. The basics of archival work (read the finding aid first, record box and folder numbers, keep quotation and paraphrase apart in your notes) are covered in [History and archaeology](../history-archaeology/README.md#working-in-an-archive) and not repeated here. One difference: historians mainly treat archives as evidence about the past, while literary scholars often care about how the text itself came into being and changed, for example the revisions between manuscript and print.

A **scholarly edition** (an edition of a work that has been critically established and annotated) is one kind of output in literary studies. The guidelines for editors from the MLA's Committee on Scholarly Editions say the basic task of a scholarly edition is to present a reliable text: to say what it promises, and deliver it[^mlacseguidelines-e1]. It usually has a textual apparatus recording changes to the text and variants (wording that differs between versions)[^mlacseguidelines-e2]; editors must also draw up and carry out a proofreading plan[^mlacseguidelines-e3]. How much a scholarly edition counts toward promotion varies between institutions; if your PhD project centers on editing an edition, first discuss with your advisor how that kind of work is valued where you are.

### Literary studies: monographs, journals, and citation style

The figures below come from an MLA task force that studied how US colleges and universities evaluate scholarship in **tenure** (a permanent appointment from which you cannot be dismissed without cause) **and promotion** reviews; its report came out in 2007. In spring 2005 it ran an online survey of 1,339 departments at 734 US institutions[^mla2007evaluating-e1]. The report itself says the responding departments are not a representative sample of US four-year institutions[^mla2007evaluating-e2]. So the figures reflect only how these US departments viewed tenure review about twenty years ago.

- **The monograph matters.** Among doctorate-granting institutions, 88.9% of responding departments rated publishing a monograph "very important" or "important" for tenure; at master's and baccalaureate institutions the figures were 44.4% and 48%[^mla2007evaluating-e3]. At doctorate-granting institutions, 49.8% of departments required progress on a second book[^mla2007evaluating-e4].
- **But this is fairly recent.** The task force saw the monograph's status as the "gold standard" as a recent development[^mla2007evaluating-e9]; the expectation that a revised dissertation would be published also made it easier for departments to require a book from candidates[^mla2007evaluating-e11].
- **Journal articles are valued too.** Only 1.6% of responding departments considered peer-reviewed journal articles "not important" for tenure and promotion[^mla2007evaluating-e5]. The task force noted that journals have shorter publication cycles than books, which allows timely exchange of ideas[^mla2007evaluating-e6], and that a set of peer-reviewed articles can demonstrate scholarly quality as well as a monograph of similar length, sometimes better[^mla2007evaluating-e8].
- **Conference papers rank low.** The MLA's 2012 guidelines for evaluating digital humanities work say that conference proceedings are among the most prestigious forms of publication in computer science, but in the humanities are generally seen as second-tier[^mla2012digital-e1]. Our sources do not cover review times or author-order conventions; follow your advisor and the requirements of your target journal or press.

**Citation style.** MLA style, set by the Modern Language Association, is widely used in US literary studies; when you submit, follow your target journal's requirements. The free pages of the MLA Style Center explain that you use brief in-text citations in the body that point to a Works Cited list at the end[^mlastylecenter-e1]; when you quote or paraphrase a specific passage, you must give the page numbers, line numbers, or time stamps in parentheses if the work has them[^mlastylecenter-e2]. When the cited work is part of a larger whole, that whole is called the "container"[^mlastylecenter-e3]; for a short story in an anthology, for example, the anthology is the container. For the footnote system many historians use (Chicago style), see [History and archaeology](../history-archaeology/README.md#writing-argument-plus-narrative).

### Linguistics: fieldwork and endangered languages

**Fieldwork** means going to a community that speaks a language and recording and analyzing it together with speakers (also called consultants: the people who provide the language material). A 2018 position statement in linguistics warns that valuable data on endangered languages may be lost[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e14]. A 1992 statement from the Linguistic Society of America (LSA) says that studying a language often depends on long-term relationships with the people who speak it[^lsa1992humansubjects-e2]. The points below come from that statement and the LSA's 2019 ethics statement, and are the positions of this US society:

- **Involve the community from the start.** Bring the community in from the earliest stages of project planning[^lsa2019ethics-e8].
- **Individual consent may not be enough.** In many communities, linguistic and cultural knowledge is regarded as collective, and no individual has the right to consent alone to sharing it with outsiders[^lsa2019ethics-e6]. Access to the materials, archiving, and dissemination of results should respect the community's wishes[^lsa2019ethics-e7].
- **Think about what you give back**: payment, formal acknowledgment of people's contributions, and so on[^lsa2019ethics-e5].
- **Return data to the community.** Wherever possible, return data and knowledge to the community's keeping; data that linguists helped collect do not all belong to the linguists[^lsa2019ethics-e9].
- **Think through the consequences of publishing**, for example whether it could affect the community's social standing or its relationship with the political system[^lsa2019ethics-e10].
- **Named or anonymous: follow the speaker's wishes.** Participants are often proud of their command of their language and may want to be named; withholding their names can do them a disservice[^lsa1992humansubjects-e3]. The LSA's 2019 ethics statement says participants have the right to decide whether recordings can be linked to their identity[^lsa2019ethics-e4]. Put together: ask in advance and do what they choose.

A 2010 LSA resolution says linguistic fieldwork is moving toward collaboration grounded in ethical responsibility to speech communities[^lsa2010documentation-e1], and supports counting grammars, dictionaries, corpora, teaching materials for communities, and the like as scholarly contributions in degree requirements, hiring, tenure, and promotion[^lsa2010documentation-e2][^lsa2010documentation-e3].

For field interviewing techniques, see [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md). The difference: interview studies usually aim to identify themes and often default to anonymity; linguistic fieldwork records the language itself over the long term, the materials are archived for future users, and whether speakers are named depends on their wishes (see above).

### Linguistics: corpora and experiments

**Corpus research** analyzes large amounts of real language material; for the data-processing side, see [Data science](../../06_methods/data_science.md). The difference is that linguistics expects you to say where the data came from so readers can find and check it (see the next section).

**Experimental research** (such as phonetic and psycholinguistic experiments) shares its methods with experimental psychology; see the [part of the Psychology page on the replication crisis](../psychology/README.md#a-replication-crisis-changed-the-field). What follows is one author's view, not a society's rules: in a 2019 article, the linguist Roettger argues that, because of the nature of what it studies, phonetic research leaves analysts many choices, known as "researcher degrees of freedom"[^roettger2019researcher-e1]. His recommendations:

- **Separate exploration from confirmation, and consider preregistration.** You can explore your data, but don't present exploration as testing a hypothesis you held in advance[^roettger2019researcher-e2]; a preregistration is a time-stamped document stating in advance how you will collect data and run the confirmatory analysis[^roettger2019researcher-e3]. For the general practice, see [From research questions to an evidence plan](../../02_do-the-project/01_question-to-evidence.md#5-be-clear-whether-you-are-testing-or-exploring). He also notes that as of 2019, very few quantitative linguistics journals offered Registered Reports (a format in which the study plan is reviewed before the study is done)[^roettger2019researcher-e6].
- **Report every analysis you ran.** If you measured ten parameters, say you measured ten[^roettger2019researcher-e4]. He believes these problems are mostly unintentional[^roettger2019researcher-e5], which is all the more reason to guard against them with rules fixed in advance.

### Linguistics: data citation and reproducibility

In 2018, Berez-Kroeker and colleagues published a position statement on citing language data in the journal *Linguistics*, representing the shared view of about 41 colleagues[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e1]. It distinguishes two ideas: **replicable** means others collect new data and test the result again; **reproducible** means others take your original data and analyze it independently[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e2]. Linguists cannot expect colleagues to recreate the conditions of the original data collection[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e3], so for field data, reproducibility is the more realistic goal.

A survey the statement cites (Gawne and colleagues 2017) found that among 100 descriptive grammars (comprehensive descriptions of a language) published from 2003 to 2012, very few authors stated their methods or data sources[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e4]. In the statement's words, linguistics risks becoming a field of "trust us"[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e5]. It recommends:

- **Say how the data were obtained**: under what conditions they were collected[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e6]; when data come from introspection (judgments by the researcher or others about what sounds acceptable), say so explicitly[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e9].
- **Let readers locate specific data**: page numbers, corpus line numbers, start and end time codes in recordings[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e7], while respecting the privacy of the people recorded[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e8].
- **Deposit in a data archive**, ideally an institution committed to long-term preservation and open access[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e10], in formats that don't depend on proprietary software[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e15]. The statement recommends that every linguist actively build a relationship with an archive[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e13].

Another document, the *Austin Principles of Data Citation in Linguistics* (version 1.0, 2018), sets out principles specifically for citing language data. The main points: cite the data your claims rest on[^berezkroeker2018austin-e1]; state the version of the data[^berezkroeker2018austin-e6]; let readers find the specific data item[^berezkroeker2018austin-e7]; choose repositories that provide persistent identifiers (such as a Digital Object Identifier, DOI)[^berezkroeker2018austin-e5]; and list contributors and their roles[^berezkroeker2018austin-e4]. Data should be as open as possible and restricted only when necessary[^berezkroeker2018austin-e3]; restricted data can still be cited, but only unrestricted data can be reused by others[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e11]. For general practice in organizing data, see [Organizing your research materials](../../08_throughout/organizing_materials.md) in Throughout the project.

## Norms and ethics in this field

For general ethical principles, see [Research integrity and ethics](../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md) in Throughout the project; this section covers only what is specific to this field. **The LSA's 2019 ethics statement** does not replace formal ethics review[^lsa2019ethics-e1]. The points that matter most for newcomers (we chose these items):

- **Exempt from review does not mean exempt from ethics.** Even when an institutional ethics committee decides a project is exempt, linguists should still do their best to follow core principles such as respect for persons[^lsa2019ethics-e2].
- **Informed consent, voluntary throughout**[^lsa2019ethics-e3]. Anonymous observation of people's behavior in public should also receive full review by an ethics committee[^lsa2019ethics-e16].
- **The statement also spells out the general integrity requirements**: no fabrication or falsification of data, no p-hacking (switching analyses until the result comes out "significant")[^lsa2019ethics-e11]; cite original sources[^lsa2019ethics-e12], and fully acknowledge the contributions of students and collaborators[^lsa2019ethics-e14]; promote data sharing and archiving where appropriate and possible[^lsa2019ethics-e13].
- **If you work on language technology, think about the consequences**, including the technology's role in social and political decisions[^lsa2019ethics-e15].

The LSA's 1992 statement arose because some members were concerned that US federal policy on protecting human subjects might not fit linguistic research[^lsa1992humansubjects-e1]; the statement says the circumstances of linguistic research are hard to fit mechanically to general rules[^lsa1992humansubjects-e4]. This is the US situation; ethics review requirements differ in other countries, so follow your institution's rules.

**Literary studies: beware of self-plagiarism.** The MLA Style Center says that reusing your own earlier ideas or sentences in a publication without citing them also counts as plagiarism[^mlastylecenter-e4].

## Common strategies and standards of evidence

A **research strategy** is the main logic by which a project produces new knowledge, decided by your central claim; see [Key concepts](../../00_start-here/key-concepts.md#research-strategy) and [Research strategies](../../05_research-strategies/README.md). The table lists only the three strategies our sources support. The grouping and the "What it looks like in this field" column are our own summary; each standard represents only the source in its footnote (mostly US societies, journals, and university handouts, plus methodological articles by a few scholars), not a rule for the whole field.

| Strategy | What it looks like in this field | Common evidence and standards | How to do it |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Interpretation and argument](../../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md) | Literary studies: first observe facts and details in the text, then interpret those observations[^kain1998closereading-e1][^kain1998closereading-e2]; in work on manuscripts and editions, also how the text came into being | The evidence is the text's language, imagery, syntax, and form[^yorkclosereading-e1]. Interpretations have no single right answer, but some are better than others[^uncwritinglit-e2]: avoid conclusions readers could reach on their own[^uncwritinglit-e4], use reasoning to show how evidence supports the claim[^uncwritinglit-e3], analyze every quotation[^uncwritinglit-e8], and make the argument answer "so what?"[^uncwritinglit-e9]. The basic task of a scholarly edition is to present a reliable text[^mlacseguidelines-e1] | [Close reading and literary argument](practices/close_reading.md), [Archival research](../../06_methods/archival_research.md) |
| [Description](../../05_research-strategies/observe_describe.md) | Linguistics: documenting a language, that is, creating, annotating, preserving, and disseminating a transparent record of it[^good2018scope-e1]; and describing its structure. Himmelmann argues for keeping documentation and description apart as two kinds of work[^riesberg2018adequacy-e2] | State the conditions under which data were collected[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e6]; make example sentences traceable to a page number, corpus line number, or recording time code[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e7]; say explicitly when data are native-speaker judgments[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e9]; deposit data in an archive committed to long-term preservation and open access[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e10] | [Language documentation and linguistic fieldwork](practices/language_documentation.md) |
| [Description](../../05_research-strategies/observe_describe.md) (corpus research) | Linguistics: counting, across a large body of real language material, how often a form occurs and under what conditions; the corpus has to be converted into a machine-readable, searchable form[^rice2018corpora-e1] | Write a data availability statement, credit and cite your data sources, share analysis code; details below the table | [Data science](../../06_methods/data_science.md), and [Corpora and experiments](#linguistics-corpora-and-experiments) above |
| [Causal explanation](../../05_research-strategies/explain_causality.md) (language experiments) | Phonetic and psycholinguistic experiments: change one condition (such as sentence structure) and see whether reading times, pronunciation, etc. change with it | Others can redo the study from the article and supplementary materials[^lsalanguage2026transparency-e6]; fix the sample size in advance; report exploratory and confirmatory analyses separately; details below the table | [Controlled experiments](../../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md), [Psychology page](../psychology/README.md#a-replication-crisis-changed-the-field) |

Notes on the "corpus research" and "language experiments" rows. The standards below come from one journal and a few scholars' articles, not from linguistics as a whole; when you submit, follow your target journal's requirements:

- **Journal requirements.** *Language*, the flagship journal of the Linguistic Society of America (LSA)[^lsalanguage2026transparency-e8], in the instructions for authors we read in October 2026, requires: every manuscript includes a data availability statement saying where and how readers can get the materials supporting the research[^lsalanguage2026transparency-e1]; if the data cannot be made public for ethical or legal reasons, the statement says so[^lsalanguage2026transparency-e3]; data sources are credited, and public data are formally cited[^lsalanguage2026transparency-e5]; sharing analysis code and models is strongly encouraged[^lsalanguage2026transparency-e4]; and articles reporting quantitative analyses or experimental results must allow others to redo the work from the article and supplementary materials[^lsalanguage2026transparency-e6]. Authors who report acceptability judgments (for example, whether a sentence can be said) should be prepared to provide further support if reviewers or editors question them[^lsalanguage2026transparency-e7].
- **Small corpora are useful too.** Rice argues that small, unannotated corpora can still reveal a great deal[^rice2018corpora-e2].
- **Processing details affect reproducibility.** Wieling and colleagues in 2018 surveyed papers from the 2011 and 2016 annual meetings of the Association for Computational Linguistics, picked 10, and tried to reproduce them with the authors' data and code; only 1 matched exactly[^wieling2018reproducibility-e1]. Citing work by Fokkens and colleagues, they note that details such as preprocessing, experimental setup, and software versions all affect whether results can be reproduced exactly[^wieling2018reproducibility-e2]. This was a survey of computational linguistics; applying it to corpus research in general is our own inference.
- **Statistics for experiments.** Vasishth and Nicenboim wrote an article on statistical methods for linguists in 2016 (we read the authors' preprint on arXiv). They recommend checking model assumptions and trying to replicate results[^vasishth2016statistical-e1]; fixing the sample size in advance based on statistical power, rather than checking as you go and stopping once the result is significant[^vasishth2016statistical-e3]. Statistical power is the probability that a study detects an effect when the effect is real. When power is low, a p-value above 0.05 only means "no effect found"[^vasishth2016statistical-e2]. Exploratory analyses are fine, but label them and report them separately from the main analyses[^vasishth2016statistical-e4]. For the advice from phonetics to "report as many parameters as you measured", see [Corpora and experiments](#linguistics-corpora-and-experiments) above.

How to use this table:

- **Start from your central claim to find your strategy**, then read the matching strategy page and how-to page. For projects that span both sides (such as using a corpus to study literary style), the interpretive part follows the standards of literary studies, and the data part those of linguistics.
- **Corpus research and language experiments are also assigned to strategies by their central claim.** If you claim "this usage is more frequent under these conditions and less frequent under those", that is description; if you claim "changing this condition caused this difference", that is causal explanation, and you need to meet the standards for experiments. For specifics, see [Corpora and experiments](#linguistics-corpora-and-experiments) and [Data citation and reproducibility](#linguistics-data-citation-and-reproducibility) above.

## Research methods common in this field

**Research methods specific to this field**:
- [Close reading and literary argument](practices/close_reading.md): read a text closely, propose an interpretation, and argue for it with textual evidence
- [Language documentation and linguistic fieldwork](practices/language_documentation.md): record, transcribe, annotate, and archive a language together with its community

**Cross-field research types you can borrow** (differences in the sections above):
- [Archival research](../../06_methods/archival_research.md): working with manuscripts, letters, and editions
- [Oral history](../../06_methods/oral_history.md): its practices for permissions and archiving are close to those of language documentation
- [Review studies](../../06_methods/review_study.md): when you survey how earlier scholars have interpreted an author or a question, you can borrow the idea of stating how you searched and what you included; literature reviews in literary studies put more weight on positioning your own argument
- [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md), [Data science](../../06_methods/data_science.md), [Controlled experiments](../../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md): for field interviews, corpus data processing, and phonetic and psycholinguistic experiments respectively; for the standards they are judged by, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence) above. More computational work (computational linguistics) follows the conventions of the [Computer science page](../computer-science/README.md)

## Advice for newcomers

1. **First, work out which tradition you are in.** Literary studies and linguistics differ in evidence, outputs, and norms; your advisor and your target journals are the most direct guide.
2. **(Literary studies) Read closely first, then make your argument; use theory to open the text, not to supply the conclusion.** Quote accurately, note page numbers, and find your topic in patterns and problems[^yorkclosereading-e3][^uncwritinglit-e7][^uncwritinglit-e5]; don't come to a topic with a conclusion already in mind[^uncwritinglit-e11], and put close reading back into context[^yorkclosereading-e5].
3. **(Literary studies) Find out early how your institution values monographs, articles, and scholarly editions.** The MLA's 2005 survey of 1,339 US departments showed that doctoral, master's, and baccalaureate institutions differ greatly in how much weight they give the monograph[^mla2007evaluating-e3]; for other countries, ask your advisor first.
4. **(Linguistics) Talk with the community before fieldwork.** Involve the community from the planning stage[^lsa2019ethics-e8], and find out who can consent on the community's behalf and how materials will be archived and used[^lsa2019ethics-e6][^lsa2019ethics-e7].
5. **(Linguistics) Contact a data archive at the start of the project**[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e13], and organize recordings and transcriptions from day one so they can be archived and cited.
6. **(Linguistics) Write your analysis plan before running an experiment**; see [Corpora and experiments](#linguistics-corpora-and-experiments) above.

## Common mistakes

**Literary studies**

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Retelling the plot with no argument | A good interpretation avoids obvious conclusions and uses reasoning to connect evidence and claim[^uncwritinglit-e4][^uncwritinglit-e3] |
| Long quotations with no analysis | Quoting without analysis doesn't show your reasoning[^uncwritinglit-e8]; analysis should be twice the length of the quotation[^yorkclosereading-e7] |
| Misquoting, or paraphrasing from memory | Accurate quotation is the first rule of close reading[^yorkclosereading-e3]; work from the text[^yorkclosereading-e8] |
| Picking a theoretical conclusion first, then hunting the text for examples | Don't come to a topic with a conclusion already in mind[^uncwritinglit-e11] |
| Doing only close reading, ignoring history and context | Close reading has been criticized for detaching texts from context[^yorkclosereading-e4] |
| Citing without page or line numbers | MLA style requires you to give the location[^mlastylecenter-e2] |
| Reusing your own earlier writing without saying so | It also counts as plagiarism[^mlastylecenter-e4] |

**Linguistics**

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Sharing materials on the strength of individual consent alone | In many communities, linguistic knowledge is collective, and no individual can consent alone[^lsa2019ethics-e6] |
| Anonymizing everyone, or naming everyone, without asking | Some speakers want to be named[^lsa1992humansubjects-e3]; others want recordings kept unlinked from their identity[^lsa2019ethics-e4] |
| Assuming that a project exempted by the ethics committee needs no ethical thought | The LSA's 2019 ethics statement holds that exempt projects should still do their best to follow core principles[^lsa2019ethics-e2] |
| Example sentences in the paper can't be traced to a source | Readers can't check them and can only "trust you"[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e5]; give the location[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e7] |
| Not saying that data are your own native-speaker judgments | Introspective data must be stated explicitly[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e9] |
| Data kept only on your own computer | Valuable data on endangered languages may be lost[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e14]; deposit them in an archive[^berezkroeker2018reproducible-e10] |
| Trying many analyses and reporting only the significant one | Roettger argues you should report everything you measured and analyzed[^roettger2019researcher-e4]; the LSA ethics statement also rules out p-hacking[^lsa2019ethics-e11] |

## Further reading

- **University of York, Department of English, *Close reading*** (free web page): what close reading is and how to do it. If you are writing your first literature paper, start here.
- **University of North Carolina Writing Center, *Literature (Fiction)*** (free handout): a step-by-step guide from reading a work to writing an analysis with an argument.
- **MLA Style Center** (style.mla.org): the free official pages on MLA style, covering in-text citations, the Works Cited list, and plagiarism.
- **MLA, *Report of the Task Force on Evaluating Scholarship for Tenure and Promotion*** (2007): based on a nonrepresentative 2005 survey of 1,339 US departments; how US departments weigh monographs, journals, and scholarly editions in tenure review.
- **LSA Ethics Statement** (2019): the Linguistic Society of America's ethics statement on responsibilities to participants, communities, students, and colleagues. Essential reading before fieldwork.
- **Berez-Kroeker et al. 2018, *Reproducible research in linguistics*** (open access) and the **Austin Principles**: why and how to cite language data.
- **Roettger 2019, *Researcher degrees of freedom in phonetic research*** (open access): the author's analysis of, and recommendations on, researcher degrees of freedom in phonetic research. Worth reading before a phonetic or language experiment.
