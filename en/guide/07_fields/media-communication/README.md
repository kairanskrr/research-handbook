# Media and communication: field overview

This page explains how research in media and communication (communication research, for short) actually works: how to do a content analysis, how to report intercoder reliability, how to use online and social media data ethically, how to cope with the fact that platform data is hard to get, and how far open science reform has come in this field. Practices shared by every field (choosing a topic, research design, writing, peer review, research ethics) are covered in the chapters listed in [The research journey at a glance](../../00_start-here/research-journey.md) and in [Throughout the project](../../08_throughout/README.md), and are not repeated here. For the research strategies communication research commonly uses and the evidence each one needs, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence); for specific methods, see [Research methods common in this field](#research-methods-common-in-this-field) at the end.

The norms on this page come mainly from three places: the journals, annual conference, and code of ethics of the International Communication Association (ICA); the internet research ethics guidelines of one scholarly association, the Association of Internet Researchers (AoIR); and one European Union regulation. Each is the rule of one organization in one year. Other countries, associations, and institutions have their own rules; follow your local requirements and your target journal.

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- name the research strategies communication research commonly uses, what evidence each relies on, what standards it is judged by, and where to find the methods;
- run an intercoder reliability test for a content analysis step by step, and report it fully in your paper;
- judge whether a set of online or social media data can be used, and how to use it without harming the people who posted it;
- understand why platform data is hard to get and what compliant routes exist;
- know the basic rules of ICA journals and the ICA conference, and where open science reform in communication stands and what is contested.

## How research works in this field

### A methodologically diverse field

ICA's Open Scholarship page says the field is highly diverse both epistemologically and methodologically[^icaopenscholarship-e1]. The submission page for ICA's 2026 conference notes that ICA has 33 divisions and interest groups[^ica2026conference-e11]. As a beginner you will most often meet four kinds of work: content analysis (coding large amounts of media content, item by item, into data and then running statistics), media effects research (using surveys or experiments to study how media use relates to attitudes and behavior), audience and qualitative research (using interviews, focus groups, and ethnography to understand how people use media), and computational communication research (using code to collect and analyze platform data). For which research strategy each belongs to and what evidence it needs, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence) below.

### Publishing culture: journals and the annual conference

**ICA journals.** According to the Journals FAQ on ICA's website (2025 archive), ICA publishes six peer-reviewed journals: Journal of Communication, Communication Theory, Human Communication Research, Communication, Culture & Critique, Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication, and Annals of the International Communication Association[^icajournalsfaq-e1]. Manuscripts must follow the Publication Manual of the American Psychological Association, 7th edition (APA style), strictly[^icajournalsfaq-e2]. ICA's Open Scholarship page also states that authors must include a Data Availability Statement in the article, saying where the data are and whether they can be accessed[^icaopenscholarship-e5].

**The ICA conference.** ICA holds a conference every year. The table uses the 2026 conference (ICA26) as an example. The rules change every year, so **always go by that year's call for papers**:

| Stage | ICA26 rule |
|---|---|
| Deadline and notification | Submissions due 3 November 2025 at noon (ICA headquarters time, US Eastern Daylight Time); decisions in mid-January of the following year[^ica2026conference-e10] |
| Submission types | Full papers (up to 8,000 words excluding tables and references, unless a division specifies otherwise)[^ica2026conference-e4], works in progress[^ica2026conference-e6], preregistrations[^ica2026conference-e5], and others |
| Exclusive submission | Each submission may go to only one division or interest group, or to the theme sessions[^ica2026conference-e1] |
| Submission limit | Each submitter may have at most three submissions under review as first author[^ica2026conference-e2] |
| Previously published work | Work already published, presented, or accepted must be disclosed in the submission; depending on the division, it may then be ineligible[^ica2026conference-e3] |
| After acceptance | You are obliged to register, attend, and fulfill your assigned role[^ica2026conference-e7] |

The general ICA26 rules are only a floor: divisions can be stricter, so read the call for papers of the division you submit to[^ica2026conference-e9]. ICA26 also has a Research Escalator session where early-career scholars and people with projects in progress get feedback and mentoring[^ica2026conference-e12].

**Authorship and duplicate publication.** The ICA Code of Ethics (2019) requires that the author list accurately reflect each person's contribution[^ica2019ethics-e5]; when you later submit to a journal, you must disclose where the study has appeared before, conference submissions included[^ica2019ethics-e6]. For general practice, see [Collaboration and advisors](../../08_throughout/collaboration_and_advisors.md#6-authorship-discuss-it-at-the-start-and-adjust-as-you-go) in Throughout the project.

Our sources do not cover review times at communication journals, how conference papers and journal articles are weighted in evaluations, or preprint conventions; follow your advisor and your target journal.

### Reproducibility: a reform agenda

For psychology's "replication crisis" and the reforms that followed, see the [psychology page](../psychology/README.md). In 2021, Dienlin and colleagues published an article in Journal of Communication proposing an open science agenda for communication. This is the authors' view, and their reasoning is: communication has not yet run large-scale replication projects, but it uses methods similar to those in fields that have already acknowledged problems, so there is reason to think it has similar problems[^dienlin2021agenda-e2]. The evidence they cite:

- A 2015 study by Matthes and colleagues found evidence of questionable research practices in leading communication journals[^dienlin2021agenda-e3] (for what these are, see [What went wrong](../psychology/README.md#where-it-went-wrong-p-hacking-and-harking) on the psychology page); other studies have documented publication bias, a preference for novel, statistically significant results[^dienlin2021agenda-e15].
- Vermeulen and Hartmann (2015) checked the p-values reported in 693 empirical communication papers from 2010–2012: 8.8% were wrong, and of those, 75% were reported too low[^dienlin2021agenda-e4].
- The authors estimate that a typical communication effect is tested on 167 participants; for most designs, that is not enough to reliably detect small to medium effects[^dienlin2021agenda-e5].
- According to Keating and Totzkay (2019), replications make up about 1.8% of published communication research[^dienlin2021agenda-e6].

They make seven recommendations: publish materials, data, and code; preregister studies and submit Registered Reports; conduct replications; collaborate; build open science skills; implement the Transparency and Openness Promotion (TOP) Guidelines, a set of journal transparency standards; and incentivize open science practices[^dienlin2021agenda-e1]. The materials to publish include content analysis codebooks and data collection code[^dienlin2021agenda-e8]. At the time of writing (around 2020), the communication journals accepting Registered Reports were Communication Research Reports, Computational Communication Research, and Journal of Media Psychology[^dienlin2021agenda-e7]. Check the current list yourself. ICA's Open Scholarship page says ICA supports voluntary open science badges recognizing studies that share data and materials or are preregistered[^icaopenscholarship-e4]; you can also apply for them when submitting to the conference, and the badges are printed in the conference program[^icaopenscholarship-e6].

> **Where sources disagree: does open science fit all of communication research?**
> - **For**: Dienlin and colleagues' recommendations are aimed mainly at confirmatory quantitative research[^dienlin2021agenda-e16]. They argue that qualitative research can also benefit from greater transparency and traceability[^dienlin2021agenda-e12], and can be preregistered too[^dienlin2021agenda-e13].
> - **Against**: ICA's Open Scholarship page describes the debate among communication scholars: whether reproducibility and replicability standards conflict with some qualitative research; the extra workload for preparing and reviewing manuscripts; and the view that open science favors the voices of scholars over the voices of the people studied[^icaopenscholarship-e2]. Critics argue that these practices elevate some forms of research and devalue others[^icaopenscholarship-e3]. ICA uses the term "Open Scholarship" precisely to acknowledge the field's epistemological and methodological diversity[^icaopenscholarship-e1].
>
> In practice: for confirmatory quantitative research (effects experiments, surveys, content analysis), preregister and share materials as described on the psychology page; for qualitative research, at least document your research design, interview guide, and coding strategy clearly. Dienlin and colleagues also say these recommendations are not all-or-nothing; adopting some of them still helps[^dienlin2021agenda-e14].

## Norms and reporting standards in this field

### Content analysis: measure and report intercoder reliability properly

**Intercoder reliability** is the extent to which independent coders, each judging a characteristic of a message, reach the same conclusion[^lombard2010practical-e2]. Reliability does not guarantee validity, but without established reliability the data and their interpretation cannot be considered valid[^lombard2010practical-e3].

Lombard, Snyder-Duch, and Bracken published an online guide to reliability (updated 2010) as a companion to their 2002 paper. The guide reports that the paper examined 200 studies in the mass communication literature from 1994–1998 and found that only 69% (137) reported intercoder reliability[^lombard2010practical-e1]. The steps the guide gives:

1. **Choose your reliability index before you collect data.** It must be decided before data collection[^lombard2010practical-e5].
2. **Don't rely on percent agreement alone.** It does not account for agreement by chance[^mcdonald2019reliability-e4]; if you report it, add an index that does account for chance agreement[^lombard2010practical-e11][^lombard2010practical-e4]. There is no consensus on which index is "best"[^lombard2010practical-e14]. The guide speaks favorably of Krippendorff's alpha (an agreement statistic): it works with multiple coders, handles missing data, and can be used for ordinal and numeric variables[^lombard2010practical-e15].
3. **Don't use correlation coefficients.** Cronbach's alpha and Pearson's correlation only measure whether two coders' scores rise and fall together, not whether their codes are the same[^lombard2010practical-e13].
4. **Set your threshold in advance.** Coefficients of .90 or above are nearly always acceptable, .80 or above is acceptable in most situations, and .70 may be appropriate for some indices in some exploratory studies[^lombard2010practical-e6].
5. **Train first, then pilot test.** In training, code small amounts of content repeatedly (ideally content not in the main sample), and revise the coding instrument and instructions[^lombard2010practical-e16]; a rule of thumb for the pilot test is 30 units[^lombard2010practical-e8]. During reliability testing, coders must work independently without discussing; if possible, the researcher should not be one of the coders[^lombard2010practical-e7].
6. **Make the formal test sample big enough.** At least 50 units or 10% of the full sample, and rarely more than 300 are needed[^lombard2010practical-e9].
7. **Report each variable separately.** Don't report an overall reliability pooled across all variables[^lombard2010practical-e12]. At a minimum, your paper should state the size of the reliability sample, how it was selected and why, the reliability for each variable on each index, and how disagreements were resolved[^lombard2010practical-e10].

**Should qualitative research calculate reliability?** This is contested. McDonald and colleagues (2019) argue that how you demonstrate reliability should fit the epistemological tradition your research draws on[^mcdonald2019reliability-e9]; if your findings rely on frequency counts, there is a strong case for calculating it[^mcdonald2019reliability-e12]. For both sides' arguments and how to do it, see [Interview study](../../06_methods/interview_study.md#step-6-make-your-analysis-trustworthy) in Research types.

### Ethics of online and social media research

The ICA Code of Ethics (2019) requirements on informed consent[^ica2019ethics-e2], ethics review boards[^ica2019ethics-e3], and protecting vulnerable populations[^ica2019ethics-e7] match principles shared across fields; see [Research integrity and ethics](../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#4-when-research-involves-people-or-could-harm-others-protect-people-first). The point that matters most for communication data: support open data, but guard against anonymized data being re-identified[^ica2019ethics-e4]. The code also says ethical requirements must take account of the different institutional, legal, and cultural settings in different countries[^ica2019ethics-e1].

When you use online data, you can consult AoIR's Internet Research: Ethical Guidelines 3.0 (published 2020). The guidelines stress that they are guidelines, not a recipe you can just follow; you have to use your own judgment[^franzke2020internet-e2]. Key points:

- **"Public" does not mean "free to use."** People who post in public or semi-public online spaces often assume their exchanges are private; technically they are not, but that expectation should be respected[^franzke2020internet-e3]. Consent may also need to be sought from the community and the site's administrators[^franzke2020internet-e11].
- **In big data projects, getting consent from each person is impossible**, which is a serious dilemma[^franzke2020internet-e4]. The guidelines say the most common approach is pseudonymization: store the key that links pseudonyms to identities separately from the data, and explain how risks will be handled (for example, by publishing only aggregate results)[^franzke2020internet-e5].
- **Be careful when quoting posts verbatim.** Someone who searches for the exact words may find the person who posted them. One option is to ask the people you will quote for consent at the publication stage[^franzke2020internet-e6]; you can also paraphrase quotes or combine several people's words into a composite example[^franzke2020internet-e10].
- **De-identification is not anonymization.** Even the best current techniques can only de-identify data, not anonymize it perfectly, so data security matters all the more[^franzke2020internet-e7]. De-identification methods mentioned by Dienlin and colleagues: delete direct and indirect identifiers (such as date of birth), bin continuous values into groups, and aggregate[^dienlin2021agenda-e10].
- **Big data has representativeness problems too.** Social media users are not the same as the general population[^franzke2020internet-e12].

**Can the data be shared?** Dienlin and colleagues note that news coverage, videos, and data scraped from platforms sometimes cannot be shared for copyright and similar reasons[^dienlin2021agenda-e9]. One compromise is "non-consumptive use": others can access the data but not copy it out, for example by working with it on site[^dienlin2021agenda-e11]. The ICA Code of Ethics recognizes fair use: copyrighted material may be used without permission when the social and cultural benefit outweighs the cost to the copyright holder; but it also requires members to follow their own country's rules on fair use[^ica2019ethics-e8].

### Platform data: can you get it, and can you use it

Computational communication research depends on platforms' data, and platforms can change their rules at any time. In 2018 Freelon published a short essay on this in Political Communication. He writes that after Facebook shut down the API (application programming interface, the official channel for programs to fetch data) for public pages, there was no longer any way to get Facebook content that complied with the terms of service[^freelon2018computational-e1]; companies can restrict or revoke API access at any time for any reason, and researchers have no recourse[^freelon2018computational-e2]. The essay's recommendations (the author's views):

- **Use officially sanctioned routes first.** Before scraping a site that prohibits scraping, exhaust every officially sanctioned way of getting the data[^freelon2018computational-e4]. For example, the Twitter API at the time was very restrictive for historical data but more generous for real-time collection, so you could set up a collection pipeline in advance and start collecting as soon as an event happened[^freelon2018computational-e7].
- **Complying with the terms of service is not the same as being ethical.** Terms of service exist to protect the company[^freelon2018computational-e5]; following them respects the company's commercial rights, not the dignity and privacy of users[^freelon2018computational-e6].
- **Think hard about the risks of violating the terms**, even on a small scale[^freelon2018computational-e8].

Terms of service also constrain how data can be stored and shared. AoIR's guidelines give examples: under Twitter's terms at the time, researchers had to keep checking which tweets had been deleted and remove them from their own datasets[^franzke2020internet-e8]; Facebook and LinkedIn prohibit automated scraping[^franzke2020internet-e9]. When Freelon was writing, Twitter allowed sharing only tweet IDs, from which others could re-fetch the tweets that had not been deleted[^freelon2018computational-e9]. These specific rules have changed many times, so **always check the platform's current terms before you use its data**.

**The EU route (EU only).** Article 40 of the European Union's 2022 Digital Services Act (DSA) requires platforms to give "vetted researchers" access to data, solely for research into systemic risks in the EU[^eu2022dsa-e1]; "platforms" here means very large online platforms and very large online search engines[^eu2022dsa-e4]. Conditions include being affiliated with a research organization, being independent of commercial interests, disclosing your funding, and committing to make research results publicly available free of charge[^eu2022dsa-e2]. Paragraph 12 also requires access for researchers to data that is already publicly accessible in the platform's interface[^eu2022dsa-e3]. The application procedure is set by the European Commission's 2025 Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/2050[^eu2025dsadata-e1]: first open an account on the Commission's data access portal[^eu2025dsadata-e3], then submit your application to the authority in the relevant member state responsible for enforcing the Digital Services Act (the Digital Services Coordinator)[^eu2022dsa-e6][^eu2025dsadata-e4]. For the exact steps, go by the European Commission's current pages.

Mining software repositories raises similar issues (the data was left by people, and platforms have terms of use); compare [Mining software repositories](../computer-science/practices/repository_mining.md) on the computer science page. The difference is that social media data often touches ordinary people's private lives and sensitive topics, so the ethical risk may be higher.

## Common strategies and standards of evidence

A **research strategy** is the main logic by which a project produces new knowledge; see [Key concepts](../../00_start-here/key-concepts.md#research-strategy) and [Research strategies](../../05_research-strategies/README.md). The table lists only the cases this page's sources support (our sources do not cover how measurement, comparative evaluation, synthesis, interpretation and argument, and so on are done in communication research or what evidence they need); the grouping and the "What it looks like in communication research" column are our own synthesis, and each standard speaks only for the source in its footnote.

| Strategy | What it looks like in communication research | Common evidence and standards | How to do it |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Description](../../05_research-strategies/observe_describe.md) (quantitative) | Content analysis: coding large numbers of news stories, ads, or posts with a codebook and then running statistics; computational communication research: collecting and analyzing platform data with code | Without properly established intercoder reliability, the data and their interpretation cannot be considered valid[^lombard2010practical-e3]; coefficients of .80 or above are acceptable in most situations[^lombard2010practical-e6]; report reliability for each variable, and state the reliability sample and how disagreements were resolved[^lombard2010practical-e10]; social media data has representativeness problems compared with the general population[^franzke2020internet-e12] | [Content analysis: measure and report intercoder reliability properly](#content-analysis-measure-and-report-intercoder-reliability-properly) above, [Data science](../../06_methods/data_science.md) |
| [Description](../../05_research-strategies/observe_describe.md) (qualitative) | Audience research: using interviews, focus groups, and ethnography to understand how people use and make sense of media | How you demonstrate reliability should fit the epistemological tradition the research draws on[^mcdonald2019reliability-e9]; quality comes from greater transparency and traceability[^dienlin2021agenda-e12]; when observing online communities, respect posters' expectations of privacy[^franzke2020internet-e3] | [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md), [Case study and ethnography](../../06_methods/case_study.md) |
| [Causal explanation](../../05_research-strategies/explain_causality.md) | Media effects research: using experiments or surveys to see how media use affects attitudes and behavior | Samples must be large enough: by the authors' estimate, a typical communication effect is tested on 167 participants, which for most designs is not enough to reliably detect small to medium effects[^dienlin2021agenda-e5]; confirmatory studies can be preregistered, with materials, data, and code made public[^dienlin2021agenda-e1]; replications are only about 1.8% of published research[^dienlin2021agenda-e6] | [Controlled experiments](../../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md), [Questionnaire surveys](../../06_methods/questionnaire_survey.md), [Replication studies](../../06_methods/replication.md) |

How to use this table:

- **Find your strategy from your central claim.** Say you first use content analysis to describe how something is covered, then run an experiment to test its effect on readers. Pick the main strategy by the one sentence you most want to prove, and hold the other parts to their own standards.
- **Different methods, different standards.** Don't hold all qualitative research to content analysis reliability standards, and don't judge effects experiments by qualitative standards; for the field's debates on this, see "intercoder reliability" and "open science" above.

## Research methods common in this field

Communication research does not yet have its own methods pages; the common methods are all in the [Research types](../../06_methods/README.md) section. Those pages were originally written for computer science (mainly software engineering), so watch for the differences below:

- **Content analysis**: this handbook does not yet have a page on it. For the manual coding part, see [Mining software repositories](../computer-science/practices/repository_mining.md) on the computer science page; test and report reliability following the steps above, and publish your codebook[^dienlin2021agenda-e8].
- **[Questionnaire surveys](../../06_methods/questionnaire_survey.md)**: used in media use and effects research. For measuring abstract concepts such as attitudes, see [Scale development and measurement studies](../../06_methods/scale_development.md) and the [psychology page](../psychology/README.md).
- **[Controlled experiments](../../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md)**: that page compares how tools affect people; here you study how media content affects attitudes and behavior. For preregistration and sample size, see the psychology page.
- **[Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md), [Case study and ethnography](../../06_methods/case_study.md)**: used in audience research and research on online communities; for whether qualitative research should calculate reliability, see above.
- **[Data science](../../06_methods/data_science.md)**: used in computational communication research; for how to get platform data and whether it can be shared, see above. [Replication studies](../../06_methods/replication.md) are rare in communication research, and Dienlin and colleagues call for more of them[^dienlin2021agenda-e1].

## Advice for newcomers

1. **For a content analysis, write the codebook first, then choose your reliability index, then train and pilot test, and finally report reliability fully in your paper.** Fix the index before collecting data[^lombard2010practical-e5], and don't rely on percent agreement alone[^lombard2010practical-e11]; state how the reliability sample was drawn, the reliability for each variable, and how disagreements were resolved[^lombard2010practical-e10].
2. **Before using online data, read the AoIR ethics guidelines and talk to your institution's ethics review board.** Publicly visible does not mean free to use[^franzke2020internet-e3].
3. **Don't quote searchable posts verbatim in your paper**; either get consent or paraphrase[^franzke2020internet-e6][^franzke2020internet-e10].
4. **For computational research, check the platform's current terms and data access routes first**, and prefer officially sanctioned routes[^freelon2018computational-e4]; don't assume that following the terms makes your work ethical[^freelon2018computational-e6].
5. **For confirmatory quantitative research, consider preregistering, and share your materials, code, and codebook**. At ICA26, for example, you could submit to the conference as a preregistration[^ica2026conference-e5] and apply for open science badges[^icaopenscholarship-e6].
6. **Before submitting to the ICA conference, read two calls**: ICA's general submission guidelines for that year and the call for papers of the division you are submitting to[^ica2026conference-e9].

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Having only one person code a content analysis, or not reporting reliability | Without established reliability, the data and their interpretation cannot be considered valid[^lombard2010practical-e3] |
| Requiring every qualitative study to calculate intercoder reliability | How you demonstrate reliability should fit the study's epistemological tradition[^mcdonald2019reliability-e9] |
| Deleting usernames and calling the data "anonymized" | Data can only be de-identified, not perfectly anonymized[^franzke2020internet-e7]; a quick search on a verbatim quote can find the person[^franzke2020internet-e6] |
| Publishing a complete dataset scraped from a platform | It may violate copyright or the platform's terms[^dienlin2021agenda-e9][^franzke2020internet-e8] |
| Building the whole research design on one platform's API | The API could be shut down at any time[^freelon2018computational-e2] |
| Generalizing findings about social media users to the whole population | Social media datasets have representativeness problems[^franzke2020internet-e12] |
| Submitting the same paper to two ICA divisions at once | It breaks the exclusive-submission rule of conferences such as ICA26[^ica2026conference-e1] |
| Not disclosing, when submitting to a journal, that the study was presented at the conference | The ICA Code of Ethics requires disclosing where the study has appeared before[^ica2019ethics-e6] |

## Further reading

The reasons given for each recommendation are our own.

- **AoIR, Internet Research: Ethical Guidelines 3.0 (2020)**: the Association of Internet Researchers' guidelines for internet research ethics, organized as questions to consider at each stage of a study. Read before using online and social media data.
- **Lombard, Snyder-Duch, and Bracken's online guide to intercoder reliability (updated 2010)**: step-by-step guidance on testing and reporting reliability in content analysis. Read before doing a content analysis.
- **Dienlin et al. 2021, An Agenda for Open Science in Communication**: the authors' open science agenda for communication, with the evidence on replicability problems, seven recommendations, and a discussion of qualitative research and common objections.
- **The ICA Code of Ethics (2019) and ICA's Open Scholarship page**: ICA's ethical requirements, the Data Availability Statement, and open science badges.
- **Freelon 2018, Computational Research in the Post-API Age**: short; on weighing scraping against the terms of service after platform APIs close.
- **McDonald et al. 2019**: when qualitative research should calculate intercoder reliability.
- **The calls for papers of the ICA conference and the division you are submitting to**: reread them every year.
