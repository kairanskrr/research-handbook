# Law: field overview

This page explains how legal research actually works: the main kinds of legal research, what "doctrinal research" really involves, how to find primary and secondary legal sources and check that they are still good law, citation rules, and publishing practices that differ sharply from country to country. Practices shared by every field (choosing a topic, reading the literature, writing, research ethics) are covered in the Parts listed in [The research journey at a glance](../../00_start-here/research-journey.md) and in [Throughout the project](../../08_throughout/README.md), and are not repeated here. For which research strategies legal research uses and what evidence each one needs, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence).

**The most important caveat first: law is tied to a particular country and legal system.** Our sources come from the US, the UK, and Australia: US law reviews, the UK citation standard and a UK society's ethics principles, and articles by two scholars working in Australia and the UK. So whenever this page describes a practice, it says **which country and which institution** it comes from. These are not worldwide rules. For your own legal system, citation conventions, journal practices, and ethics review, **follow the rules of your own country, your university, and your target journal** `[Our analysis]`.

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- name the main kinds of legal research, and say what doctrinal research contributes;
- tell primary from secondary legal sources, and know how to check that a case or statute is still good law;
- know the main differences between the US and the UK in citation and publishing;
- avoid the most common mistakes when doing empirical legal research or interviewing judges and lawyers.

## How research works in this field

### The main kinds of legal research

Hutchinson and Duncan, two scholars teaching in Australia and the UK, cite several reports' classification of legal research in a 2012 article (each definition in the table comes from those reports):

| Kind | What it does |
|---|---|
| Doctrinal research (also called black-letter research) | Gives a systematic exposition of the rules governing a particular area of law, analyzes the relationships between rules, explains areas of difficulty, and perhaps predicts future developments[^hutchinson2012defining-e1] |
| Reform-oriented research | Evaluates whether existing rules are adequate, and recommends changes to rules found wanting[^hutchinson2012defining-e2] |
| Theoretical research | Deepens understanding of the conceptual bases of legal principles[^hutchinson2012defining-e3] |
| Fundamental research | Studies law as a social phenomenon, including its historical, philosophical, linguistic, economic, social, and political implications[^hutchinson2012defining-e4]; socio-legal studies (research on how law actually operates in society) belongs roughly here `[Our analysis]` |

All of these reports agree that **doctrinal research is the most accepted methodology in law**[^hutchinson2012defining-e5]. This page treats two other kinds separately:

- **Empirical legal studies**: learning about the world using quantitative data or qualitative information[^epstein2002rules-e1], for example counting case outcomes or interviewing lawyers. Epstein and King, two US scholars, argue that a large share of legal scholarship makes at least some claims about the world based on observation or experience[^epstein2002rules-e15]. So even if your project is doctrinal, any claim in it about the real world (for example "this rule has led to …") has to meet empirical standards `[Our analysis]`.
- **Comparative and historical research**: studying another legal system or the law of another era `[Our analysis]`; Minow's list of contributions legal scholarship can make (see the next section) gives it its own category[^hutchinson2012defining-e21].

### Doctrinal research: first locate the law, then interpret it

Doctrinal research is normally a two-step process[^hutchinson2012defining-e6]:

1. **Locate the sources of law**, such as the relevant legislation and case law. Other researchers can easily replicate this step[^hutchinson2012defining-e30].
2. **Interpret and analyze the text**, reading the rules in their specific context. The outcome of this step depends on the researcher's own expertise, views, and methods[^hutchinson2012defining-e7], and no one else can replicate it exactly[^hutchinson2012defining-e14].

**Doctrinal research is more than a literature review.** Every project starts by reading secondary literature; the core of doctrinal research is locating and analyzing the primary documents of the law to establish what the law is and where its boundaries lie[^hutchinson2012defining-e8][^hutchinson2012defining-e9]. The crucial step is "reading, analysing and linking" new information to the known body of law[^hutchinson2012defining-e10].

**What does doctrinal research contribute?** The scholar Minow drew up a list of the contributions legal scholarship can make, which Hutchinson and Duncan cite. The ones most directly related to doctrinal research:

- **Restatement**: organizing case law into coherent elements, categories, and concepts[^hutchinson2012defining-e27], and distinguishing settled law from emerging law[^hutchinson2012defining-e22];
- **Recombination**: putting together several lines of cases from different areas of law and showing why they belong together, or exposing unjustified discrepancies among them[^hutchinson2012defining-e23];
- the list also includes policy analysis[^hutchinson2012defining-e28], comparative and historical inquiry[^hutchinson2012defining-e21], and jurisprudence and philosophy of law[^hutchinson2012defining-e29].

**How practitioners and scholars differ.** Practicing lawyers and students use problem-based doctrinal research[^hutchinson2012defining-e19]. Academics use a similar method, except that they do not have to find a specific answer for a client, and can choose the topic and the breadth and depth of the investigation themselves[^hutchinson2012defining-e20]. So **simply describing the current law is usually not enough to count as a scholarly contribution**; say what new view you reached by organizing, comparing, or critiquing it `[Our analysis]`.

**Write your method down.** The method of doctrinal research is often left implicit, and many lawyers think there is no need to spell it out[^hutchinson2012defining-e15]. Hutchinson and Duncan looked at 60 research theses in law, and only 38 of them (63.3%) discussed methodology[^hutchinson2012defining-e16]. They recommend at least one sentence, for example "This study will include a doctrinal analysis of legislation and case law"[^hutchinson2012defining-e17], and that the research be "systematic and purposive with a robust framework"[^hutchinson2012defining-e18]. This matters most in research proposals and grant applications `[Our analysis]`.

### Primary sources, secondary sources, and "is this still good law?"

**Primary sources** are the law itself, such as legislation and case law `[Our analysis]`; **secondary sources** are commentary on the law: textbooks, journal articles, government reports, policy documents, law reform documents, and media reports[^hutchinson2012defining-e11]. The UK citation standard OSCOLA says that when you cite primary and secondary sources for the same point, **cite the primary source first**[^goudkamp2026oscola-e4]. A textbook saying "the law is X" is no substitute for reading the statute and the judgment yourself `[Our analysis]`.

**Not the same as "primary sources" in history.** Historians treat primary sources as evidence of facts; legal researchers read primary sources to draw conclusions about what the law is[^hutchinson2012defining-e12]. Doctrinal research focuses mostly on "privileged voices"[^hutchinson2012defining-e13]: legislation and judgments carry weight because of the parliament or court that made them[^hutchinson2012defining-e31]. For how historians scrutinize a source (who wrote it, and why), see the [History and archaeology page](../history-archaeology/README.md); the same methods apply when you work on legal history or study the legislative process `[Our analysis]`.

**The law changes, so check every time that it is still good law:**

- **US cases and statutes**: use a citator to see what has happened to a case or statute since it was issued and who has cited it[^northwesterngoodlaw-e1]. The Northwestern University law library guide says this is always the last step of legal research, and a mandatory one[^northwesterngoodlaw-e2] (the guide is written for lawyers appearing in court, but the same applies to writing a paper `[Our analysis]`). Don't rely on the tool's colored flags alone: a red flag does not necessarily mean the case is no longer good law[^northwesterngoodlaw-e3], so always read the citing decisions in context[^northwesterngoodlaw-e4].
- **UK legislation**: on legislation.gov.uk, the UK's official legislation website, revised versions of legislation show whether there are changes not yet incorporated[^legislationgovukhelp-e1]; the site aims to incorporate changes **within three months** of their coming into force[^legislationgovukhelp-e2]. So the text you see online may temporarily lag behind the law in force `[Our analysis]`.
- **Other countries**: our sources do not cover them; ask your university's law librarian or your advisor `[Our analysis]`.

### Citation: the footnotes are part of the argument

Legal citation rules are numerous and detailed, and **they differ from country to country**:

- **UK**: OSCOLA (the Oxford University Standard for the Citation of Legal Authorities). According to the OSCOLA website, it is widely used by law schools, journals, and publishers in the UK and beyond[^goudkamp2026oscola-e9]. This page uses the 5th edition of 2026 (a free PDF is on the Oxford Law Faculty website). It sets out two golden rules: consistency, and consideration for the reader[^goudkamp2026oscola-e1]. It is a footnote style, with sources cited in footnotes[^goudkamp2026oscola-e2]; when citing a case, you must give a page or paragraph number (a "pinpoint") unless you are referring to the case as a whole[^goudkamp2026oscola-e3]; and the 5th edition no longer uses "ibid"[^goudkamp2026oscola-e5].
- **US**: the *Yale Law Journal*, for example, requires quotation marks around anything quoted verbatim, and citations that conform to *The Bluebook* (the standard US legal citation manual)[^yljsubmissions-e1]. We have not read *The Bluebook* itself; check its specific rules with your library `[Our analysis]`.
- **Citing foreign law**: OSCOLA asks you to cite foreign primary sources the way they are cited in their own jurisdiction[^goudkamp2026oscola-e6]. Keep this in mind in comparative work `[Our analysis]`.

No citation standard can cover every case; OSCOLA itself says it does not set a rule for every citation problem[^goudkamp2026oscola-e8]. In practice: **ask which standard your target journal or university uses, then use only that one, from start to finish** `[Our analysis]`.

### Publishing: the US is very different from elsewhere

**US: student-edited law reviews, and simultaneous submission.** The publishing guide of the Legal Writing Institute (LWI, the US professional organization of legal writing teachers) says there are hundreds of law reviews in the US, most of them run by students[^lwipublishingtips-e1]. Epstein and King describe the same thing: each US law school's flagship journal is run and edited by students, who often consult faculty informally before deciding on an article[^epstein2002rules-e19]. The table below draws on the LWI guide and the submission guidelines of the *University of Chicago Law Review* and the *Yale Law Journal*, and **applies only to US law reviews**:

| Stage | Practice (source) |
|---|---|
| Submission | LWI: you may submit one article to several law reviews at once[^lwipublishingtips-e2]; the vast majority of journals do not require exclusive submission[^lwipublishingtips-e4], though a few do, such as the *Journal of Legal Education*[^lwipublishingtips-e3]. The two most common online submission systems are ExpressO and Scholastica[^lwipublishingtips-e10] |
| Expedited review | Chicago: if you have an offer from another journal, you can tell it and ask for an expedited review[^uclrsubmissions-e3]. LWI: many top journals may not read a manuscript until they receive an expedite request[^lwipublishingtips-e5] |
| Time to decide | Yale: in 2011 it and several peer journals jointly committed to giving every author at least 7 days to decide whether to accept an offer[^yljsubmissions-e4] |
| Closing out | LWI: when you have several offers, accept one and decline the rest[^lwipublishingtips-e6]; from acceptance to publication can take months, or in extreme cases a year or two[^lwipublishingtips-e8] |
| Review | Yale: the committees that review Articles and Essays do not know who the author is[^yljsubmissions-e3]. Chicago: sometimes asks experts in the field to comment on submissions[^uclrsubmissions-e2] |

Epstein and King criticize this system: some of the most prestigious outlets in US legal scholarship use no form of blind peer review at all, and leave refereeing to law students[^epstein2002rules-e10]. That is the two authors' view; our sources do not cover how US legal academia as a whole regards it.

Note: **simultaneous submission is a convention of US law reviews only**. Many journals in other fields treat duplicate submission as misconduct; always read your target journal's rules before you submit `[Our analysis]`.

**SSRN's Legal Scholarship Network.** The Legal Scholarship Network of SSRN (the Social Science Research Network) is an open-access preprint platform for spreading working papers and early-stage research quickly[^ssrnlsn-e1]. The LWI guide says that in a cover letter to a US law review you can mention your SSRN downloads (if there are many)[^lwipublishingtips-e7]. Economics also has a habit of circulating working papers first; see the [Economics page](../economics/README.md).

**UK and Australia: more weight on peer-reviewed journals.** Hutchinson and Duncan's article is mainly about Australia and the UK. They say academics are being directed toward peer-reviewed journals with academic audiences, rather than journals aimed at practitioners[^hutchinson2012defining-e26]. They also point out that the quality of legal research is judged not only by citations from other researchers but also by whether courts or law reform bodies refer to it[^hutchinson2012defining-e24]. Our sources do not cover the standing of monographs in legal academia in different countries; follow your advisor's guidance or your institution's evaluation rules.

**Working alone or in a team.** The same article says the old model in law schools was the individual researcher working alone; there is now more emphasis than before on team, interdisciplinary, and empirical research[^hutchinson2012defining-e25]. Our sources do not cover authorship conventions in different countries; follow your advisor's guidance or your target journal's requirements.

## Norms and reporting standards in this field

### Empirical legal studies: Epstein and King's "rules of inference"

"Rules of inference" are the rules you follow when drawing conclusions from data `[Our analysis]`. In 2002, two US scholars, Epstein and King, published a long article in the *University of Chicago Law Review* on empirical research in US law reviews. They read 231 articles published in US law reviews between 1990 and 2000[^epstein2002rules-e2], and say every one they read violated at least one of the rules they discuss[^epstein2002rules-e3]. What follows are **the two authors' claims**, about the situation in the US more than 20 years ago; our sources do not say whether legal scholars generally accept them. In our view these recommendations match what other fields require of empirical research, and are worth following `[Our analysis]`:

- **Don't treat your hypothesis like a lawyer.** They say PhD training teaches you to subject your favored hypothesis to every possible test, while legal training teaches you to amass only the evidence for your side[^epstein2002rules-e4]. In research, expect every rival explanation you have not explored to be tested[^epstein2002rules-e13].
- **Say which cases you chose and how.** First identify the population of interest, collect as much data as feasible, record how the data came to be observed, and avoid selection bias[^epstein2002rules-e6]. Of one study that picked only a few cases, they comment that its conclusion is "possible", but possible is not the same as conclusive[^epstein2002rules-e11]. Looking only at published opinions can also bias results: in one example they give, conclusions drawn only from published opinions will be biased in a predictable direction[^epstein2002rules-e7].
- **Write your coding rules down clearly.** Remove human judgment from measurement as far as possible; where judgment is needed, state the rules so that others can follow them completely[^epstein2002rules-e14].
- **Report uncertainty.** This does not mean adding "fairly certain" to every sentence; it means estimating how uncertain each conclusion is and reporting that estimate with the conclusion[^epstein2002rules-e8]. In qualitative work, you can identify the weakest link in your chain of reasoning: the part that rests on the least evidence or is most open to attack[^epstein2002rules-e9].
- **Let others replicate your work.** Another researcher should be able to understand, evaluate, and reproduce your research without asking you for more information[^epstein2002rules-e5]. Studies that code cases should make public their data, their complete coding rules, and which case each number corresponds to[^epstein2002rules-e16]. Some US journals now require this: the *Yale Law Journal* publishes only empirical articles whose data and code the editors can access throughout the process[^yljsubmissions-e6], and authors who cannot share them must submit a request for an exemption explaining why[^yljsubmissions-e7].

For the general approach, see the Part 2 chapter [Validity and limitations](../../02_do-the-project/04_validity-and-limits.md).

### Interviewing judges and lawyers, and using confidential documents

The points below come from the research ethics principles of the **UK** Socio-Legal Studies Association (SLSA, the UK organization of scholars who study how law works in society), revised in 2021. The principles do not impose a single model of ethics[^slsa2021ethics-e1], and the association does not itself grant ethics approval[^slsa2021ethics-e2], so **approval still has to go through your own institution's ethics review**; in other countries, follow your own country's and university's rules `[Our analysis]`. The points most relevant to legal research:

- **Even when a gatekeeper lets you in, get each person's own consent.** Even if someone with authority to grant access agrees (for example the head of a court or law firm `[Our analysis]`), you still need informed consent directly from each participant[^slsa2021ethics-e3].
- **Keep confidences, but don't over-promise.** What participants say must be kept confidential, and identifiable statements must not be attributed to them without permission[^slsa2021ethics-e4]; but do not give unrealistic guarantees of confidentiality[^slsa2021ethics-e5]. When a combination of a participant's characteristics makes them easy to identify, it is hard to conceal their identity without distorting the data[^slsa2021ethics-e7]; with judges and senior lawyers, who are few in number, this is often the case `[Our analysis]`. When anonymity is hard to achieve, tell participants in advance[^slsa2021ethics-e8].
- **Know the legal limits of confidentiality.** Researchers may be required to give evidence in court or hand over documents[^slsa2021ethics-e10]; for information about serious crimes, there may in some circumstances be a legal duty to disclose[^slsa2021ethics-e6]. Find out the rules in the relevant jurisdiction beforehand[^slsa2021ethics-e9].
- **Scrutiny of power.** When those being studied abuse their power, obligations such as protecting anonymity may carry less weight[^slsa2021ethics-e11], but they should not be set aside lightly[^slsa2021ethics-e12].
- **Data security.** Keep data secure throughout the research, and manage it according to your institution's data management policy[^slsa2021ethics-e13].

Interviews also raise a methodological problem. Epstein and King argue that **asking people about their motives is one of the worst ways of measuring motive**[^epstein2002rules-e17]. Their example: if you ask US Supreme Court justices whether their political preferences affect their votes, the justices will say no, that they vote on the basis of neutral principles[^epstein2002rules-e20]. They argue that it is usually better to look at what can be observed directly in real behavior[^epstein2002rules-e18].

For general ethical principles, see [Research integrity and ethics](../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#4-when-research-involves-people-or-could-harm-others-protect-people-first) in Throughout the project.

## Common strategies and standards of evidence

A **research strategy** is the main logic by which a project produces new knowledge; see [Key concepts](../../00_start-here/key-concepts.md#research-strategy) and [Research strategies](../../05_research-strategies/README.md). The table lists only the strategies our sources support; the grouping and summaries are ours, and each standard speaks only for the country and author in its footnote `[Our analysis]`.

| Strategy | What it looks like in law | Common evidence and standards | How-to page |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Interpretation and argument](../../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md) (doctrinal research) | A systematic exposition of the rules in an area of law, analyzing the relationships between rules and explaining areas of difficulty[^hutchinson2012defining-e1]; first locate the sources of law, then interpret and analyze the text[^hutchinson2012defining-e6] | The core is analysis of primary legal sources, not just a literature review[^hutchinson2012defining-e9]; before submitting, check the law is still good law (US)[^northwesterngoodlaw-e2]; at least one sentence on method[^hutchinson2012defining-e17] | [Doctrinal legal research](practices/doctrinal_research.md) |
| [Interpretation and argument](../../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md) (reform-oriented research) | Evaluates whether existing rules are adequate, and recommends changes to rules found wanting[^hutchinson2012defining-e2] | Present the problem, canvass the alternatives, propose evaluative criteria, recommend a preferred solution[^hutchinson2012defining-e36]; when judging which approach is "better", say what criteria you used[^hutchinson2012defining-e34]; quality is also judged by whether courts or law reform bodies cite it[^hutchinson2012defining-e24] | [Doctrinal legal research: evaluating the current law](practices/doctrinal_research.md#step-7-evaluate-the-current-law-and-propose-reform) |

| [Interpretation and argument](../../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md) (legal history) | Works out where today's law came from and why it is the way it is[^vanhoecke2015methodology-e9] | Reflect on the concept of "law" you are using; handle historical sources rigorously; see below the table | [Archival research](../../06_methods/archival_research.md), [History and archaeology page](../history-archaeology/README.md) |

| [Description](../../05_research-strategies/observe_describe.md), [Causal explanation](../../05_research-strategies/explain_causality.md) (empirical legal studies) | Learning about the world using quantitative data or qualitative information[^epstein2002rules-e1], for example counting case outcomes or interviewing lawyers | Epstein and King argue: identify the population of interest first and avoid selection bias[^epstein2002rules-e6]; state coding rules so others can follow them completely[^epstein2002rules-e14]; report how uncertain each conclusion is[^epstein2002rules-e8]; others should be able to redo the study without asking you for information[^epstein2002rules-e5]. See [rules of inference](#empirical-legal-studies-epstein-and-kings-rules-of-inference) below | [Data science research](../../06_methods/data_science.md), [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md) |

Notes on the comparative law, legal history, and legal philosophy rows. The standards in each of these rows come from a single source; they are the author's claims, not agreed norms in these areas:

- **Comparative law**, based on a 2015 article by Van Hoecke (then a professor of comparative law at universities in the UK and Belgium). He identifies six comparative methods[^vanhoecke2015methodology-e1], and which one to use depends mainly on the research question[^vanhoecke2015methodology-e2]. His specific claims:
  - Which countries to compare depends mainly on how the research question is framed[^vanhoecke2015methodology-e3]; even if you chose them because you know the language, give a scholarly justification[^vanhoecke2015methodology-e4].
  - In-depth comparison requires reading the local language; if you can only read English-language sources, contact local scholars, check your understanding with them, and ask whether the law there has changed recently[^vanhoecke2015methodology-e5].
  - Don't stop at legislation, or even at case law: social reality may differ greatly from what the rules suggest[^vanhoecke2015methodology-e6].
  - Don't look at foreign law through the lens and doctrinal framework of your own law[^vanhoecke2015methodology-e7]; simply transplanting foreign law is not a method[^vanhoecke2015methodology-e8].
- **Legal history**, based on a 2014 article by Duve on the methods of European legal history (the author is director of the Max Planck Institute for European Legal History in Germany). He argues: the "legal space" studied (roughly, the geographic area the research covers) should be a result of the research, not a frame drawn in advance[^duve2014european-e1]; you should reflect on the concept of "law" you use to organize your analysis, because applying your own past concepts to other regions can only lead to the conclusion that "it is different out there"[^duve2014european-e2]; and writing legal history from a global perspective requires the matching skills and regional knowledge, and rigorous handling of sources[^duve2014european-e3].
- **Legal philosophy**, based on Atiq and Marmor's entry in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (revised 2025; the authors are at Cornell University in the US, and the entry deals mainly with Anglo-American analytic jurisprudence). They divide the methods of jurisprudence into five main approaches[^atiq2025nature-e1]; two examples:
  - Conceptual analysis: characterizing the concept of law in a way that fits our intuitions[^atiq2025nature-e2]; it succeeds if it gives intuitively correct results in particular cases, in a principled rather than ad hoc way[^atiq2025nature-e3]. The key question is where these intuitions come from and whether they are reliable[^atiq2025nature-e4]; the "naturalized jurisprudence" Leiter advocates insists on empirical methods only and refuses to rely on intuitions[^atiq2025nature-e5].
  - Dworkin's interpretive approach: an interpretation of legal practice must both "fit" actual practice and "justify" it morally[^atiq2025nature-e6]; but the entry's authors note that it remains unclear what concrete standard should be used to evaluate competing theories morally[^atiq2025nature-e6].
  - The authors suggest that the current state of jurisprudence may best be described as "methodological pluralism"[^atiq2025nature-e7].

How to use this table `[Our analysis]`: doctrinal research is the most accepted method in law[^hutchinson2012defining-e5], but one article often mixes several kinds. All 32 Australian criminal law reform articles Hutchinson examined used doctrinal methodology to some extent, and 23 of them made reform recommendations[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e14][^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e10]. Whenever an article makes a claim about the real world, such as "this rule has led to …", that part has to meet the standards in the empirical legal studies row. Likewise, comparing foreign law is often a way to propose reforms to your own: the comparison follows the comparative law row, and the recommendations follow the reform-oriented row.

## Research methods common in this field

**Specific to this field**: [Doctrinal legal research](practices/doctrinal_research.md), covering how to do doctrinal and reform-oriented research. A related page is [Philosophical argument](../philosophy-ethics-religion/practices/philosophical_argument.md) in philosophy. Other methods:

- **Theoretical research and legal philosophy**: examine the conceptual bases of legal principles[^hutchinson2012defining-e3], relying on argument rather than formal proof `[Our analysis]`. For the standards by which the main approaches are judged, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence) above.
- **Comparative law**: compares the rules and practices of different legal systems[^hutchinson2012defining-e21]. Cite foreign law the way it is cited in its own jurisdiction[^goudkamp2026oscola-e6], and watch for differences in translation and institutional context `[Our analysis]`. Van Hoecke puts it this way: a comparative lawyer should be a professional, not a tourist[^vanhoecke2015methodology-e10]; for his specific claims, see the notes below the table above.
- **Legal history**: its methods are close to those of history `[Our analysis]`; for standards, see the notes below the table above, and for how to do it, see [Archival research](../../06_methods/archival_research.md) and the [History and archaeology page](../history-archaeology/README.md).
- **Empirical legal studies**: for counting decisions and coding cases, see [Data science research](../../06_methods/data_science.md); for interviewing judges, lawyers, and litigants, see [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md). When interviewing, remember that judges and senior lawyers are few and easy to identify `[Our analysis]`, and that confidentiality has legal limits (see above).
- **Case study**: note that a "case" in law usually means a court case, whereas [Case study](../../06_methods/case_study.md) in the Research types section is a method for studying a few subjects (such as an organization or a program) in depth in their real setting. Analyzing a few judgments is not the same as doing a case study `[Our analysis]`.
- **Systematic review**: [Review studies](../../06_methods/review_study.md) in the Research types section is about searching and screening literature according to a protocol. In doctrinal research, the literature review is only the first step[^hutchinson2012defining-e8], and it cannot replace analysis of primary legal sources.

## Advice for newcomers

1. **Decide first which country and which legal system you are studying.** Sources, citation, and publishing conventions all follow from that `[Our analysis]`.
2. **Read the primary sources yourself, and check before you submit that they are still good law.** For US cases, use a citator and read the citing decisions in context[^northwesterngoodlaw-e4]; for UK legislation, look at the change notices on legislation.gov.uk[^legislationgovukhelp-e1]; for other countries, ask your university's law librarian `[Our analysis]`.
3. **Make clear that your contribution is more than a description of the current law.** Say at the outset whether it is restatement, recombination, critique, or reform proposals[^hutchinson2012defining-e22][^hutchinson2012defining-e23] `[Our analysis]`.
4. **Use the right citation standard from day one.** Ask first whether your target journal or university uses the UK's OSCOLA, the US *Bluebook*, or another standard `[Our analysis]`, and then be consistent[^goudkamp2026oscola-e1].
5. **Read the target journal's submission guidelines before you submit.** US law reviews generally accept simultaneous submissions[^lwipublishingtips-e2]; don't assume other journals do `[Our analysis]`.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Citing only textbooks, without reading the statutes and judgments | The core of doctrinal research is analysis of primary legal sources[^hutchinson2012defining-e9] |
| Citing a case or statute that has been overruled or amended | You didn't check before submitting that it was still good law[^northwesterngoodlaw-e2] |
| Treating a case as bad law because the citator shows a red flag | A red flag does not necessarily mean it is no longer good law; read the context[^northwesterngoodlaw-e3] |
| An article that only describes the current law | Readers see no new view from you; scholars can choose their own topic and depth, and don't have to find a single answer for a client the way a lawyer does[^hutchinson2012defining-e20] `[Our analysis]` |
| Mixing citation styles, or citing cases without page or paragraph numbers | Breaks OSCOLA's rule of consistency[^goudkamp2026oscola-e1]; OSCOLA requires pinpoints[^goudkamp2026oscola-e3] |
| Carrying the US law review practice of simultaneous submission over to other journals | It is a convention of US law reviews only; many other journals do not allow it `[Our analysis]` |
| Asking a judge or lawyer "why did you do that?" and taking the reply as the answer | Epstein and King consider this one of the worst ways of measuring motive[^epstein2002rules-e17] |
| Promising interviewed judges or lawyers "complete confidentiality" | The UK SLSA principles warn against unrealistic guarantees; researchers may be required to give evidence or hand over documents[^slsa2021ethics-e5][^slsa2021ethics-e10] |

## Further reading

- **Hutchinson & Duncan 2012**, Defining and Describing What We Do: Doctrinal Legal Research (*Deakin Law Review*, open access): read this first if you want to understand what kind of method doctrinal research is and how to write a methodology section. Written from an Australian and UK perspective.
- **Epstein & King 2002**, The Rules of Inference (*University of Chicago Law Review*, free on the authors' websites): worth reading if you plan to do empirical legal research. It is long (133 pages); you can start with Part II and Part VIII (on selecting observations).
- **OSCOLA, 5th edition** (2026, free PDF on the Oxford Law Faculty website): the UK legal citation standard; consult it when your law paper has to follow OSCOLA.
- **Socio-Legal Studies Association, Statement of Principles of Ethical Research Practice** (revised 2021): a UK society's ethics principles; useful before you interview judges or lawyers or use confidential documents.
- **Legal Writing Institute, Publishing Tips**: read it if you plan to submit to US law reviews; it covers simultaneous submission, expedited review, and what happens after an offer.
