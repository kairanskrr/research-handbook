# Doctrinal legal research

This page explains how to **carry out a doctrinal research project step by step** (doctrinal research is also called black-letter research): finding a question, framing a thesis, planning your research, reading cases, interpreting statutes, synthesizing, proposing reform, and writing up your method. What doctrinal research is, the kinds of contribution it can make, the difference between primary and secondary sources, how to check that the law is still good law, and citation rules are covered on the [law field overview](../README.md#doctrinal-research-first-locate-the-law-then-interpret-it); read that first.

**Scope** `[Our analysis]`: this is a research method specific to law. The general logic of putting forward an interpretation of texts or institutions and arguing for it is on the [Interpretation and argument](../../../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md) strategy page; steps shared by every field, such as choosing a topic, planning, and writing, are covered in the main Parts, for example [Converging on a project](../../../01_find-a-problem/05_converge.md) and [Telling the story and writing the paper](../../../03_write-and-publish/01_story-and-paper.md). Claims about the real world in legal research have to meet the standards of empirical research; see Section 1 below.

**Strategy it usually serves**: [Interpretation and argument](../../../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md). That page's "common research methods" section lists this page.

**Which jurisdictions this covers.** Our sources deal mainly with practice in the US, the UK, and Australia (methodology articles by individual authors, US law school handouts and research guides, a report by the US Congressional Research Service, and Wex, Cornell University's free legal dictionary); the two paragraphs on civil law systems and on China draw on comparative law and international organization sources.

Wex says civil law systems are based primarily on written codes and statutes, while common law is based on judicial decisions and precedent; civil law judges focus on applying the law to the facts rather than interpreting earlier rulings[^liiwex-e9]. So the tools in Step 4 below (ratio decidendi, obiter dicta, distinguishing cases) belong only to common law jurisdictions such as the US and the UK.

**How civil law systems differ.** In 1999, Tetley of McGill University in Canada compared the two legal traditions in the journal of the International Institute for the Unification of Private Law (UNIDROIT). This is one scholar's summary, drawn mainly from France, and should not be taken as true of every civil law country. He writes that civil law gives priority to doctrine (the writings of legal scholars, including the codifiers' reports) over case law, while common law does the opposite[^tetley1999mixed-e1]; the task of doctrine is to draw rules and principles out of the disorganized mass of cases and books, to guide the courts in future cases[^tetley1999mixed-e6]. Case law is only a secondary, explanatory source of law[^tetley1999mixed-e2]. Civil law also has no stare decisis (the English common law doctrine that lower courts must follow the decisions of higher courts); judgments have only the "authority of reason"[^tetley1999mixed-e3]. When a provision is ambiguous, the first step is to read the legislation as a whole, together with its preparatory materials (travaux préparatoires), to find the legislator's intention[^tetley1999mixed-e5]. The comparative lawyer Van Hoecke, however, reminds us (2015) that in both common law and civil law systems, legislation and case law are decisive for finding out what the law is[^vanhoecke2015methodology-e12].

**Guiding cases in China.** The China chapter of the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) *International Patent Case Management Judicial Guide* (2025 edition) was written by two Chinese judges, Du Weike and He Juan. It says that Article 18(2) of the Law on the Organization of the People's Courts provides that the Supreme People's Court may publish guiding cases[^wipo2025international-e1]; under the 2015 implementing rules, courts at all levels hearing cases similar to a guiding case in their basic facts and application of law shall decide by reference to the guiding case's adjudication rules, and the guiding case is cited as part of the reasoning, not as the legal basis of the decision[^wipo2025international-e2]. Tetley is not writing about China, and this paragraph covers only the guiding-case system; for how doctrinal research is done in China, follow Chinese textbooks and your advisor `[Our analysis]`.

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- find a doctrinal question, turn it into a one-sentence thesis with reasons, and write a research plan and a research log;
- brief a judgment using fixed headings, and tell ratio decidendi from obiter dicta (in common law jurisdictions);
- say which method of statutory interpretation you are using, evaluate the current law against criteria stated in advance, and write your method down.

## 1. What this research is, and when to use it

The core of doctrinal research is a critical conceptual analysis of all relevant legislation and case law, to arrive at a statement of what the law is on the question you are investigating[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e1]. It can range from solving a practical problem or describing a new law to innovative theory building; the simpler versions are the foundation for the more complex ones[^hutchinson2012defining-e32].

How it differs from related kinds of research (for definitions, see the [field overview](../README.md#the-main-kinds-of-legal-research); the table is our own summary `[Our analysis]`):

| | Doctrinal research | Reform-oriented research | Empirical legal studies | Comparative law |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Question it answers | What the law is, and how the rules relate to each other | Whether the current rules are good enough, and how to change them | How the law works in practice | How another legal system deals with the same problem |
| Main materials | Legislation and case law | Legislation and case law, plus factual evidence | Quantitative data, interviews, and similar material | Primary legal sources from your own and other jurisdictions |

These kinds are often mixed in one article. In 2015 Hutchinson examined 32 Australian journal articles on criminal law reform published in 2013: all of them used doctrinal methodology to some extent[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e14], 23 made reform recommendations[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e10], and 23 included a comparative review of the existing law across jurisdictions (mainly the Australian states)[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e8].

**When to use it.** If your question is "what exactly does this rule mean?", "how do these rules fit together?", or "does this new judgment or regulation fit the existing body of law?", it is a doctrinal question `[Our analysis]`. The scholar Westerman describes a typical approach: take a new legal development as the starting point (for example a new interpretation of a doctrine, or a new piece of European regulation), and show how it fits into the existing area of law; where it does not fit, show how the existing system should be adjusted[^hutchinson2012defining-e33]. If your question is "what effect has this rule had in practice?", that is a claim about the real world and has to meet the standards of empirical research; see [the rules of inference on the field overview](../README.md#empirical-legal-studies-epstein-and-kings-rules-of-inference).

## 2. How to do it

The steps are written in order, but in practice you will loop back. Hutchinson considers the doctrinal method fluid at heart and hard to reduce to an algorithm[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e3]; a Georgetown University handout likewise says research is recursive, and retracing your steps once you know more is part of the process[^georgetown2026research-e9].

### Step 1: Find a doctrinal question

**How.** Look in four directions `[Our analysis: the four directions are our own grouping, based on the sources below]`:

- **Gaps**: a new judgment or regulation has come out, and no one has yet explained how it relates to the existing rules[^hutchinson2012defining-e33]. A Georgetown handout also suggests watching for recent developments in the law and new cases[^georgetown2020topic-e2].
- **Conflicts**: several lines of cases, put side by side, do not agree. The scholar Minow's list of contributions to legal scholarship counts "exposing unjustified discrepancies" as one contribution[^hutchinson2012defining-e35], and counts exposing internally inconsistent structures within a body of law as critical scholarship[^hutchinson2012defining-e37].
- **Uncertainty**: a provision looks unambiguous at first but causes difficulties when applied in the real world[^brannon2023statutory-e6]; or courts are split on how to interpret an area of law[^georgetown2025cases-e8]. The scholar McCrudden says that on anything but the most banal question, the applicable legal norm is likely to be complex and contested[^hutchinson2012defining-e50].
- **A need for reform**: the current rules are not good enough, and need to be evaluated and changes proposed (see the [field overview](../README.md#the-main-kinds-of-legal-research)).

**Narrowing the topic.** The handout suggests these angles: look at how one law applies in one jurisdiction; challenge the current definition of a legal term; compare one issue with another[^georgetown2020topic-e3]. But don't narrow too far at the start: the narrower topic should still allow several different theses[^georgetown2020topic-e4]. **Run a preemption check.** After choosing a topic and before settling your thesis, check whether someone has already published the same argument[^georgetown2026research-e4]. Finding articles on your topic is to be expected and does not mean you have been preempted[^georgetown2026research-e5]; you are preempted only if someone has published a paper with the same thesis defended for the same reasons, or if the facts your thesis rests on change (for example, a new case comes out)[^georgetown2020topic-e5].

**Why.** The topic has to be "thesis worthy": able to support an original and significant thesis[^georgetown2020topic-e1]. **Where people go wrong:** choosing a topic that only lets you describe the current law. For general advice on finding a topic, see the Part 1 chapter [Finding candidate problems](../../../01_find-a-problem/02_generate-problems.md).

### Step 2: Write your research question and thesis

**How.**

1. **Write the research question first**, for example "Does the term X in statute Y cover situation Z?" `[Our analysis]`. Hutchinson and Duncan saw in examples from theses that graduate students increasingly formulate clear research questions to explain and justify the choices made in their work[^hutchinson2012defining-e42].
2. **Then write the thesis** (the claim you will argue). The Georgetown handout says a thesis is an original, supportable assertion that targets a specific aspect of the law, articulates a problem, and ideally tries to resolve it[^georgetown2018thesis-e1]. You can start with a "should … because …" formulation[^georgetown2018thesis-e2]. In doctrinal research, this "main argument" often works as a kind of hypothesis[^hutchinson2012defining-e40]. A US example from the handout: based solely on prior case law, the court in one case should have held that an arbitration clause is enforceable against an employee regardless of the employee's union status[^georgetown2018thesis-e6].
3. **Decide what kind of argument it is**: from precedent, interpretive, normative (is this rule good?), or institutional (should courts, the legislature, or an agency act?)[^georgetown2018thesis-e4].
4. **State the thesis and your main reasons in one or two sentences.** If you can't, you are not ready to draft[^georgetown2026checklist-e1].

**Why.** The thesis decides what you search for and read next `[Our analysis]`. The researcher's stance often determines the research question and the possible conclusions, yet it is often left unstated[^hutchinson2012defining-e39]; writing down your thesis forces you to show where you stand `[Our analysis]`. **Where people go wrong:** never revising the thesis once it is set. The handout suggests testing your thesis against known and hypothetical situations as you write, and revising it accordingly[^georgetown2018thesis-e3].

### Step 3: Make a research plan and keep a research log

**How.**

1. **Write down a research plan**: which sources you will consult, in what order, and your deadlines for research and writing[^georgetown2026research-e1]. Put it on paper and check it as you go, so that departing from the plan is a conscious decision[^georgetown2026research-e2].
2. **Set the scope**: which jurisdiction, which time period, which kinds of material `[Our analysis]`. Primary materials include legislation, delegated legislation (rules made by government bodies under powers given by statute), and case law[^hutchinson2012defining-e49]. The scope can be large, for example all current and historical legislation of every Australian state over the last century[^hutchinson2012defining-e43].
3. **Start with secondary sources for background**[^georgetown2026research-e6]. The handout distinguishes two kinds: descriptive sources simply explain the state of the law, while analytical sources (such as law review articles) evaluate it and suggest what the law should be[^georgetown2026research-e7]. Secondary sources can lead you to the leading cases[^northwesterncaselaw-e3].
4. **Then search case law systematically.** The Northwestern guide advises: unlike secondary sources, with cases you want to find every case that may be relevant, **both for and against your argument**[^northwesterncaselaw-e2]. Start from "one good case": look back at the cases it cites, look forward at later cases that interpret it, and look sideways at other cases under the same topic heading[^northwesterncaselaw-e4]. Use at least one source in which an editor has gathered like cases together, and run at least one Boolean search (combining search terms with AND, OR, and so on) to catch cases you missed or that are too recent[^northwesterncaselaw-e6].
5. **Watch versions when searching legislation.** On the UK's official legislation website, for example, some legislation is held only in its original enacted form, with no revised version[^legislationgovukhelp-e3]. For checking that the law is still good law, see the [field overview](../README.md#primary-sources-secondary-sources-and-is-this-still-good-law).
6. **Keep a research log throughout**: where you searched, what terms you used, and the results of each step. That way you don't repeat work, and if you can't find a definitive answer you can report what you found and where you looked[^northwesterncaselaw-e1]. The Georgetown handout suggests also noting why you are looking at each source[^georgetown2026research-e3].

**An example to copy.** Hutchinson (2015) reported her own search: the database was AustLII (the Australasian Legal Information Institute), the search string `crim* w/10 (reform* OR recommend*)`, the scope Australian journals in 2013, the search date 16 February 2015; it returned 60 items, and after removing book reviews, speeches, and government publications, 32 articles remained[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e4]. At minimum, your log should let you write a paragraph like that `[Our analysis]`.

**Why.** Locating the law is the step others should be able to replicate (see the [field overview](../README.md#doctrinal-research-first-locate-the-law-then-interpret-it)), and the log is the evidence `[Our analysis]`. Hutchinson and Duncan also point out that the screening criteria for primary legal materials are necessarily rule-bound and intricate[^hutchinson2012defining-e48]; if you don't write them down, no one can see how you chose `[Our analysis]`. For the general approach to searching and screening literature according to a protocol, see [Review studies](../../../06_methods/review_study.md) in the Research types section. **When to stop.** The Northwestern guide says you are about done when you keep seeing the same cases over and over without anything new[^northwesterncaselaw-e5]. The Georgetown handout gives three tests: you have covered the most-cited cases and articles on your topic; you have checked recent publications; and every key part of your thesis is supported[^georgetown2026research-e11].

### Step 4: Read and analyze judgments (common law jurisdictions)

**How: write a case brief for each judgment.** The handout says this lets you dissect a case first and reassemble it later[^georgetown2025cases-e2]. Useful headings: the issue (the legal question put to the court), the relevant facts, the procedural posture, the holding (the court's answer to the issue and its reasoning), dicta, and the court's analytical approach[^georgetown2025cases-e3]. We suggest adding two more: the name and level of the court, and how the judgment has been treated since `[Our analysis]`.

**Tell ratio decidendi from obiter dicta.** These are common law concepts. The definitions below come from Wex, and most of its examples are from US courts; for details in the UK, Australia, and elsewhere, follow your own country's textbooks `[Our analysis]`.

- The ratio decidendi is the key factual point or chain of reasoning that drives the judgment, and it creates binding precedent; to identify it, look at the material facts of the case and the law used to decide it[^liiwex-e1][^liiwex-e2].
- An obiter dictum is not necessary to resolve the case and does not bind other courts, but it can be cited as persuasive authority; dissenting opinions are generally treated as dicta too[^liiwex-e3].
- Legal scholars often disagree about what counts as dicta[^liiwex-e4], so give reasons for how you classify statements `[Our analysis]`. Dicta are not useless either: they are frequently taken up in later judgments and sometimes become their basis[^liiwex-e5].
- A judgment is precedent only on the issues the court explicitly considered[^liiwex-e7]; whether it is binding depends on the rank and jurisdiction of the court[^liiwex-e8].

**Learn to distinguish cases.** Common law reasoning rests on the basic assumption that like cases should be decided alike[^georgetown2017comparison-e1]: similar facts lead to a similar outcome (analogizing); different facts lead to a different outcome (distinguishing)[^georgetown2017comparison-e2]. To distinguish a case is to argue that, because of some difference between the two cases, the earlier one should not apply as precedent to the later one[^liiwex-e6]. The key point: a difference is legally significant only if it relates to an element of the rule or to a principle behind it[^georgetown2017comparison-e3].

**Why read the full text.** Headnotes in databases are editor-written descriptions of the main legal issues in a case[^georgetown2026research-e8], not the judgment itself. The Northwestern guide advises reading in full every case you might cite, that your opponent might cite, or that comes from the highest court[^northwesterncaselaw-e7].

**Where people go wrong.** First, **mistaken negation**: you cannot argue that because the precedent has fact x and the present case does not, the outcome must be different[^georgetown2017comparison-e4]. Second, **cherry-picking favorable cases**: you have to anticipate and rebut counterexamples, cases with similar facts but the opposite outcome[^georgetown2017comparison-e5].

### Step 5: Interpret statutes

**How.** The tools below come from US sources and describe US practice.

1. **Start with the text of the statute.** The statutory language is always the starting point; your first reading may not be the only plausible interpretation, or even the correct one[^georgetown2026statutes-e1]. Many statutes have a "definitions" section; find it first[^georgetown2026statutes-e4].
2. **Read the cases interpreting the statute.** Quoting the statute and applying it to your case is not enough; your analysis should include the key language from the case law that interprets and develops the statute[^georgetown2025cases-e6]. If the statute codified existing common law, earlier cases are still good law; if it rejected the common law, earlier cases are probably no longer good law[^georgetown2025cases-e5].
3. **Say which method of interpretation you are using.** A 2023 report by the US Congressional Research Service describes two main theories in the US federal courts: purposivism favors interpretations that advance the statute's purpose, and textualism looks mainly at the statutory text[^brannon2023statutory-e1]. Judges most often rely on five types of tools: the ordinary meaning of the words, the statute's context and structure, the canons of construction (presumptions about how courts read statutes), legislative history (the record of Congress's deliberations), and how the statute has been implemented[^brannon2023statutory-e3]. Both camps may use any of these tools; they differ in the order in which they apply them and how much weight they give each[^brannon2023statutory-e2].

**Why state your method.** Judges disagree even about the ordinary meaning of the same word[^brannon2023statutory-e4]. Canons do not settle things either: the Georgetown handout warns that for every canon supporting your reading, there is often an opposing canon that can be used against you[^georgetown2026statutes-e3]; the report, however, also records another view, that many "opposing canons" are really a rule and its exception[^brannon2023statutory-e7]. So the safer course is to state which tools you use and in what order, and to respond to the competing reading `[Our analysis]`. **Where people go wrong:** treating legislative history from different sources as equally reliable. Some US judges consider committee reports stronger evidence of Congress's understanding than individual floor statements by non-sponsors[^brannon2023statutory-e5]. Our sources do not cover methods of interpretation in the UK or in civil law countries; follow your own country's textbooks.

### Step 6: Synthesize, and find the inconsistencies

**How.**

1. **Put the cases in a table**: one row per case, with the court level, date, key facts, outcome, and reasoning `[Our analysis]`.
2. **Synthesize the rule.** Where the rule comes from case law, examine several cases to find the major premise underlying them all[^hutchinson2012defining-e46]. The handout's example: three cases, two for the claim and one against, synthesized into one rule[^georgetown2025cases-e7]. You can also ask whether the cases can be synthesized into a framework, even one the courts have not articulated themselves[^northwesterncaselaw-e8].
3. **Where the rule comes from legislation, reasoning is mostly deductive**, because the general rule is given by the legislation[^hutchinson2012defining-e45]; **for similar situations, reason by analogy**: similar cases should be governed by the same principle and have similar outcomes[^hutchinson2012defining-e47].
4. **Mark what is inconsistent or unsettled**: distinguish settled law from emerging law[^hutchinson2012defining-e34], and point out unjustified discrepancies[^hutchinson2012defining-e35]. "Identifying and resolving inconsistencies, logical errors, and omissions" can be a thesis in itself[^georgetown2018thesis-e5].

**Why.** Listing the current law is not enough; the value lies in the new view you reach by organizing, comparing, and critiquing it (see the [field overview](../README.md#doctrinal-research-first-locate-the-law-then-interpret-it)). **Where people go wrong:** collecting only the material that supports you, the way a lawyer does. The US scholars Epstein and King point out that lawyers are trained to amass the evidence for their side, while PhDs are trained to seek out the evidence against their own theory[^epstein2002rules-e4]. Research should follow the latter `[Our analysis]`, and address seriously the counterarguments that have already been made[^georgetown2026research-e10].

### Step 7: Evaluate the current law and propose reform

**How: follow the four steps of "policy analysis" in Minow's list**: present the problem; canvass the alternatives; propose evaluative criteria or a method; recommend a preferred solution[^hutchinson2012defining-e36]. Under "restatement", Minow also says that when judging which approach is "better", you should ideally explain the criteria you used[^hutchinson2012defining-e34]. The criteria might be: consistency with other rules, clarity and predictability, fairness, enforceability `[Our analysis: our sources give no fixed list]`. The key is to **state your criteria first, then judge every option against the same criteria** `[Our analysis]`.

**Why: reform proposals need a factual basis.** The scholar Barnett says law reformers need to see the whole picture and identify the real problem before looking for policy solutions[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e12]. As soon as you write "this rule has led to …", you are making a claim about the real world[^epstein2002rules-e15], which needs evidence, not just reasoning `[Our analysis]`. Of the 32 Australian criminal law reform articles Hutchinson examined, only 2 reported surveys, interviews, or statistical work by the authors themselves, while 18 used statistics published elsewhere to show the basis for concern[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e6]. **Where people go wrong:** criticizing without offering a way forward. Five of the 32 articles simply critiqued the current law[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e10]; critique alone can be a contribution, but say so in the introduction `[Our analysis]`.

### Step 8: Write the methodology section

As the field overview explains, the method of doctrinal research is often left unstated, and you should at least include one sentence on it (see the [field overview](../README.md#doctrinal-research-first-locate-the-law-then-interpret-it)). In the 32 articles Hutchinson examined, the doctrinal method design was always implicit, while non-doctrinal methods such as surveys were explained, with tables[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e5]. To do better, state:

- which databases you searched, with what search strings, when, and how you screened the results, following the way Hutchinson (2015) reported her search[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e4];
- whether your line of inquiry is conceptual, evaluative, or explanatory[^hutchinson2012defining-e44];
- your theoretical stance, because it often determines your conclusions yet is often left unstated[^hutchinson2012defining-e39];
- your criteria for evaluation and reform (see Step 7)[^hutchinson2012defining-e36];
- the jurisdiction, the date at which the law is stated, the methods of reasoning and statutory interpretation you used, and what material you did not search `[Our analysis]`.

On structure, the Georgetown checklist for scholarly writing asks for a roadmap of the whole paper in the introduction[^georgetown2026checklist-e2], for all points of view to be accurately presented and responded to[^georgetown2026checklist-e3], and for writing that analyzes rather than describes[^georgetown2026checklist-e4]. For general advice, see the Part 4 chapter [Telling the story and writing the paper](../../../03_write-and-publish/01_story-and-paper.md).

### Step 9 (optional): Add some comparative law

**How.** Hutchinson says the current expectation in the literature is a statement of the legal position across jurisdictions, or at least an acknowledgment of obvious differences in practice elsewhere[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e9]. Of the 32 articles, only 2 had comparison as their main objective, but 23 included a comparative review[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e8]. Minow describes the task of comparative research as describing a contrasting legal regime and illuminating the differences, choices, or continuities the comparison reveals[^hutchinson2012defining-e38].

**Why.** Hutchinson notes that the Arthurs Report saw comparative and historical research as likely to lead to "new" solutions[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e13]. **Where people go wrong:** transplanting foreign rules without looking at your own country's circumstances. Hutchinson and Duncan cite the argument of an Australian doctoral thesis: China's reform of its corporate disclosure law relied heavily on the US model, which may not suit China[^hutchinson2012defining-e41]. Common law and civil law systems differ in their sources of law and in the role of judges[^liiwex-e9], so explain the institutional background on both sides before comparing `[Our analysis]`. The UK citation standard OSCOLA requires foreign primary sources to be cited the way they are cited in their own jurisdiction[^goudkamp2026oscola-e6]. Our sources do not cover comparative law method in full; follow comparative law textbooks and your advisor.

## 3. Checklist

**Must do**

- [ ] State which jurisdiction you are studying and the date at which the law is stated `[Our analysis]`
- [ ] You can state your thesis and main reasons in one or two sentences[^georgetown2026checklist-e1]
- [ ] You have read the full text of the legislation and judgments, not just the headnotes[^georgetown2026research-e8][^northwesterncaselaw-e7]
- [ ] You have looked for cases both for and against you, and responded to counterarguments and counterexamples[^northwesterncaselaw-e2][^georgetown2026checklist-e3][^georgetown2017comparison-e5]
- [ ] You have a research log: where you searched, what terms you used, what you found[^northwesterncaselaw-e1]
- [ ] (Common law jurisdictions) You have not treated obiter dicta as binding rules[^liiwex-e3]
- [ ] Before submitting, you checked that the statutes and cases you cite are still good law, for example with a citator in the US[^georgetown2026statutes-e2] or the change notices on the legislation website in the UK[^legislationgovukhelp-e1]

**Better practice**

- [ ] The methodology section states the search strings, search dates, and screening process[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e4], and your line of inquiry[^hutchinson2012defining-e44]
- [ ] You state your method of statutory interpretation and respond to the competing reading `[Our analysis]`
- [ ] Evaluative criteria are stated before reform proposals[^hutchinson2012defining-e36], and claims about the real world have a factual basis[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e12]
- [ ] You describe the position in other jurisdictions, or obvious differences[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e9]

**Fine to skip; not a flaw**

- No separate "literature review" chapter: in the 32 Australian criminal law reform articles Hutchinson examined, secondary literature was always analyzed, but mostly cited throughout the discussion rather than under a separate heading[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e7]. Whether a thesis needs a separate chapter depends on your university's rules `[Our analysis]`.
- No empirical work of your own: of the 32 articles, 18 used statistics published elsewhere, and only 2 reported the authors' own surveys[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e6]. But the data you use must be reliable and its source stated `[Our analysis]`.

**Criticisms that don't hold**

- "Without experiments and statistics, it isn't research": the reports on legal research that Hutchinson and Duncan cite all agree that doctrinal research is the most accepted methodology in law[^hutchinson2012defining-e5].
- "Interpretation varies from person to person, so it is unreliable": the interpretive step depends on the researcher's expertise and methods by its nature (see the [field overview](../README.md#doctrinal-research-first-locate-the-law-then-interpret-it)); what should be required is a clear statement of method and reasons, not the elimination of personal judgment `[Our analysis]`.

## 4. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Giving up as soon as you find articles on the same topic | You are preempted only if the thesis and the reasons are the same[^georgetown2020topic-e5] |
| Looking only for cases that support you | You need to find all the cases, both for and against[^northwesterncaselaw-e2] |
| Reading only the headnotes | They are editor-written descriptions[^georgetown2026research-e8] |
| Treating obiter dicta as binding precedent | Dicta do not bind other courts[^liiwex-e3] |
| Arguing that because the precedent has fact x and this case does not, the outcome must differ | This is "mistaken negation"[^georgetown2017comparison-e4] |
| Distinguishing cases on factual differences unrelated to the rule | A difference counts only if it relates to an element of the rule or a principle behind it[^georgetown2017comparison-e3] |
| Drawing a conclusion from a single canon of construction | There is often an opposing canon that can be used against you[^georgetown2026statutes-e3] |
| Citing the statute without the cases that interpret it | Your analysis must include the cases interpreting the provision[^georgetown2025cases-e6] |
| Reform proposals with no stated criteria or no factual basis | Policy analysis requires proposing evaluative criteria first[^hutchinson2012defining-e36], and identifying the real problem first[^hutchinson2015doctrinal-e12] |
| Transplanting foreign rules wholesale | A foreign model may not suit your own country[^hutchinson2012defining-e41] |

## 5. Further reading

- **Hutchinson & Duncan 2012**, Defining and Describing What We Do: Doctrinal Legal Research (*Deakin Law Review*, open access): the two steps of doctrinal research, methods of legal reasoning, and how to write the methodology section. Australian and UK perspective.
- **Hutchinson 2015**, The Doctrinal Method (*Erasmus Law Review*; an author version is in the Queensland University of Technology repository): how reform-oriented doctrinal research uses statistics and comparative law; a good model for reporting your search. Set in Australia.
- **Georgetown University Law Center Writing Center handouts** (free PDFs, written for US law students): from choosing a topic and developing a thesis to research, using cases, case comparison, reading statutes, and a final checklist.
- **Northwestern University law library, Researching Case Law**: how to find all the relevant cases and when to stop; the tools are US commercial databases.
- **US Congressional Research Service report, Statutory Interpretation** (Brannon 2023): the theories and tools US federal courts use to interpret statutes.
- **Wex** (the free legal dictionary of Cornell University's Legal Information Institute): look up ratio decidendi, obiter dictum, precedent, and other terms.
