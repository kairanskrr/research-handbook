# Political science: field overview

This page explains how research in political science (including international relations, public administration, and public policy) actually works: quantitative, experimental, qualitative, and formal-theory approaches living side by side; the long-running debate over how transparent data and research processes should be; journal replication checks and pre-analysis plans for field experiments; and the ethical problems that come with doing research in real political settings. Practices shared by every field (choosing a topic, writing, peer review, research ethics) are covered in the chapters listed in [The research journey at a glance](../../00_start-here/research-journey.md) and in [Throughout the project](../../08_throughout/README.md), and are not repeated here; for the research strategies political science commonly uses and the evidence each one needs, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence). The norms on this page draw mainly on documents from the American Political Science Association (APSA) and US journals; in other countries and institutions, local rules apply `[Our analysis]`.

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- explain what kinds of questions the main approaches in political science answer, and how they differ from economics and sociology;
- understand what quantitative and qualitative scholars each worry about in the debate over data access and research transparency;
- know roughly what journal replication checks and pre-analysis plans ask of you, and where the common gaps are;
- in research involving elections, officials, or authoritarian settings, anticipate ethical problems that other fields rarely run into.

## How research works in this field

### Several research traditions side by side

Political science has no single dominant method, and one department often houses all of the following kinds of research `[Our analysis]`:

- **Quantitative analysis of observational data**: statistical analysis of election results, opinion polls, legislative votes, conflict events, and similar data, often to answer causal questions. The thinking about causal identification is close to economics; see [the economics overview](../economics/README.md#the-credibility-revolution-first-answer-whats-your-identification-strategy) `[Our analysis]`.
- **Experiments**: field experiments intervene at random in the real world (for example, mailing get-out-the-vote letters to a random subset of voters), and lab experiments take place in a controlled setting `[Our analysis]`; a survey experiment is simply "an experiment run inside a survey"[^egapsurveyexperiments-e1]. Among studies that registered a pre-analysis plan (explained below), experiments are the vast majority: of the 195 plans from 2011–2016 that Ofosu and Posner sampled, 63% came from field experiments, 27% from survey experiments, and 4% from lab experiments, while observational studies made up only 4%[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e3]. This sample also includes plans from economics, so it does not show the mix of methods across political science as a whole `[Our analysis]`.
- **Qualitative case studies and process tracing**: in-depth study of one or a few countries, events, or policies, tracing step by step how things happened, to test or build causal explanations `[Our analysis]`.
- **Comparative research**: systematic comparison of the institutions and outcomes of several countries or regions; it can be qualitative or quantitative `[Our analysis]`.
- **Elite interviews**: interviewing officials, legislators, diplomats, and leaders of parties and organizations `[Our analysis]`. Under APSA's 2020 principles for human subjects research, what is ethically permitted when studying powerful people is not quite the same as when studying ordinary people (see the ethics section below)[^apsa2020humansubjects-e4].
- **Formal theory**: using game theory and other mathematical models to derive how political actors will choose under institutional constraints `[Our analysis]`.
- **Interpretive and ethnographic research**: focuses on political meaning, discourse, and everyday practice `[Our analysis]`.

This pluralism comes with tension. Jacobs and colleagues' 2021 overview notes that, in many people's view, qualitative traditions are losing ground to quantitative methods on several fronts, including space in top journals[^jacobs2021qualitative-e4]. Qualitative researchers also disagree among themselves at a basic level: some think "transparency" is at least partly valuable, while others think the very concept is incompatible with how they understand knowledge to be produced[^jacobs2021qualitative-e14]. Before you commit to an area, find out what kind of evidence its leading journals and reviewers accept `[Our analysis]`.

### Survey experiments: measuring sensitive attitudes inside a questionnaire

One use of survey experiments is measuring sensitive attitudes. On questions such as racial prejudice or illegal behavior, asking directly underestimates how common they are[^egapsurveyexperiments-e2]. Several common designs:

| Design | How it works | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| List experiment | The control group gets a list of harmless items, the treatment group gets the same list plus one sensitive item, and respondents are asked only "how many of these apply to you?" `[Our analysis]` | Attitudes or behavior respondents might lie about if asked directly[^egapsurveyexperiments-e5] |
| Endorsement experiment | Respondents are asked how much they support a policy; the treatment group is told the policy is backed by a controversial figure or group `[Our analysis]` | Respondents don't have to state a position directly, which suits politically sensitive settings[^egapsurveyexperiments-e4] |
| Conjoint experiment | Respondents repeatedly choose between two options whose attributes are randomly varied[^egapsurveyexperiments-e3] | Preferences that involve several factors at once `[Our analysis]` |

Two cautions: respondents may answer carelessly, so keep questions and stimulus materials short and easy to understand[^egapsurveyexperiments-e6]; and results measured in a survey do not necessarily carry over to the real world[^egapsurveyexperiments-e7].

### Publishing culture

Political science has both journal articles and scholarly books `[Our analysis]`. Each point below reflects one school, one journal, or one cross-disciplinary study, not a rule for the whole discipline `[Our analysis]`:

- **How books and conference papers count (one US college as an example).** The reappointment, tenure, and promotion standards of the political science department at The College of New Jersey (2023 revision) state: there is no exact formula for converting books into articles, but a book generally counts as two to four articles[^tcnj2023polisci-e1]; conference papers are usually not peer reviewed and are mainly seen as evidence that research is ongoing and headed for a journal[^tcnj2023polisci-e2]; and the department considers it rare in political science for conference papers to be published in proceedings[^tcnj2023polisci-e3]. These are the rules of this one department at this one college; other schools and countries may differ, so follow your own institution `[Our analysis]`. Our sources do not say which areas value books more.
- **How long review takes.** The editorial team of the *American Political Science Review* that took office in June 2020[^apsr2024editorial-e1] gave figures for its own term in its fall 2023 report (the table covers submissions through the end of July 2023[^apsr2024editorial-e4]): the median time from submission to desk rejection was 10 days, to rejection after review 74.5 days, and to a revise-and-resubmit after review 89 days[^apsr2024editorial-e2]; of the manuscripts on which this team made a final decision, the overall acceptance rate was 7.9%[^apsr2024editorial-e3]. In a cross-disciplinary study published in 2013, Björk and Solomon drew 2,700 papers from 135 journals in the Scopus citation database[^bjork2013publishing-e1], all published around 2012, with the journals roughly sorted into nine groups and political science not broken out separately[^bjork2013publishing-e2]; in the social sciences group, the average time from submission to acceptance was 6.2 months, and from acceptance to publication 7.9 months[^bjork2013publishing-e6]. Differences between journals within a group were larger than differences between groups[^bjork2013publishing-e5], so for how long your target journal takes, rely on the figures it publishes itself `[Our analysis]`.
- **Author order.** Waltman (2012) analyzed multi-author papers indexed in Web of Science[^waltman2012empirical-e2], comparing papers from 2007–2011 by journal subject category[^waltman2012empirical-e3]. In the "political science" category, 60.8% of papers had authors in exactly alphabetical order; after removing papers that are alphabetical by chance, about 36.3% were estimated to be intentionally alphabetical[^waltman2012empirical-e7]. The "intentional" share is a model estimate, not a direct count[^waltman2012empirical-e4]. When authors are listed alphabetically, the order tells you nothing about who contributed more[^waltman2012empirical-e8]. So in political science, intentionally alphabetical collaborations are not unusual, but most papers do not use them; agree on the order with your coauthors when you start `[Our analysis]`.

For the practice of circulating working papers first, see [the economics overview](../economics/README.md#working-papers-first-journal-publication-is-slow) `[Our analysis]`.

On authorship, APSA's ethics guide (2012 revision) is explicit: faculty must not claim students' independent research as their own, and research assistance must be fully acknowledged whether or not it was paid[^apsa2012ethics-e7]; an advisor has no right to demand coauthorship of a student's dissertation[^apsa2012ethics-e8].

## How this field differs from others

| | Political science | Comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Research methods | Quantitative, experimental, qualitative case studies, formal theory, and interpretive research side by side `[Our analysis]` | Empirical work in economics is more concentrated on causal identification `[Our analysis]`; see [the economics overview](../economics/README.md#the-credibility-revolution-first-answer-whats-your-identification-strategy) |
| Focus of the transparency debate | Whether uniform transparency and data-sharing requirements fit qualitative research (such as full interview transcripts)[^jacobs2021qualitative-e1][^jacobs2021qualitative-e7] | Data policies in economics mainly target quantitative data and code; see [the economics overview](../economics/README.md#data-and-code-must-reproduce-the-results) `[Our analysis]` |
| Effects on political processes | Research can intervene directly in real political processes such as elections, and APSA's 2020 human subjects principles have a principle specifically about this impact[^apsa2020humansubjects-e11] | In psychology, the people studied are mostly individual participants; see [the psychology overview](../psychology/README.md) `[Our analysis]` |

Sociology also uses interviews, ethnography, and surveys a lot; see [the sociology overview](../sociology/README.md#the-main-approaches). What sets political science apart is that its subject is often power itself, and that research can feed back into the political process `[Our analysis]`.

## Norms and reporting standards in this field

### Data Access and Research Transparency (DA-RT)

DA-RT is the umbrella name for a set of documents and debates through which US political science pushed for research transparency in the 2010s `[Our analysis]`.

**APSA's ethics guide** (2012 revision) says researchers have an ethical obligation to make it possible for others to evaluate or replicate their conclusions, through three kinds of openness[^apsa2012ethics-e1]:

- **Data access**: for data you collected yourself, provide access, or explain why you cannot[^apsa2012ethics-e2];
- **Production transparency**: give a full account of how the data were collected or generated[^apsa2012ethics-e3];
- **Analytic transparency**: make clear the link between the data and the conclusions[^apsa2012ethics-e4].

The first two can be waived for reasons of privacy, confidentiality, or legal compliance (including copyright)[^apsa2012ethics-e5]; people who collected the data can delay releasing them for one year after publication[^apsa2012ethics-e6].

**The Journal Editors' Transparency Statement (JETS, 2014)** is a joint statement by political science journal editors. The signing journals committed to putting several requirements in place by January 15, 2016[^dart2014jets-e1]: authors deposit the data they cite in a trusted digital repository at publication[^dart2014jets-e2], and tell the editor at submission if the data are restricted[^dart2014jets-e3]; explain the analytic steps and provide analysis materials where possible[^dart2014jets-e4]; and cite data created by others as scholarly work[^dart2014jets-e5]. As of March 2019, 27 political science journals had signed the statement[^jacobs2021qualitative-e3].

> **Where sources disagree: what does transparency mean for qualitative research?**
>
> - **Proponents**: transparency lets research be evaluated and replicated[^apsa2012ethics-e1], and data should go into trusted repositories[^dart2014jets-e2].
> - **Critics**: according to the statement website's November 2015 update, several scholars organized a petition asking for implementation of these transparency standards to be delayed[^dart2014jets-e6]; according to Jacobs and colleagues' 2021 overview, more than 1,100 political scientists signed an open letter in fall 2015[^jacobs2021qualitative-e2]. Hundreds of political scientists took part in the Qualitative Transparency Deliberations (QTD)[^jacobs2021qualitative-e5], which Jacobs and colleagues' article summarizes. Its main conclusions: there is no single transparency standard that fits all qualitative research[^jacobs2021qualitative-e6]; sharing some raw material is valuable, but uniform, maximal sharing requirements are deeply problematic[^jacobs2021qualitative-e7]; protecting participants should take priority over sharing information with readers[^jacobs2021qualitative-e8]; and the working groups agreed that sharing qualitative data should not be uniformly required[^jacobs2021qualitative-e15]. The reasons include: in closely watched communities, word choice, the events mentioned, or even the timing of an interview can expose a respondent[^jacobs2021qualitative-e10]; information that is low-risk today can become high-risk if the political situation changes[^jacobs2021qualitative-e11]; and respondents who know the full transcript will be published are less likely to speak frankly[^jacobs2021qualitative-e12].
> - **Common ground**: qualitative research communities broadly agree that publications should explain in detail how the evidence was produced, how the conclusions were reached, what risks participants faced, and how they were protected[^jacobs2021qualitative-e9]. Sharing is not all-or-nothing either: you can provide just the most important documents or interview excerpts[^jacobs2021qualitative-e13].
>
> Practical advice for newcomers: whether your work is quantitative or qualitative, make clear "where the evidence came from and how you reached your conclusions"; whether to release raw material, and how much, depends first on the risk to participants `[Our analysis]`. For the same debate in sociology, see [the sociology overview](../sociology/README.md#transparency-in-qualitative-research-the-debate-continues).

### Journal replication checks

Take the *American Journal of Political Science* (AJPS, published by the Midwest Political Science Association) as the example. The points below are the rules of this one journal only (based on its website as accessed in October 2026; the page notes that the relevant documents will soon be updated); other journals have different requirements, so **follow your target journal's current policy**.

- After a paper is accepted, the corresponding author must provide enough material for others to verify every analytic result in the text and appendix[^ajps2026verification-e1], deposited in the AJPS Dataverse on Harvard Dataverse[^ajps2026verification-e2].
- When you submit the final version, the journal checks whether the materials actually reproduce the results in the paper; only papers that pass are published[^ajps2026verification-e4].
- The journal has separate verification checklists for quantitative and qualitative data[^ajps2026verification-e3]. Papers that do not analyze empirical data are exempt[^ajps2026verification-e5].
- You can ask for an exemption for the data because access is restricted or to protect human subjects, but other materials, such as program commands, must still be provided[^ajps2026verification-e6].

Why check before publication? Look at another journal's experience. The *Quarterly Journal of Political Science* (QJPS) has required submissions to include data and code since it was founded in 2005[^eubank2016lessons-e1]. Eubank reported in 2016 that of the 24 empirical papers that went through the journal's in-house replication review since September 2012, only 4 replication packages needed no changes; in 14 (58%), the results in the paper differed from what the authors' own code produced[^eubank2016lessons-e2]. Of the other 20, the code for 13 ran with errors, and 8 were missing code for some results[^eubank2016lessons-e3]. Each review took about 6 hours and cost about $180[^eubank2016lessons-e4]. Eubank concluded that most problems came from changing the code without updating the paper, or from copying results into the paper wrongly[^eubank2016lessons-e6]. This is one journal's experience with 24 papers and cannot be taken as a rate for the whole discipline `[Our analysis]`. For general ways to organize a replication package, see [Organizing your research materials](../../08_throughout/organizing_materials.md) in Throughout the project.

### Pre-analysis plans

A **pre-analysis plan** is a research design and analysis plan written before the analysis and usually registered on a third-party website[^egappreanalysis-e1]. The methods guides of the research network Evidence in Governance and Politics (EGAP) recommend:

- at a minimum, state all the hypotheses to be tested and how the relevant variables will be measured, with a verifiable timestamp[^egappreanalysis-e2];
- if you have more than one hypothesis, say in advance how you will handle multiple testing[^egappreanalysis-e3];
- in experiments and studies that collect their own data, you get only one chance to collect the data[^egappreanalysis-e4], so think through in advance what you need to collect `[Our analysis]`;
- register experimental studies before the final data collection at the latest, and observational studies before any analysis (including looking at descriptive statistics) at the latest; you can revise the plan at any time afterward[^egappreanalysis-e6];
- exploratory analysis is fine; the plan's job is to make clear where it came from[^egappreanalysis-e5].

Ofosu and Posner observe that graduate students are now taught that registering a pre-analysis plan is an essential part of doing research[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e2]. But when they examined the 195 plans mentioned above (registered on the EGAP and American Economic Association platforms[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e1]), they found plenty of gaps: 44% did not specify which control variables would be included[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e6]; only 25% said how missing data or attrition would be handled[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e8]. Looking at the corresponding papers in the sample: in 14 papers the models departed from the plan, and only 1 said so[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e7]; the median paper failed to report 25% of the hypotheses in its plan[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e9]; and of the papers that reported unregistered hypotheses, 82% did not say so[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e10]. The cost is not small either. In 2018 they surveyed researchers in two research networks, EGAP and Innovations for Poverty Action (IPA), sending the survey to 664 people and getting 155 responses[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e4][^ofosu2023preanalysis-e5]. Of these 155, 88% said writing the plan for a typical project took a week or more[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e11], but 64% agreed it was "time-consuming but worth it"[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e13]. When you are racing to run an experiment before an election, the delay of writing a plan can sink the project[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e12], so prepare early `[Our analysis]`. For the difference between exploratory and confirmatory research, see [From research questions to an evidence plan](../../02_do-the-project/01_question-to-evidence.md#5-be-clear-whether-you-are-testing-or-exploring) in Part 2.

### Ethics: doing research in real political settings

**APSA's Principles and Guidance for Human Subjects Research** (adopted April 2020)[^apsa2020humansubjects-e1] states plainly that the principles are not rules or a checklist[^apsa2020humansubjects-e2]; researchers must think through ethical questions themselves and cannot "outsource" them to an institutional review board (IRB)[^apsa2020humansubjects-e3]. This is a US association's document; if you do research in another country, you also have to follow local rules `[Our analysis]`. The following points matter especially for political research `[Our analysis]`:

- **Getting consent.** You should generally obtain informed consent from direct participants, especially when the risk is more than minimal or when you can expect they would refuse if asked[^apsa2020humansubjects-e6].
- **Studying powerful people.** When studying some public officials, institutions, companies, and other powerful actors, covert or deceptive research with more than minimal risk can sometimes be allowed[^apsa2020humansubjects-e4]; when studying elites, you can also choose not to promise confidentiality, as long as you make this clear to them[^apsa2020humansubjects-e19]. Note: the guidance to these principles points out that the US "new rule" no longer exempts research on elected officials and public officials[^apsa2020humansubjects-e5]. This refers to the revised US federal rule for the protection of human subjects that took effect in January 2019, and it covers only US research; if you study officials in the US, you still go through your university's ethics review as usual `[Our analysis]`.
- **Don't damage the political process.** In general, you should not compromise the integrity of a political process for research purposes without the consent of direct participants[^apsa2020humansubjects-e11]. The principles give the example of placing imitation campaign ads on Facebook: this counts as minimal risk at the societal level only if the scale is too small to change an election result and no false information is introduced[^apsa2020humansubjects-e13]. Approval from an election authority provides some justification but does not relieve researchers of their own ethical judgment[^apsa2020humansubjects-e15]; working with a third party does not hand all ethical responsibility over to that party either[^apsa2020humansubjects-e14]. When you publish, report the effects on participants and on the political process, and whether consent was sought and from whom[^apsa2020humansubjects-e16]. If your findings merely reflect badly on a politician and so affect votes, that generally does not count as the problem meant here[^apsa2020humansubjects-e20].
- **The same design carries different risk under a different regime.** Inviting citizens to sign a petition is low-risk in a democracy where political rights are protected; under an authoritarian regime, signers may face reprisals[^apsa2020humansubjects-e12].
- **Protecting respondents.** When assessing harm, consider that a breach of confidentiality could expose dissidents and their associates[^apsa2020humansubjects-e7]; breaches can come from carelessness, data sharing, cybersecurity incidents, freedom-of-information requests, court subpoenas, mandatory reporting, and more[^apsa2020humansubjects-e10]. Material you share must be redacted so that even someone with inside knowledge cannot tell who said it[^apsa2020humansubjects-e8]; even when a journal requires data sharing, your duty to protect participants still stands[^apsa2020humansubjects-e9].
- **Watch for trauma and multiple jurisdictions.** Research on war or sexual violence is more likely to cause trauma[^apsa2020humansubjects-e18]; if you do research in several countries or regions, or you are based in one place and the research is in another, learn the laws and regulations of each place[^apsa2020humansubjects-e17].

> **Where sources disagree: should election field experiments be done?**
>
> - **Critics**: in a 2020 opinion piece in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* (PNAS), McDermott and Hatemi point out that many studies have made large-scale interventions in real elections to learn about turnout[^mcdermott2020ethics-e2], some targeting minority groups large enough to change the result[^mcdermott2020ethics-e3]. A review they cite found that field experiments in top political science journals from 2013–2017 showed almost no evidence of complying with current ethical requirements[^mcdermott2020ethics-e1]. They propose adding a principle of "respect for societies"[^mcdermott2020ethics-e4], under which any field experiment in a real society should obtain consent from the relevant public and debrief it afterward[^mcdermott2020ethics-e5].
> - **APSA's 2020 principles**: in general, do not compromise a political process without consent[^apsa2020humansubjects-e11], but this is not one-size-fits-all: whether the risk is minimal, and whether the subjects are powerful actors or institutions, are among the factors for judging exceptions[^apsa2020humansubjects-e21]; and the effects and consent must be reported publicly[^apsa2020humansubjects-e16].
>
> Practical advice for newcomers: before designing any intervention that touches a real election or political process, discuss it with your advisor, the ethics committee, and colleagues outside your area, and report the effects and consent honestly in the paper `[Our analysis]`. For general informed consent and privacy protection, see [Research integrity and ethics](../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#4-when-research-involves-people-or-could-harm-others-protect-people-first) in Throughout the project.

## Common strategies and standards of evidence

A **research strategy** is the main logic a project uses to produce new knowledge; see [Key concepts](../../00_start-here/key-concepts.md#research-strategy) and [Research strategies](../../05_research-strategies/README.md). The table lists only the strategies our sources support; the grouping and summaries are our own synthesis, and each standard represents only the organization or journal in its footnote (mostly American) `[Our analysis]`. Our sources do not cover the standards of evidence for formal theory or interpretive research; for how to write proofs in formal theory, the computer science page on [Theoretical research](../computer-science/practices/theoretical_research.md) can serve as a reference.

| Strategy | What it looks like in political science | Common evidence and how it is judged | How to do it |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Causal explanation](../../05_research-strategies/explain_causality.md) (experiments and observational data) | Among studies that registered a pre-analysis plan, 63% are field experiments and 27% survey experiments[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e3] | The pre-analysis plan states all hypotheses and how variables are measured, with a verifiable timestamp[^egappreanalysis-e2], and says in advance how multiple testing will be handled[^egappreanalysis-e3]; departures from the plan are explained[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e7]; AJPS checks before publication that the results can be reproduced[^ajps2026verification-e4]; in general, do not compromise a political process without consent[^apsa2020humansubjects-e11] | [Field experiments](../../06_methods/field_experiment.md), [Controlled experiments](../../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md), [Quasi-experiments and causal identification](../../06_methods/quasi_experiment.md) |
| [Causal explanation](../../05_research-strategies/explain_causality.md) (within a case) | Process tracing: analyzing the processes and sequence of events within a case to develop or test hypotheses about causal mechanisms[^bennett2012process-e3]; the political scientist George introduced this usage in 1979[^bennett2012process-e2] | Explain how the evidence was produced, how conclusions were reached, and how participants were protected[^jacobs2021qualitative-e9]; when citing public sources, give enough location information, down to the page number[^jacobs2021qualitative-e16] | [Process tracing and comparative case studies](../../06_methods/process_tracing.md) |
| [Description](../../05_research-strategies/observe_describe.md) | When the inference is descriptive rather than causal, case studies are usually more useful[^gerring2004case-e7] | Likewise, explain how the evidence was produced and how conclusions were reached[^jacobs2021qualitative-e9] | [Case study and ethnography](../../06_methods/case_study.md), [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md) |
| [Measurement](../../05_research-strategies/measure.md) | Using survey experiments to measure sensitive attitudes, because asking directly underestimates them[^egapsurveyexperiments-e2] | Use indirect designs such as list experiments and endorsement experiments[^egapsurveyexperiments-e5]; survey results do not necessarily carry over to the real world[^egapsurveyexperiments-e7]. See [Survey experiments](#survey-experiments-measuring-sensitive-attitudes-inside-a-questionnaire) above | [Questionnaire surveys](../../06_methods/questionnaire_survey.md) (survey experiments) |

How to use this table `[Our analysis]`: political science is methodologically plural, and its standards of judgment are not uniform; some qualitative researchers even consider the concept of "transparency" incompatible with how they understand knowledge to be produced[^jacobs2021qualitative-e14]. First find your strategy from your central claim, then check what kind of evidence your target journal accepts; quantitative and qualitative research face different transparency expectations, see [Data Access and Research Transparency](#data-access-and-research-transparency-da-rt) above.

## Research methods common in this field

Political science has no method pages of its own; here is how its common methods map onto the cross-disciplinary method pages (the mapping and the notes on differences are our own synthesis `[Our analysis]`; the supporting sources are in the footnotes):

- **Field, lab, and survey experiments**: see [Field experiments](../../06_methods/field_experiment.md), [Controlled experiments](../../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md), and [Questionnaire surveys](../../06_methods/questionnaire_survey.md). Difference: interventions may affect real political processes, so you have to consider the impact on society as well[^apsa2020humansubjects-e11].
- **Case studies and process tracing**: see [Process tracing and comparative case studies](../../06_methods/process_tracing.md) and [Case study and ethnography](../../06_methods/case_study.md). Difference: the cases are often large-scale events such as states, wars, and reforms `[Our analysis]`.
- **Elite and field interviews**: see [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md). Difference: respondents may be officials, and you can choose not to promise confidentiality[^apsa2020humansubjects-e19]; in authoritarian or conflict settings, interview records themselves can endanger respondents[^jacobs2021qualitative-e10].
- **Quantitative analysis of observational data**: see [Quasi-experiments and causal identification](../../06_methods/quasi_experiment.md) and [Data science research](../../06_methods/data_science.md). Difference: at journals like AJPS, you must pass a replication check before publication[^ajps2026verification-e4].
- **Multi-method research and replication studies**: see [Mixed methods](../../06_methods/mixed_methods.md) (for example, cross-national statistics first, then selected cases in depth) and [Replication studies](../../06_methods/replication.md).

## Advice for newcomers

1. **From day one, organize everything so others can verify it.** Set a random seed for any analysis that uses randomness[^eubank2016lessons-e7]; before submitting, run your replication package on another computer (ideally with a different operating system)[^eubank2016lessons-e5], and check every number in the paper against the code output[^eubank2016lessons-e6].
2. **If you run an experiment, write a pre-analysis plan first.** Spell out the control variables and how you will handle missing data and attrition, which are exactly the things most often left out[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e6][^ofosu2023preanalysis-e8]; go ahead with unplanned analyses, but label them in the paper and explain why[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e14].
3. **Do a risk assessment before you go into the field.** Think through how risky the design is under the regime where you will work[^apsa2020humansubjects-e12], whether the timing, place, or wording of interviews could expose respondents[^jacobs2021qualitative-e10], and what local laws require[^apsa2020humansubjects-e17].
4. **If your data are restricted, say so early.** Journals that signed the Journal Editors' Transparency Statement require you to tell the editor at submission[^dart2014jets-e3]; at AJPS, you can request an exemption for the data, but code and other materials must still be provided[^ajps2026verification-e6].

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Changing the code without updating the paper, or copying results by hand incorrectly | At one journal, QJPS, of the 24 papers reviewed in-house since September 2012, 14 had results that did not match the authors' own code[^eubank2016lessons-e2]; Eubank thinks this is where most replication problems come from[^eubank2016lessons-e6] |
| Writing a vague pre-analysis plan that leaves out control variables and how missing data will be handled | It leaves room to pick analyses after the fact `[Our analysis]`; of the 195 plans from 2011–2016 that Ofosu and Posner sampled, 44% did not specify control variables[^ofosu2023preanalysis-e6] |
| Assuming that "anonymizing" makes it safe to publish full interview transcripts | In watched communities, details alone are enough to identify respondents[^jacobs2021qualitative-e10]; the risk can rise when the situation changes[^jacobs2021qualitative-e11] |
| Handing over respondents' material because a journal requires data sharing | Your duty to protect participants does not go away because a journal asks[^apsa2020humansubjects-e9] |
| Copying a design from democracies into an authoritarian setting unchanged | The same petition signature can expose participants to reprisals under an authoritarian regime[^apsa2020humansubjects-e12] |
| Assuming that research on officials in the US can go through ethics review under a "public official exemption" | APSA's 2020 principles point out that the new US rule no longer exempts research on elected officials and public officials[^apsa2020humansubjects-e5] (this is a US rule, in effect since January 2019 `[Our analysis]`) |

## Further reading

- **APSA, Principles and Guidance for Human Subjects Research (2020)**: 12 principles plus detailed guidance, covering power, consent, deception, harm, confidentiality, and impact on political processes. Read before doing research that involves people or real political processes.
- **APSA, A Guide to Professional Ethics in Political Science (2012 revision)**: item 6 is the source of the data access and research transparency obligations. Read to learn the discipline's professional norms.
- **Jacobs, Büthe, et al. 2021, The Qualitative Transparency Deliberations** (*Perspectives on Politics*; we used the author-accepted manuscript): the overview of the Qualitative Transparency Deliberations. Read before doing interview, archival, or ethnographic research.
- **EGAP methods guides** (pre-analysis plans, survey experiments, and more): free web guides from the EGAP research network. Read before designing an experiment.
- **Ofosu & Posner 2023, Pre-Analysis Plans: An Early Stocktaking**: the gaps in 195 pre-analysis plans registered in 2011–2016, plus a 2018 survey of 155 researchers on their costs. Read before writing your first plan.
- **Eubank 2016, Lessons from a Decade of Replications at the QJPS**: one journal's experience with replication review, and its advice to authors. Read before putting together a replication package.
- **The AJPS Verification Policy and its quantitative and qualitative verification checklists**: these apply to AJPS only; check against them before submitting to AJPS, and for other journals, read their own policies.
