# History and archaeology: field overview

This page explains how research actually runs in history and archaeology: what counts as "evidence" here, how to work in an archive, why the field centers on the monograph, and the ethical duties specific to oral history and archaeological excavation. For what applies in every field (choosing a topic, reading the literature, writing, research integrity), see the chapters of each Part listed in [The research journey at a glance](../../00_start-here/research-journey.md) and [Throughout the project](../../08_throughout/README.md); this page does not repeat them. For the research strategies the field commonly uses and the evidence each one relies on, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence); for how to do the work, see [Research methods common in this field](#research-methods-common-in-this-field).

Our sources are mainly the guidelines of American and British professional societies, an introductory website led by an American historian, and a 2012 interview study of 39 American historians. So when this page describes what a society requires, that is **the practice of that society in that country**, not a worldwide rule. Laws, archive systems, academic evaluation, and conventions differ from country to country; **follow the rules of your own country, institution, and the repository that holds your sources**. Also, history and archaeology are two disciplines whose norms come from different places: for history we rely mainly on the American Historical Association, and for archaeology on the Society for American Archaeology and the UK's Chartered Institute for Archaeologists. The page treats them separately; do not carry the practice of one straight over to the other `[Our analysis]`.

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- explain the difference between primary and secondary sources in history, and how to examine a source critically;
- know what to do before you visit an archive and what to record while you are there;
- understand why history centers on the monograph, and how a dissertation becomes a book;
- know your responsibilities to people, communities, and sites when doing oral history or archaeological excavation;
- name the research strategies the field commonly uses, and what evidence and standards each one is judged by;
- avoid common mistakes such as presentism, cherry-picking evidence, and reading sources out of context.

## How research works in this field

### What "evidence" means here

**The past no longer exists.** Science can run experiments and make field observations to describe the world in front of it; the past leaves only fragments[^cronon2009learning-e1]. Historical research means gathering those fragments and working out what they mean. So whatever you want to ask about the past, your second question should always be "What are the documents?"[^cronon2009learning-e2]

"Documents" here is a very broad term. The professional standards of the American Historical Association (AHA, the professional society of historians in the US) say that documents are all the surviving records of the past: written texts, artifacts, images, film, statistics, oral recollections, buildings and the natural environment, and more[^aha2023standards-e1].

**Primary and secondary sources.** Primary sources are the original documents through which we know the past; secondary sources are interpretations of the past that scholars and others have built from primary sources[^cronon2009learning-e26]. The AHA standards call this one of the most basic distinctions in history, but the line is not simple: whether a document counts as primary or secondary depends largely on the question you are asking of it[^aha2023standards-e2]. For example, a history textbook from the 1950s is a secondary source if you are studying the period it covers, but a primary source if you are studying how people in the 1950s told history `[Our analysis]`. **Manuscripts and archives.** Manuscripts are unpublished papers of individuals or organizations[^cronon2009learning-e12], such as letters and diaries. Each one is unique and is kept in an archive's collections[^cronon2009learning-e4].

**Source criticism**: do not treat any source as straightforward fact; first ask who wrote it, when, and why `[Our analysis]`. Specifically, you can ask:

- What was the author's background? What biases might they have?[^cronon2009learning-e27]
- Does the document really come from the period you are studying?[^cronon2009learning-e24]
- Who was the intended audience, and how did that shape what the author wrote and left out?[^cronon2009learning-e19]

Documents are always products of a particular time and place[^cronon2009learning-e5]. Even a document that is plainly lying can, once interpreted, tell you something important about the past[^cronon2009learning-e6]. Secondary sources need scrutiny too: are the author's claims supported by the evidence they give[^cronon2009learning-e21]?

**In archaeology, the evidence is material remains and records.** The UK's Chartered Institute for Archaeologists (CIfA, the professional body for archaeologists in the UK) defines archaeological excavation as intrusive fieldwork, meaning work that digs up and destroys the original state of a site, carried out with defined research objectives to examine, record, and interpret archaeological remains (deposits, features, and structures)[^cifa2023excavation-e2]. The ethics principles of the Society for American Archaeology (SAA) say that the archaeological record is a nonrenewable resource[^saa2024principles-e5], and that the collections and records produced by research (physical and digital) are themselves part of the archaeological record[^saa2024principles-e7]. So in archaeology, **how well you record decides whether the evidence survives at all** `[Our analysis]`.

**How this differs from "evidence" in the main Parts.** Much of what the Parts and the research strategies say about evidence and validity (for example, [Validity and limitations](../../02_do-the-project/04_validity-and-limits.md) and [Comparative evaluation](../../05_research-strategies/compare_evaluate.md)) is aimed mainly at quantitative, comparative research: set a baseline, control variables, hold out test data, repeat the experiment. Most historical research cannot be "run again" `[Our analysis]`; the AHA standards also say that the record of the past is fragmentary, so absolute historical knowledge is impossible[^aha2023standards-e14]. What stays the same: match every claim to its evidence, do not fool yourself, and report limitations honestly `[Our analysis]`. The corresponding requirements in the AHA standards are:

- **Do not pick and choose evidence.** You must not omit evidence that runs against your interpretation[^aha2023standards-e6].
- **Let others check your work.** Notes and bibliographies let other historians retrace every step of your argument and see whether each step is supported by sources[^aha2023standards-e3]. The sloppier your notes, the harder it is for colleagues to trust you[^aha2023standards-e4].
- **Accept that interpretations change.** Historians use a growing body of secondary scholarship to place primary documents in a wider context, so interpretations of the same record keep changing[^aha2023standards-e16]. Cronon and colleagues' introductory website (see the next section) gives an example: two scholars read the same banknote and built very different arguments from it[^cronon2009learning-e22].

### Working in an archive

An archive is not a library. Even the best-known manuscript collections are cataloged less thoroughly than a library[^cronon2009learning-e25]. The practices below come from an introductory website on historical research written by the environmental historian William Cronon and his graduate students:

1. **Check the rules before you go.** Whether you may bring a camera or a laptop depends on the repository's rules[^cronon2009learning-e15].
2. **Read the finding aid first.** A finding aid is the guide archivists prepare to help you navigate a manuscript collection[^cronon2009learning-e13]; read it before deciding which boxes to request `[Our analysis]`.
3. **Write down box and folder numbers.** Something you looked at but did not locate may be impossible to find again[^cronon2009learning-e14]. You will also need these locations later for your footnotes `[Our analysis]`.
4. **Let your question change with the sources.** Once you are in the archive, your research question will change[^cronon2009learning-e16]. Questions and documents should shape each other[^cronon2009learning-e9].

**Photographing is convenient, but it has a cost.** In 2012 the nonprofit research and consulting organization Ithaka S+R interviewed 39 American historians and graduate students[^rutner2012supporting-e1]. The report itself notes that the sample does not fully represent the historical profession[^rutner2012supporting-e9], and it reflects the situation in the US more than a decade ago. The report argues that capturing sources with digital cameras and scanners may be the most significant change in how historians do research[^rutner2012supporting-e2]. Interviewees often took large numbers of photographs in the reading room and looked at them only after they got home; some said they no longer read and thought about sources closely in the archive[^rutner2012supporting-e10]. Back home, they relied on elaborate folder structures and good memory to find their photos[^rutner2012supporting-e3]. One interviewee's habit is worth copying: photograph the call slip (the form you fill in to request material, which names the archive and the collection) together with the documents[^rutner2012supporting-e4]. For general ways to organize photos and notes, see [Organizing your research materials](../../08_throughout/organizing_materials.md) in Throughout the project.

**Keep three things apart in your notes**: verbatim quotation, paraphrase, and your own ideas. The AHA lists strictly distinguishing quotation from paraphrase as a basic rule of note-taking[^aha2023standards-e12]; Cronon and colleagues remind you to mark clearly which notes are your own ideas[^cronon2009learning-e11]. Before you start taking notes on a document, record its full bibliographic information[^cronon2009learning-e10].

### Writing: argument plus narrative

The goal of historical research is not to pile up material but to build an argument and tell a story[^cronon2009learning-e3]. In its most basic form, an argument is a claim supported by evidence[^cronon2009learning-e18]. Historians are also storytellers[^cronon2009learning-e17].

What the Part 4 chapter [Telling the story and writing the paper](../../03_write-and-publish/01_story-and-paper.md) says about stating your claim up front and organizing by logic applies to historical writing too `[Our analysis]`. The difference is that papers in many experimental fields have fixed sections, Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion (often abbreviated IMRaD), whereas history articles and monographs are usually divided into chapters by argument and narrative, with no fixed "Methods" or "Results" section `[Our analysis]`.

**Position your argument.** Working out how your view relates to those of other scholars is called "positioning your argument," and it can greatly increase the significance of your work[^cronon2009learning-e23]; see the Part 4 section [Related work: say where you stand](../../03_write-and-publish/01_story-and-paper.md#5-related-work-show-where-you-stand).

**Cite with footnotes.** The notes-bibliography system of *The Chicago Manual of Style* (a widely used American style manual) cites sources in numbered footnotes or endnotes[^chicago2024citation-e1]. It can accommodate all kinds of unusual sources[^chicago2024citation-e3], which is why many people in the humanities, including history, prefer it[^chicago2024citation-e2]. The manual's website also says most authors choose the system common in their field or required by their publisher[^chicago2024citation-e4]. So before you start writing, check the style requirements of your target journal or publisher `[Our analysis]`.

### Monographs, and turning the dissertation into a book

**History is a book-based discipline.** Those are the words of a 2013 AHA statement[^aha2013embargo-e1]. A monograph is a scholarly book by one or a few scholars on a single topic `[Our analysis]`. The points below all come from American sources and describe the situation in American academia; other countries may value books and articles differently and evaluate faculty for promotion differently, so ask your advisor first `[Our analysis]`.

- **Getting from dissertation to book takes years.** The AHA statement says it is not unusual for newly graduated historians to spend five or six years revising their dissertation into a book manuscript to submit to a press[^aha2013embargo-e2]. This usually involves expanding the research, sharpening the argument, and improving the writing[^aha2013embargo-e3].
- **Books are tied to jobs.** The same statement says many universities grant tenure (a permanent position from which you cannot easily be dismissed) only to junior faculty who have published a monograph within six years of the PhD[^aha2013embargo-e4]. The 39-person interview study mentioned above also found that, for these interviewees, the monograph was still central to tenure review[^rutner2012supporting-e6].
- **Think about whether to make your dissertation public right away.** The AHA statement says a growing number of university presses are reluctant to offer contracts to new PhDs whose dissertations are freely available online[^aha2013embargo-e5]. The AHA therefore encourages universities to allow history PhDs to embargo the electronic version of their dissertations for up to six years[^aha2013embargo-e6]. Before deciding whether to embargo, ask your advisor, your university library, and your target press `[Our analysis]`.
- **State the status of a manuscript accurately.** The wording the AHA standards recommend: an accepted, complete manuscript is "forthcoming"; one with a signed contract but no final manuscript submitted is "under contract"[^aha2023standards-e13]. Your CV must not list unwritten or unpublished books and articles as completed work[^aha2023standards-e20].

### Working alone or in a team

**History: traditionally mostly solo.** The 39-person American interview study mentioned above calls the typical mode of historical research the "lone scholar"[^rutner2012supporting-e8]. But collaboration is growing: AHA guidelines from 2021 say more and more historians are collaborating on monographs, scholarly editions, articles, exhibitions, and websites[^aha2021collaborators-e1]. These guidelines set no single rule for author order: it depends on the project, and can follow the size of each contribution or be alphabetical[^aha2021collaborators-e2]. The AHA standards also require that, however you order names, you acknowledge the help you received and give collaborators the credit they are due[^aha2023standards-e18]. For general principles of authorship, see [Collaborators and advisors](../../08_throughout/collaboration_and_advisors.md#6-authorship-discuss-it-at-the-start-and-adjust-as-you-go) in Throughout the project.

**Archaeology: excavation projects need the right specialists.** The UK CIfA excavation guidance says the project team must be able to draw on appropriate expertise and reference resources[^cifa2023excavation-e3]; the project design (the overall plan written before work begins) must set out staffing, including internal and external specialists[^cifa2023excavation-e5]. The SAA's ethics principles in the US also describe archaeology as a multidisciplinary field[^saa2024principles-e12]. At the journal level, we checked only one: *American Antiquity*, the SAA's journal. Its instructions for authors (Cambridge University Press website, consulted October 2026) require all authors to confirm that everyone listed qualifies as an author under the conventions of the discipline, and that no one who should be an author has been left out[^amantiquity2026instructions-e2]. On submission, the corresponding author must use CRediT to state each person's contribution. CRediT, the Contributor Roles Taxonomy, is a standard set of role names for saying exactly who did what. People who contributed but do not qualify as authors should also be described this way and acknowledged[^amantiquity2026instructions-e3]. Other archaeology journals may differ `[Our analysis]`. We found no reliable source on how authors are ordered or what the order means, so we draw no conclusion here. In practice `[Our analysis]`: excavation projects often draw on many specialists (see above), so it is best to agree with the project director at the start who will be an author and in what order, write it down, and check it again against the target journal's rules before you submit.

## Norms and ethics in this field

For general ethical principles, see [Research integrity and ethics](../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md) in Throughout the project. This section covers only what is specific to this field.

### History: the AHA Statement on Standards of Professional Conduct

These are the standards of an American society. Historical associations, universities, and archives in other countries have their own rules, but the integrity baseline these points describe is shared `[Our analysis]`.

- **Do not fabricate evidence**[^aha2023standards-e17]; **do not omit contrary evidence**[^aha2023standards-e6].
- **Do not "borrow" citations from secondary works.** Copying citations to primary sources you have not checked yourself, without crediting the secondary work you took them from, is also improper[^aha2023standards-e11].
- **Disclose special advantages.** Disclose funding, sponsorship, or privileged access to sources, especially when they might bias your conclusions[^aha2023standards-e7].
- **Settle confidentiality up front.** Confidentiality terms should be agreed before the research begins[^aha2023standards-e9]; any promise you make about how sources will be used must be kept[^aha2023standards-e8].
- **Be ready to make your sources available.** Document your findings and be prepared to make available the sources, evidence, and data you used, including records created through interviews[^aha2023standards-e15].
- **Protect archives for those who come after.** Do nothing that would prevent later researchers from accessing the sources[^aha2023standards-e10].

### Oral history: the narrator's consent and control

Oral history records people's first-hand accounts of the past through audio or video interviews `[Our analysis]`. The requirements below come from the 2018 ethics statement and best practices of the Oral History Association (OHA) in the US; practice and legal requirements may differ in other countries:

- **Get trained first.** People doing interviews for the first time should be trained first[^oha2018principles-e9].
- **Find a home for the interviews first.** At the start of the project, arrange a repository that will preserve the interviews[^oha2018principles-e7].
- **Document informed consent.** Before the interview, obtain the narrator's informed consent, that is, a record that they agreed to take part knowing what is involved[^oha2018principles-e1]. Explain the goals and risks, and that once made public, the interview may be used by anyone in any way[^oha2018principles-e3].
- **Do not promise what you cannot deliver**[^oha2018principles-e2]. For example, the OHA warns that legal processes such as subpoenas and freedom of information requests may make some restrictions set by narrators unenforceable[^oha2018principles-e10].
- **Mind the power relations.** Every interview involves power relations[^oha2018principles-e12]. During the interview, minimize harm, tell narrators they may decline to answer, and be honest about your institution and your position[^oha2018principles-e5].
- **Let narrators review.** Narrators may delete or restrict parts of the interview, correct errors, or choose delayed release or no release[^oha2018principles-e4].
- **Back up right away.** Export the original recordings as soon as possible after the interview, make several copies, and store them in separate places[^oha2018principles-e11].
- **Stay true to the narrator's meaning when you use the interview**, and do not quote out of context[^oha2018principles-e6]. Treat interviews as subjective sources, and verify factual statements in them where you can[^oha2018principles-e8].

A key difference from [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md): the OHA says oral history aims to put the narrator's account into the public record[^oha2018principles-e13], and narrators may choose a pseudonym in some circumstances[^oha2018principles-e14]. Many interview studies, by contrast, anonymize participants by default `[Our analysis]`. Whether you need approval from an ethics review board differs by country and institution; ask your own institution first `[Our analysis]`.

### Archaeology: stewardship of sites, and working with communities

Of the nine ethics principles the Society for American Archaeology (SAA) revised in 2024, the following matter most to a newcomer `[Our analysis: items selected by us]`. Other countries have their own laws and society guidelines:

- **Stewardship**: care for the archaeological record collaboratively, for the benefit of all people[^saa2024principles-e1], while respecting the rights and beliefs of Indigenous and descendant communities (the descendants of the people whose remains are studied) about how their heritage is represented[^saa2024principles-e2].
- **Collaborate with affected groups wherever possible**[^saa2024principles-e3], listen to them, and incorporate their knowledge and concerns[^saa2024principles-e6]; decisions about records and research should involve descendant communities[^saa2024principles-e13].
- **No commerce.** Do not buy or sell archaeological objects for profit, and do not authenticate them for commercial purposes[^saa2024principles-e4].
- **Use existing collections first; new excavation needs a justification.** Research designs should give priority to analyzing existing collections; new fieldwork must be justified and should conserve existing resources as far as possible[^saa2024principles-e11].
- **Archive and publish your records**[^saa2024principles-e8], and share results and data promptly, with open access where possible[^saa2024principles-e9]; but when publishing, protect sensitive information about the past and minimize unintended consequences[^saa2024principles-e10], for example by thinking about whether to publish a site's exact location `[Our analysis]`.

The UK CIfA's standard and guidance for excavation (aimed at its members) puts these requirements into the project documents: before work starts, write a project design detailed enough for all parties to understand and agree on what will be done[^cifa2023excavation-e7], specifying the recording methods[^cifa2023excavation-e4] and the archiving strategy[^cifa2023excavation-e8]; every excavation must produce a report and a stable, ordered, accessible archive[^cifa2023excavation-e1]; and the report should generally contain enough data and archive references for others to challenge its interpretations[^cifa2023excavation-e6]. If you excavate in another country, follow its laws and the requirements of its institutions `[Our analysis]`.

### Digital methods (briefly)

The 2012 US interview study of 39 people mentioned above notes that digital work such as a geographic information system (GIS, software for maps and spatial data) can rarely be done by a single historian, and calls for new forms of collaboration[^rutner2012supporting-e5]. For these interviewees, digital outputs were in most cases seen as part of, or a supplement to, a monograph[^rutner2012supporting-e7]. If you want a digital project to be your main output, first check with your advisor how your department recognizes it `[Our analysis]`.

## Common strategies and standards of evidence

A **research strategy** is the main logic by which a project produces new knowledge, determined by its central claim; see [Key concepts](../../00_start-here/key-concepts.md#research-strategy) and [Research strategies](../../05_research-strategies/README.md). The table below lists only the three strategies our sources support. The grouping and the summaries in the "What it looks like in this field" column are our own; the standards for history come mainly from the American Historical Association and Cronon and colleagues' introductory website, and those for archaeology from the SAA in the US and CIfA in the UK, and represent only these bodies `[Our analysis]`.

| Strategy | What it looks like in this field | Common evidence and standards | How to do it |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Interpretation and argument](../../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md) | The core of history: start from surviving documents and make a claim supported by evidence[^cronon2009learning-e18]; the goal is to build an argument and tell a story[^cronon2009learning-e3] | The evidence is primary and secondary sources, examined first through [source criticism](#what-evidence-means-here); do not omit contrary evidence[^aha2023standards-e6]; notes must let other historians retrace every step of the argument[^aha2023standards-e3]; accept that interpretations change with new scholarship[^aha2023standards-e16] | [Archival research](../../06_methods/archival_research.md), [Oral history](../../06_methods/oral_history.md) |
| [Description](../../05_research-strategies/observe_describe.md) | Archaeological fieldwork: examine, record, and interpret remains with defined research objectives[^cifa2023excavation-e2]; you can also analyze existing collections, which the SAA makes a priority for research design[^saa2024principles-e11] | The evidence is remains and excavation records; records must be archived, curated, and published[^saa2024principles-e8]; the report must contain enough data and archive references for others to challenge its interpretations[^cifa2023excavation-e6] | [Archaeological excavation and field survey](practices/archaeological_excavation.md) |
| [Synthesis](../../05_research-strategies/synthesize.md) | Historiography and literature reviews: survey earlier interpretations and work out how your view relates to them, that is, "position your argument"[^cronon2009learning-e23] | Examine secondary sources: are the author's claims supported by the evidence they give?[^cronon2009learning-e21] Our sources do not say whether to follow the fixed procedure of a systematic review | [Review studies](../../06_methods/review_study.md) |

How to use this table `[Our analysis]`:

- **Choose the main strategy by your central claim.** Historical research is often mixed: you first reconstruct an event from the archives, then argue for an interpretation. In that case the main strategy is interpretation and argument, and the reconstruction should meet the standards of description.
- **For the standards that interpretation and argument is judged by**, see the [Interpretation and argument](../../05_research-strategies/interpret_argue.md#2-what-evidence-is-enough) strategy page and the footnotes in the table above; for how to do the work, see [Archival research](../../06_methods/archival_research.md). Most requirements in the main Parts, such as setting baselines and controlling variables, do not apply to historical research; see [What "evidence" means here](#what-evidence-means-here) above. Our sources do not cover how comparative evaluation, causal explanation, measurement, building and improving, or proof are done in this field.

## Advice for newcomers

1. **Point your question at documents.** Once you have a question, ask right away, "What are the documents?"[^cronon2009learning-e2]
2. **Do not neglect primary sources.** They are harder to find and harder to read, so newcomers tend to avoid them[^cronon2009learning-e20].
3. **Do your homework before visiting an archive.** Check the rules, read the finding aid, and write down box and folder numbers[^cronon2009learning-e15][^cronon2009learning-e13][^cronon2009learning-e14]. When you take photos, capture the source information in the same shot[^rutner2012supporting-e4].
4. **When the evidence contradicts your hypothesis, change the hypothesis**[^cronon2009learning-e8]; see [Analysis and interpretation](../../02_do-the-project/07_analyze-and-interpret.md#7-when-the-evidence-doesnt-match-the-claim-change-the-claim).
5. **Write while you collect.** If you start writing while you are still finding documents, you will likely discover important documents you did not know you were missing[^cronon2009learning-e28].
6. **Before doing oral history or archaeology, read your country's professional society guidelines.** The OHA advises first-time interviewers to get trained first[^oha2018principles-e9]. In both cases, decide early where your materials will end up: the OHA requires you to arrange a repository at the start of the project[^oha2018principles-e7], and CIfA requires the project design to set out an archiving strategy[^cifa2023excavation-e8].
7. **Think early about what will happen to your dissertation.** The AHA says a growing number of American university presses are reluctant to sign dissertations that are already online[^aha2013embargo-e5]. If your goal is a book, find out your target press's position before making the dissertation public `[Our analysis]`.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Presentism: judging people in the past by today's standards | Presentism means interpreting the past through today's concepts and standards[^hunt2002against-e1], and it easily breeds a sense of moral superiority[^hunt2002against-e2]. Try to see their world through the eyes of people at the time[^aha2023standards-e5]. But present-day concerns are not wrong in themselves; they can also reveal forgotten historical experience[^hunt2002against-e4]. Keep the tension between the two[^hunt2002against-e3] |
| Picking only the sources that support your view | It is easy to fall into confirmation bias and see only what fits your expectations[^cronon2009learning-e7]; the AHA standards explicitly forbid omitting contrary evidence[^aha2023standards-e6] |
| Reading a document out of context | Documents are always products of a particular time and place[^cronon2009learning-e5] and must be interpreted in a wider context[^aha2023standards-e16] |
| Treating a primary source as fact itself | Documents can lie; ask about the author, the date, and the audience[^cronon2009learning-e6][^cronon2009learning-e27][^cronon2009learning-e24][^cronon2009learning-e19] |
| Copying citations to primary sources from someone else's book without crediting it | The AHA regards this as improper[^aha2023standards-e11] |
| Notes that do not separate quotation from paraphrase | This is a basic rule of note-taking[^aha2023standards-e12]; "my notes were unclear" is no excuse for plagiarism[^aha2023standards-e19] |
| Only taking photos in the archive, without recording locations or thinking | In the 2012 US interview study, interviewees had to rely on elaborate folders and good memory to find their photos afterward[^rutner2012supporting-e3], and some no longer thought about sources while in the archive[^rutner2012supporting-e10]; that leaves no chance to adjust your question on the spot[^cronon2009learning-e16] `[Our analysis]` |
| Promising a narrator "complete confidentiality" | The OHA warns that legal processes may make restrictions unenforceable[^oha2018principles-e10]; do not promise what you cannot deliver[^oha2018principles-e2] |
| Not planning recording and archiving before excavating | Excavation is intrusive (CIfA)[^cifa2023excavation-e2], and the records produced by research are themselves part of the archaeological record (SAA)[^saa2024principles-e7] |

## Further reading

- **Cronon et al., Learning to Do Historical Research: A Primer** (williamcronon.net/researching): a free introductory website on historical research covering asking questions, finding documents, taking notes, working with manuscripts, and writing arguments. If this is your first piece of historical research, start here.
- **AHA, Statement on Standards of Professional Conduct**: the American Historical Association's professional standards, covering primary and secondary sources, notes, plagiarism, and confidentiality promises.
- **OHA, Principles and Best Practices**: the Oral History Association's document, with an ethics statement, best practices, and information for narrators. Worth reading before you do oral history.
- **SAA, Principles of Archaeological Ethics**: the Society for American Archaeology's nine principles of archaeological ethics (revised 2024).
- **CIfA, Standard and guidance for archaeological excavation**: specific requirements for an excavation project, from the project design to the report and archive (British, but the items work as a checklist elsewhere too).
- **Hunt 2002, Against Presentism**: a short essay on two forms of presentism and how to respond to them.
- **The citation quick guide on *The Chicago Manual of Style* website**: examples of how to write footnotes and bibliography entries.
