# Archaeological excavation and field survey

This page covers how to do **archaeological fieldwork**: from writing a research design and starting with survey that leaves the ground undisturbed, through excavating by stratigraphy and recording unit by unit, to post-excavation assessment, archiving, and writing the report. The ethical principles of archaeology (stewardship of sites, respect for descendant communities, no commercial trading) and the basic requirements of the UK CIfA excavation standard are covered in the [History and archaeology field overview](../README.md#archaeology-stewardship-of-sites-and-working-with-communities) and are not repeated here. For general fieldwork practice such as safety, logistics, and instrument calibration, see the steps [Safety and logistics](../../../06_methods/field_study.md#step-4-safety-and-logistics) and [Instrument calibration](../../../06_methods/field_study.md#step-5-instruments-and-field-calibration) in Field observation studies, a cross-disciplinary research-type page whose examples come mostly from observing water, soil and rock.

**Scope.** This is a research practice specific to archaeology and applies only to archaeological fieldwork. In this handbook's scheme of strategies it mainly serves [Description](../../../05_research-strategies/observe_describe.md): recording what the remains are like, then interpreting them (see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](../README.md#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence) in the field overview). For what every field shares, such as research design, validity, and planning, see the Part 2 chapter [From research questions to an evidence plan](../../../02_do-the-project/01_question-to-evidence.md) and [Writing a research plan](../../../02_do-the-project/03_research-plan.md). **Strategies it usually serves**: the strategy pages do not list this page yet.

**First, where this page's sources come from.** They come from a few countries and one scholar. Each has its own scope, and none is a rule for the whole discipline. If you excavate in another country, permits, ownership of finds, and archiving follow local law; CIfA's guidance itself requires you to follow the law and local guidance of the place where you work[^cifa2023excavation-e12].

- **UK, CIfA**: four standards and guidance documents from the Chartered Institute for Archaeologists (CIfA, the UK professional body for archaeologists), covering field evaluation, excavation, geophysical survey, and archives. Reading each document's version notes, we take them to be requirements for **its members**, written for work in the UK and the Isle of Man[^cifa2020archives-e12].
- **England, Historic England**: the project management guidance of Historic England (the government heritage body for England). Research projects it **funds** must follow it; others can use it as a reference[^historicengland2015morphe-e1]. We also use its 2022 radiocarbon dating guidance (edited by Bayliss and Marshall), written for archaeological projects in England[^bayliss2022radiocarbon-e7]; this page draws only on its advice on choosing samples.
- **US, SAA**: the 2024 Principles of Archaeological Ethics of the Society for American Archaeology (SAA), which are that society's requirements for archaeologists.
- **US Department of the Interior, 1983 standards**: the standards and guidelines for archeological documentation that the US Department of the Interior published in the *Federal Register* in 1983, used in historic preservation at the US federal level (our reading of its version notes). They are more than forty years old, and some practices may have been updated since.
- **Harris's stratigraphy**: Edward Harris's *Principles of Archaeological Stratigraphy* (2nd edition, 1989), the book the "Harris Matrix" comes from. This is **a method developed by one scholar**, not the rules of any institution.

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- explain what question each of desk-based assessment, non-intrusive survey, field evaluation, and excavation answers, and why the ones that don't disturb the ground come first;
- write a project design that others can check your work against;
- excavate by stratigraphy, and for each stratigraphic unit assign a number, fill in a record sheet, draw a plan, and record its stratigraphic relationships;
- tell "the order of the layers" apart from "the date of the layers", and use finds to date layers correctly;
- know that a project is finished only after post-excavation assessment, archiving, and reporting.

## 1. What this kind of research is and when to use it

Archaeological fieldwork means going to a site to find, record, and interpret the remains of human activity that lie buried or on the surface. The biggest difference from other research is that **excavation destroys the evidence itself**. Harris says archaeological stratification is an irreversible process: once a unit of stratification is formed, it can only be altered or decay; it can never be made again[^harris1989principles-e1]. Soil you dig out can't be put back, so once the digging is over, your records are the only evidence left.

| Type of work | What question it answers | Does it damage the site? | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Desk-based assessment | What has been found here before; what historical records exist | No | We added this step; our sources do not cover it in detail |
| Non-intrusive survey (geophysical survey, etc.) | What might be underground | No | UK CIfA geophysics standard[^cifa2020geophysics-e1] |
| Field evaluation (may include trial trenching) | Whether there are archaeological remains in this area | Possibly | UK CIfA evaluation standard[^cifa2023evaluation-e1] |
| Excavation | Examining, recording, and interpreting remains, with defined research objectives | Yes | See the description of the CIfA definition in the [field overview](../README.md#what-evidence-means-here) |
| Collections research | Answering questions with existing finds and records | No | US SAA[^saa2024principles-e11] |

**How to decide which to do.** The sources point the same way: **if you don't have to dig, don't dig yet**. The SAA says a research design should give priority to analyzing existing collections, and new fieldwork needs to be justified (details in the [field overview](../README.md#archaeology-stewardship-of-sites-and-working-with-communities))[^saa2024principles-e11]. CIfA's evaluation guidance says non-intrusive methods should be considered first wherever possible, and intrusive techniques used only where necessary[^cifa2023evaluation-e2]. The US Department of the Interior's 1983 guidelines say not to use destructive methods where nondestructive ones will do[^nps1983standards-e7]. So first ask, "Can existing collections and records answer my question?" Then ask, "Can survey without digging answer it?" Excavate only after that.

## 2. How to do it

### Step 1: Write the research design and the project design

**How.** Before you start, put your aims, methods, recording system, sampling and dating strategy, and archiving plan into one document; CIfA's documents call it the project design. The US Department of the Interior's 1983 guidelines say archeological documentation can only be carried out once you have defined clear objectives and the methods for reaching them[^nps1983standards-e2]. CIfA's excavation guidance lists what a project design should contain. These three items bear most directly on research quality (we chose these items): methods of investigation, including environmental sampling and scientific dating strategies[^cifa2023excavation-e10]; methods of recording, including standards for collecting spatial data[^cifa2023excavation-e4]; and the archiving strategy, including the data management plan, which materials are kept, and the requirements of the local repository[^cifa2023excavation-e8]. Historic England's project management guidance (which applies to projects it funds) defines the "Initiation" stage as the design stage, and requires uncertainties, potential problems, and opportunities to be recorded in a **Risk Log**[^historicengland2015morphe-e2].

**Why.** The project design is the benchmark you check the work against afterward: when the work is done, you compare it with the design to see whether you did what you said (CIfA)[^cifa2023excavation-e9].

**Where people go wrong.** Copying a template. Both CIfA guidance documents warn that the project design must be specific to this project, and its methods must suit the nature, extent, preservation, and significance of the remains likely to be found; templates should be used with care[^cifa2023excavation-e9][^cifa2023evaluation-e3].

### Step 2: Start with survey that doesn't disturb the ground

**How.** Geophysical survey (some examples of our own: magnetometry, resistivity survey, and ground-penetrating radar) uses non-intrusive, nondestructive techniques to determine whether there are underground "anomalies" likely to be caused by archaeological remains[^cifa2020geophysics-e1]. You can use the geophysics results to decide where to put evaluation trenches or the excavation.

**Why.** It doesn't damage the site, which fits the "don't dig if you don't have to" principle above[^cifa2023evaluation-e2]. The US Department of the Interior's 1983 standards for archeological documentation also require nondestructive techniques where appropriate, and methods chosen so that the results can be verified if necessary[^nps1983standards-e1].

**Where people go wrong.**

- **Treating an anomaly as a feature.** CIfA's geophysics guidance says the cause of an anomaly often cannot be determined from the geophysical data alone, and the report should state how uncertain the inferences are[^cifa2020geophysics-e2].
- **Forgetting that geophysics needs a permit too.** This UK guidance notes that geophysical survey over scheduled monuments (monuments protected by law in the UK) and other protected places requires a Section 42 Licence[^cifa2020geophysics-e3].

### Step 3: Get permits, and consult landowners and communities

**How.**

- **Follow the law.** The SAA requires archaeologists to comply with all laws and regulations, and to work with affected groups wherever possible to reach outcomes both sides accept and benefit from[^saa2024principles-e3]. Which permits you need and which authority to apply to vary from country to country; follow the requirements of the local authorities, the receiving institution, and your advisor.
- **Excavating in China: qualification first, then approval.** Under China's Law on Cultural Relics Protection (文物保护法) as revised in 2024 (the English text on the National People's Congress English website)[^npc2024cultural-e1]: every archaeological excavation must go through approval; an entity that excavates must first hold an archaeological excavation qualification certificate issued by the cultural heritage administration under the State Council; and no entity or individual may excavate without approval[^npc2024cultural-e2]. For excavation undertaken for scientific research, the excavating entity submits its excavation plan to the cultural heritage administration under the State Council for approval[^npc2024cultural-e3], and the results must be reported truthfully[^npc2024cultural-e5]. So you cannot apply for an excavation permit as an individual; you have to work through a qualified entity. The official Chinese text and its implementing regulations are authoritative.
- **Find out who owns the finds.** Take the UK: in England, Wales, Northern Ireland, and the Isle of Man, finds belong to the landowner unless another law overrides this[^cifa2020archives-e11]. CIfA requires you to contact the museum that will receive the archive at the project planning stage, and to set out the deposition arrangements in the project design[^cifa2020archives-e10].
- **Consult descendant and local communities.** The SAA says many descendant and Indigenous communities regard the archaeological record as their heritage, and they should be involved in decisions about its documentation and study[^saa2024principles-e13]. The US Department of the Interior's 1983 guidelines say that responsiveness to local groups' concerns (such as Native American groups) during survey should continue into excavation, because excavation usually disturbs the site[^nps1983standards-e4].

### Step 4: Decide the excavation strategy and sampling plan

**How.**

- **Accept that you can't dig everything.** The US Department of the Interior's 1983 guidelines say an archeological investigation can rarely collect and record all possible data[^nps1983standards-e3]. So decide in advance where to dig and how much; CIfA requires the methods of investigation and the environmental sampling strategy to be written into the project design[^cifa2023excavation-e10].
- **Keep sampling consistent.** When a site is worked in several phases, it is best to keep the sampling design consistent (US Department of the Interior)[^nps1983standards-e6]; otherwise the data from different phases are hard to compare.
- **Process some environmental samples on site.** CIfA recommends processing environmental samples at the fieldwork stage where appropriate, to help assess the site's significance and preservation[^cifa2023excavation-e13].
- **Scale to significance.** The scale of post-excavation analysis, publication, and archive should match the significance and research potential of the remains (CIfA)[^cifa2023excavation-e21].

**You may adjust along the way, but keep a record.** The US Department of the Interior's 1983 guidelines say the research design should be applied in the field flexibly enough to deal with unexpected discoveries and changing site conditions[^nps1983standards-e5]. Historic England's guidance divides a project into execution stages, with a review against the aims at the end of each and an updated project design if needed[^historicengland2015morphe-e3]; it considers this flexible approach suited to the unpredictable nature of historic-environment research[^historicengland2015morphe-e4]. CIfA requires any change to be circulated within the project team and agreed in writing by all relevant parties[^cifa2023excavation-e11].

### Step 5: Excavate by stratigraphy and record unit by unit

This is the core of excavation. The practices below come from Harris's book and are a method he proposed himself; your organization's recording system may have its own rules.

**Two concepts first.** One is the **Law of Superposition**: in a sequence of layers as originally created, the upper units are younger and the lower units are older[^harris1989principles-e2]. The other is the **unit of stratification**: Harris divides these into two kinds, deposits (layers) and "interfaces"[^harris1989principles-e1]. An interface is, for example, the sides and bottom of a pit left by digging; British recording systems usually call each unit a context.

**How.**

1. **Dig by stratigraphy, not by fixed depth.** Harris says that where there is obvious stratification, the stratigraphic method of excavation must be used[^harris1989principles-e3]; digging in arbitrary levels on a site with clear stratification destroys the very primary data you are looking for[^harris1989principles-e4].
2. **Number every unit, using a single number series.** Work down from the latest units, numbering each one; a single series of numbers in the records is enough[^harris1989principles-e5].
3. **Fill in a sheet for every unit.** Stratigraphic relationships are most easily recorded on pre-printed sheets. Look for three relationships: which units lie above it, which lie below it, and which can be stratigraphically correlated with it as the same unit[^harris1989principles-e6].
4. **Plan before you dig.** Before excavating a unit, draw a plan of its surface. On complex sites with many overlapping deposits, plan every unit separately (single-layer plans)[^harris1989principles-e7].
5. **Build the stratigraphic sequence as you dig.** You can build up the stratigraphic sequence on paper as the excavation proceeds[^harris1989principles-e9]. This diagram is the Harris Matrix, which shows the order of units with boxes and connecting lines. If you build it as you go, you can go back and check contradictions while they are still in the ground.

**Why.** Only if each unit has its own plan can you use those plans to "reconstruct" the site after excavation and combine them into composite plans[^harris1989principles-e8].

**Where people go wrong.** Not every site needs a separate plan for every unit: Harris says that on sites with little stratification, a composite plan is enough[^harris1989principles-e8]. Organizations often have their own recording manuals with their own sheet layouts and rules for filling them in. One public example is the *Excavation Recording Manual* of the Surrey Archaeological Society in England (2010), written by this local society for its members, drawing on manuals including the Museum of London Archaeology Service's[^surrey2010excavation-e1]. It specifies that context numbers are issued from a single register and must never be duplicated (blocks of numbers can be reserved for different excavation areas), and that fields marked "Required" may not be left blank: if there is nothing to enter, write a dash or n/a[^surrey2010excavation-e2]. Each context record sheet records a description of the soil matrix (color, composition, texture, inclusions), the stratigraphic relationships, the numbers of the plans, sections, samples, and photos it appears in, an interpretation, and so on; stratigraphic relationships are entered reciprocally on all the relevant sheets: if 1 is above 2, then sheet 2 must record that it is below 1[^surrey2010excavation-e3]. We could not find a publicly available original of the Museum of London *Archaeological Site Manual*; for the details of filling in sheets, follow your own organization's manual.

### Step 6: Record photographs, drawings, finds, and samples

**How.** CIfA's archives standard requires: every drawing and photograph is marked with the project identifier and, where relevant, the subject, scale, location, and orientation[^cifa2020archives-e6]; paper and digital records use consistent, well-documented terminology[^cifa2020archives-e5]; finds are collected, sorted, cleaned, marked, recorded, and packed according to recognized standards[^cifa2020archives-e8]; and all digital material is backed up[^cifa2020archives-e7]. The US Department of the Interior's 1983 guidelines set two general requirements: field records should, as far as possible, be interpretable by others independently, and their format and level of detail should be consistent[^nps1983standards-e9].

**Where people go wrong.** Recording only the "useful" finds. Harris says that whether finds are indigenous, infiltrated, or residual, they must all be recorded the same way in the field, so that they can be told apart later[^harris1989principles-e11]. Also, CIfA's archives standard states plainly that health and safety take priority over archaeological requirements[^cifa2020archives-e13].

### Step 7: Dating evidence: first the sequence, then the dates

**How.**

1. **Use stratigraphy to establish the sequence first.** Harris says the absolute date of a unit can never change its position in the stratigraphic sequence[^harris1989principles-e13]. If the dates and the sequence disagree, go back and check whether the dates or the records are at fault.
2. **When dating a layer by its finds, look at the latest one.** Harris says the latest object in a layer is nearest to the date of the layer. It gives a terminus post quem (Latin for "limit after which"): the layer must be later than the date the object was made. This assumes the layer has been sealed against any later intrusion[^harris1989principles-e12].
3. **Watch for residual and infiltrated material.** Residual objects are much older than the layer they are found in; infiltrated objects are later than the layer and got mixed in afterward[^harris1989principles-e10].
4. **Plan scientific dating in advance.** CIfA requires the scientific dating strategy to be written into the project design[^cifa2023excavation-e10]. For choosing radiocarbon samples, Historic England's 2022 dating guidance gives several key points; it was written for archaeological projects in England[^bayliss2022radiocarbon-e7]:
   - **Assume a sample is residual until shown otherwise.** "Residual" means what the previous point described: much older than the layer it is in. Dating dates the sample itself, not the layer; only if the sample was buried when the layer formed can the stratigraphic order be used to check the dates[^bayliss2022radiocarbon-e2]. For every sample, state why it is related to the activity you want to date[^bayliss2022radiocarbon-e1]; unless you can show it was buried at the time, treat it as residual[^bayliss2022radiocarbon-e3].
   - **Date single, short-lived items.** Dating material known to come from a single organism (such as one cereal grain) carries the least risk of including residual material[^bayliss2022radiocarbon-e4]. For wood and charcoal, choose twigs or the outer rings of a tree: in a 300-year-old oak, the carbon at the heart of the tree was already 300 years old while the tree was still alive[^bayliss2022radiocarbon-e5].
   - **If there is no suitable sample, don't date.** Submitting second-rate material anyway gives dates that mislead more than they help[^bayliss2022radiocarbon-e6].

   **Radiocarbon results also need statistical modeling.** This guidance uses Bayesian chronological modeling: you combine the calibrated dates with other things you know about the samples, such as that they come from the same site, or the order given by the stratigraphy, to get date estimates that take all the evidence into account[^bayliss2022radiocarbon-e8]. The guidance lists modeling software that is free to use, such as OxCal and BCal[^bayliss2022radiocarbon-e9]. It stresses that modeling is a collaboration between archaeologists and modeling specialists, and depends on the skill, experience, and understanding of the archaeologists involved[^bayliss2022radiocarbon-e10]. For how to build a model, see the guidance itself.

### Step 8: Assess after excavation, then decide how to analyze

**How.** CIfA splits post-excavation work into two steps: first **assessment**, then **analysis**.

- After fieldwork (or during it, where suitable), assess the findings against the project design: how far the research aims have been or could be met, and whether there are new research questions to add to an updated project design[^cifa2023excavation-e14].
- The assessment report should state the quantity and perceived quality of the data in the site archive, its potential to answer the research aims, and recommendations for analysis and for data storage and curation[^cifa2023excavation-e15].
- If assessment turns up material that merits further study, update the project design, even if the research aims are unchanged[^cifa2023excavation-e16].
- The final output should integrate the results of the specialist analyses with the site's stratigraphic sequence, so that important data are not overlooked[^cifa2023excavation-e17].

**Why.** Get a clear view of what you have and what is worth doing before you spend time and money on analysis. The US Department of the Interior's 1983 guidelines add that data you don't analyze should still be recorded and preserved for future use[^nps1983standards-e8].

### Step 9: Put the archive in order and deposit it with a repository

**How.** First be clear what the archive includes. CIfA defines it as all parts of the archaeological record: finds, samples, and digital records, as well as written, drawn, and photographic documentation[^cifa2020archives-e1]. Before work starts, set out in the project design the selection strategy, the data management plan, and the repository where the archive will finally be deposited (CIfA)[^cifa2020archives-e4]; the US Department of the Interior's 1983 guidelines likewise require curation to be arranged at the planning stage[^nps1983standards-e11]. Historic England's guidance says the archive should be compiled and indexed as it is created, throughout the project[^historicengland2015morphe-e5], and in the end should allow independent third parties to query and check it[^historicengland2015morphe-e6].

**Why.** The project archive is the key to understanding the published interpretations[^cifa2020archives-e2]. CIfA says a project is not complete until the archive has been transferred successfully and is fully accessible[^cifa2020archives-e3]. The SAA also requires archaeologists to ensure, following current professional standards, that records are archived, curated, and published[^saa2024principles-e8].

### Step 10: Write the report and make it public promptly

**How.** An excavation report is not necessarily formally published; CIfA's archives guidance counts such reports as "grey literature" (reports that have not gone through formal publishing channels)[^cifa2020archives-e9]. It still needs to be written well:

- **Say where the archive is.** CIfA requires project reports (including grey literature and online entries) to name the repository holding the archive and give its accession number[^cifa2020archives-e9].
- **Report deviations and confidence.** CIfA's evaluation guidance requires the report to state the methods used, including any departures from the project design[^cifa2023evaluation-e4]; the conclusions should give a confidence rating for the techniques used, and recommendations for further work that could raise that confidence[^cifa2023evaluation-e5].
- **Let others relocate the areas and challenge the interpretation.** Plans should carry accurate spatial information so that future workers can locate the areas investigated[^cifa2023evaluation-e6]; the report should contain enough data and references to the archive for others to challenge its interpretations[^cifa2023excavation-e17].
- **Submit it and put it online.** CIfA requires that, subject to reasonable confidentiality agreements, the report be submitted to the appropriate national or local record within the timeframe set in the project design[^cifa2023excavation-e18], and the digital version be made available online[^cifa2023excavation-e19]. The SAA requires timely sharing and publication, with open access wherever possible[^saa2024principles-e9].

**Where people go wrong.** Publishing too much detail. The SAA requires you to consider protecting sensitive information when you publish[^saa2024principles-e10]; the US Department of the Interior's 1983 guidelines say that making information such as exact locations public may invite vandalism[^nps1983standards-e10]. One approach is to give only the general location in the public version, and keep the exact coordinates in the archive, accessible under the repository's rules.

## 3. Checklist

The items below are our own compilation from the UK CIfA standards and guidance (for its members), the SAA's ethics principles, the US Department of the Interior's 1983 guidelines, and Harris's method. In other countries, local rules take precedence.

**Must do**

- [ ] Before work starts, there is a project design specific to this project, setting out aims, methods, recording, sampling and dating, and archiving strategy (CIfA)[^cifa2023excavation-e10][^cifa2023excavation-e4][^cifa2023excavation-e8]
- [ ] Local laws are followed and all permits obtained (SAA, CIfA)[^saa2024principles-e3][^cifa2023excavation-e12]
- [ ] Where stratification is obvious, excavation follows the stratigraphy, and every unit is numbered with its above/below relationships recorded (Harris)[^harris1989principles-e3][^harris1989principles-e6]
- [ ] Drawings and photographs are marked with the project identifier, scale, location, and orientation (CIfA)[^cifa2020archives-e6]
- [ ] Changes to the project design are agreed in writing by all parties (CIfA)[^cifa2023excavation-e11]
- [ ] The project produces a report and a stable, ordered, accessible archive (CIfA)[^cifa2023excavation-e1]; the report names the repository and the accession number[^cifa2020archives-e9]

**Better practice**

- [ ] Existing collections are analyzed and non-intrusive methods considered before deciding to excavate (SAA, CIfA)[^saa2024principles-e11][^cifa2023evaluation-e2]
- [ ] Descendant and Indigenous communities are involved in the relevant decisions (SAA)[^saa2024principles-e13]
- [ ] The stratigraphic sequence diagram is built during excavation (Harris)[^harris1989principles-e9]
- [ ] Some environmental samples are processed at the fieldwork stage (CIfA)[^cifa2023excavation-e13]
- [ ] The digital report is put online, with open access wherever possible (CIfA, SAA)[^cifa2023excavation-e19][^saa2024principles-e9]

**Fine to skip; not a flaw**

- On sites with simple stratigraphy, you don't need a separate plan for every unit; a composite plan is enough (Harris)[^harris1989principles-e8].
- Not every excavation needs a monograph: analysis and publication only need to be scaled to the significance of the remains (CIfA)[^cifa2023excavation-e21].
- You may depart from general guidance, but you need a clear justification, formally documented (CIfA)[^cifa2023excavation-e20].

## 4. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Copying another project's design template | The project design is the benchmark for checking the work and must be specific to this project (CIfA)[^cifa2023excavation-e9][^cifa2023evaluation-e3] |
| Digging straight away when collections or geophysics could answer the question | Stratification can't be made again (Harris)[^harris1989principles-e1]; the SAA requires new fieldwork to be justified[^saa2024principles-e11] |
| Treating a geophysical anomaly as a feature | Geophysics alone often can't establish what caused an anomaly (CIfA)[^cifa2020geophysics-e2] |
| Digging by fixed depth when the stratigraphy is clear | It destroys the primary data you are looking for (Harris)[^harris1989principles-e4] |
| Recording only the "important" finds | All finds must be recorded the same way so they can be told apart later (Harris)[^harris1989principles-e11] |
| Taking an object's date as the date of the layer | A layer can only be later than its latest object, and only if nothing later has intruded; residual material must also be ruled out (Harris)[^harris1989principles-e12][^harris1989principles-e10] |
| Changing the plan on site at will, without a record | Changes must be agreed in writing by all parties (CIfA)[^cifa2023excavation-e11] |
| Thinking about who will take the archive only after excavation ends | Deposition must be arranged at the planning stage (CIfA)[^cifa2020archives-e10]; the project isn't complete until the archive is transferred[^cifa2020archives-e3] |
| The report gives interpretations without data or archive references | Others can't challenge or check it (CIfA)[^cifa2023excavation-e17] |
| The report publishes the exact location of the site | It may invite vandalism (US Department of the Interior 1983)[^nps1983standards-e10]; the SAA requires sensitive information to be protected[^saa2024principles-e10] |

## 5. Further reading

- **Harris, *Principles of Archaeological Stratigraphy* (2nd edition, 1989)**: free download from the author's website, harrismatrix.com. Covers units of stratification, superposition, plans, and the Harris Matrix; Chapter 12 is an outline for stratigraphic recording on excavations, and is a good place for beginners to start.
- **CIfA, the four standards and guidance documents on field evaluation, excavation, geophysical survey, and archaeological archives**: free download from archaeologists.net. These are UK professional requirements; their content lists for project designs and reports can double as checklists.
- **Historic England, *MoRPHE (Management of Research Projects in the Historic Environment) Project Managers' Guide* (2015)**: how to divide a research project into stages, set review points, and keep a risk log.
- **US Department of the Interior, *Secretary of the Interior's Standards and Guidelines for Archeology and Historic Preservation* (1983)**: free download from the US National Park Service website; the "Archeological Documentation" section covers research design, field recording, reporting, and curation.
- **Historic England, *Radiocarbon Dating and Chronological Modelling***: Historic England's 2022 guidance on radiocarbon dating and chronological modeling (edited by Bayliss and Marshall); consult it when you plan dating. This page draws only on its advice on choosing samples, not on the modeling sections.
- **Archaeology Data Service, *Guides to Good Practice***: published by the UK's digital archive for archaeology; covers how to create, organize, and archive digital data (this page does not draw on their content).
- For general principles of research design, see the Part 2 chapter [From research questions to an evidence plan](../../../02_do-the-project/01_question-to-evidence.md); for how to generalize from a single site, see [Case studies](../../../06_methods/case_study.md).
