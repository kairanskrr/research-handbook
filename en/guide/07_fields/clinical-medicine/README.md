# Clinical medicine: field overview

This page explains how clinical research actually runs. Because the subjects are people, ethics review, prospective registration, reporting guidelines, authorship, and conflicts of interest are all governed by firm written rules. Practices shared by every field (choosing a topic, study design, writing, peer review) are covered in the chapters of each Part listed in [The research journey at a glance](../../00_start-here/research-journey.md) and in [Throughout the project](../../08_throughout/README.md), and are not repeated here. This page covers only what is specific to clinical medicine. For the research strategies clinical medicine commonly uses and the evidence each one relies on, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence); for how to carry them out, see [Research methods common in this field](#research-methods-common-in-this-field).

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- name the checkpoints a clinical study must pass on its way from project start to publication;
- explain why a clinical trial must be registered before the first patient is enrolled;
- find the reporting guideline that matches your study design;
- understand what the "evidence pyramid" is good for and where it falls short;
- avoid the mistakes newcomers most often make with ethics, registration, authorship, and conflicts of interest.

## How research works in this field

### Your subjects are people, so ethics comes first

Clinical research studies people. It must follow the Declaration of Helsinki, the ethical principles drawn up by the World Medical Association (WMA) for medical research involving human participants, including research on identifiable human material or data[^wma2024helsinki-e1]. The current version is the 2024 revision[^icmje2026recommendations-e11]. Its bottom line: the goals of research must never take precedence over the rights and interests of individual participants[^wma2024helsinki-e2].

In practice, this means a few hard checkpoints:

- **Get research ethics committee approval before the study starts.** The protocol must be submitted to a research ethics committee for review and approval before the study begins[^wma2024helsinki-e4]. For international research, ethics committees in both the sponsoring country and the host country must approve it[^wma2024helsinki-e5].
- **Informed consent must be explained clearly and documented.** In plain language, tell potential participants the aims, methods, possible benefits, risks and burdens, sources of funding, conflicts of interest, and so on[^wma2024helsinki-e6]; then a physician or another qualified person obtains their freely given consent and formally documents it, on paper or electronically[^wma2024helsinki-e7].
- **Medical journal editors require this too.** The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) requires that research involving people be planned, conducted, and reported in line with the 2024 Declaration of Helsinki[^icmje2026recommendations-e11]. For general ethical principles, see [Research integrity and ethics](../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#4-when-research-involves-people-or-could-harm-others-protect-people-first) in Throughout the project.

### Physician and researcher: keep the two roles apart

A physician may also be the researcher, and the participants may be the physician's own patients. The Declaration sets specific limits on this: physicians may involve their own patients in research only if the study has potential preventive, diagnostic, or therapeutic value and the physician has good reason to believe that taking part will not harm the patients' health[^wma2024helsinki-e3].

Patients depend on their doctors and may find it hard to say no `[Our analysis]`. So the Declaration requires that when a participant is in a dependent relationship with the researcher, or may consent under pressure, consent should be sought by an appropriately qualified person who is independent of that relationship[^wma2024helsinki-e8].

### Register before you enroll the first patient

This is one of the most distinctive rules in clinical medicine `[Our analysis]`. The Declaration requires that medical research involving human participants be registered in a publicly accessible database before the first participant is recruited[^wma2024helsinki-e9]. The ICMJE recommends that all medical journal editors require, as a condition of considering a trial for publication, registration in a public trial registry at or before the time the first patient gives consent to enroll[^icmje2026recommendations-e15].

Some common misunderstandings:

- **"Clinical trial" is broader than you think.** The ICMJE defines it as any research project that prospectively assigns people to an intervention to study the relationship between that intervention and a health outcome, with or without a comparison or control group[^icmje2026recommendations-e16]. Interventions are not only drugs, surgery, and devices; they also include behavioral treatments, educational programs, dietary interventions, quality improvement interventions, process-of-care changes, and more[^icmje2026recommendations-e17].
- **Ethics approval is not registration.** They are two separate things, and you need both[^icmje2026recommendations-e18].
- **Registering after the fact is useless.** Registration exists to prevent selective publication and selective outcome reporting, to avoid unnecessary duplication of research, and to let patients and the public know which trials are planned or under way that they could join[^icmje2026recommendations-e19]; registering at submission time achieves none of these[^icmje2026recommendations-e20].
- **For non-trial studies (observational studies, for example), the ICMJE encourages but does not require registration.** The reason is that the exposure or intervention in such studies (smoking, for example) is not assigned by the researcher[^icmje2026recommendations-e21]. Note that the Declaration's registration requirement covers all medical research involving human participants[^wma2024helsinki-e9], which is broader than what the ICMJE makes mandatory `[Our analysis]`.

Why so strict? Ioannidis argued in a 2005 essay that the more flexibility a field allows in designs, definitions, outcomes, and analyses, the less likely its findings are to be true, and the easier it is to turn "negative" results into "positive" ones[^ioannidis2005why-e1]. He suggested that for designs such as randomized trials, registering in advance might help[^ioannidis2005why-e3]. This is an essay, and its conclusions come from a probability model, not from counting published studies; when you cite it, do not say it "proved that most medical research is false" `[Our analysis]`.

### Publishing is mostly in journals, and the rules are fairly uniform

Clinical medicine publishes mainly in journals `[Our analysis]`. The ICMJE's *Recommendations for the Conduct, Reporting, Editing, and Publication of Scholarly Work in Medical Journals* (the "ICMJE Recommendations" below) is a public rule document. Many journals that are not ICMJE members adopt it voluntarily, but the ICMJE cannot enforce it; authors should use it together with each journal's instructions for authors[^icmje2026recommendations-e1]. So reading the ICMJE Recommendations once gives you many of the basic requirements that medical journals share `[Our analysis]`.

**Preprints** (manuscripts made public before formal publication): policies vary among medical journals `[Our analysis]`. Check the target journal's policy before posting one[^icmje2026recommendations-e13], and when you submit, tell the journal about the preprint and give its link[^icmje2026recommendations-e14].

**Make all results public, including negative ones.** The Declaration requires negative and inconclusive results to be made public just like positive ones[^wma2024helsinki-e10]. The ICMJE Recommendations note that some consider failure to publish the results of clinical trials and other human studies a form of research misconduct[^icmje2026recommendations-e12]. Why you should report all your results is covered in [Research integrity and ethics](../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#2-be-honest-with-the-evidence-dont-fool-yourself-report-all-results) in Throughout the project.

### Projects are long and often multicenter

Clinical research moves slowly `[Our analysis]`: a cohort study follows a group of people over time to see whether outcomes occur[^vandenbroucke2007strobe-e1]; a trial takes time at every step, from writing the protocol, ethics review, and registration to recruitment and follow-up, and how long depends on the study `[Our analysis]`.

Large studies are often run by many hospitals, sometimes across countries `[Our analysis]`. This affects both authorship and ethics: large multi-author groups should ideally decide who will be an author before the work starts[^icmje2026recommendations-e6]; contributors who do not qualify for authorship can be acknowledged under a group name such as "Clinical Investigators", with each person's contribution specified[^icmje2026recommendations-e8]; and international studies need approval from ethics committees in several countries[^wma2024helsinki-e5].

## Norms and reporting standards in this field

### Reporting guidelines: pick the checklist for your study type

A **reporting guideline** is a checklist, flow diagram, or structured text, developed with explicit methods, that guides authors in reporting a particular type of study[^equatorreporting-e3]. It lists the minimum information a paper must make clear[^equatorreporting-e2]. The ICMJE encourages journals to ask authors to follow reporting guidelines[^icmje2026recommendations-e24].

To find a reporting guideline, go to the EQUATOR Network (Enhancing the QUAlity and Transparency Of health Research). It is an international initiative that seeks to improve the reliability of the health research literature by promoting transparent, accurate reporting and robust reporting guidelines[^equatorreporting-e1].

| Study design | Reporting guideline | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Randomized trials | CONSORT 2025 | A 30-item checklist plus a participant flow diagram[^hopewell2025consort-e1] |
| Trial protocols | SPIRIT (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials) | Specifically covers how to write a trial protocol[^hopewell2025consort-e4] |
| Observational studies (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional) | STROBE | A 22-item checklist[^vonelm2007strobe-e4] covering all three designs[^vonelm2007strobe-e2] |
| Systematic reviews and meta-analyses | PRISMA 2020 | A 27-item checklist[^page2021prisma-e1] |
| Diagnostic accuracy studies | STARD (Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies) | See the list in the ICMJE Recommendations[^icmje2026recommendations-e25] |
| Case reports | CARE (CAse REport guidelines) | A 13-item checklist[^gagnier2013care-e5] |

Two things to keep in mind:

- **Reporting guidelines govern how you write, not how you do the study.** CONSORT 2025 does not tell you how to design, conduct, or analyze a trial[^hopewell2025consort-e6]; STROBE states explicitly that it does not prescribe how to design a study[^vonelm2007strobe-e3]; nor does PRISMA[^page2021prisma-e2]. That said, the PRISMA authors note that becoming familiar with it while planning and conducting a review helps make sure you record everything you will need[^page2021prisma-e2].
- **A reporting guideline is not a quality score.** Meeting the checklist does not mean the study was done well[^hopewell2025consort-e7][^vonelm2007strobe-e3].

### Authorship: the ICMJE's four criteria

The ICMJE requires that authors meet **all four** of the following[^icmje2026recommendations-e2]:

1. Substantial contributions to the conception or design of the work, or to the acquisition, analysis, or interpretation of data;
2. Drafting the work or revising it critically for important intellectual content;
3. Final approval of the version to be published;
4. Agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work.

Everyone listed as an author should meet all four criteria, and everyone who meets all four should be listed as an author[^icmje2026recommendations-e3]. Acquiring funding, general supervision or administrative support, or writing assistance and language editing alone do not qualify someone for authorship[^icmje2026recommendations-e7]. Who is an author is for the people doing the work to decide, ideally at the planning stage, adjusting as the work progresses[^icmje2026recommendations-e4]; disputes are investigated by the institution where the work was done, not by journal editors[^icmje2026recommendations-e5]. For general principles of authorship, see [Research integrity and ethics](../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#5-authorship-citation-and-conflicts-of-interest-give-every-contributor-due-credit) in Throughout the project.

### Conflicts of interest: the appearance counts too

A **conflict of interest** exists when your professional judgment about a primary interest (a patient's welfare, the validity of the research) may be influenced by a secondary interest (financial gain, for example). The ICMJE stresses that the appearance of a conflict matters as much as an actual one[^icmje2026recommendations-e9]. Financial relationships such as employment, consultancies, stock ownership, honoraria, patents, and paid expert testimony are the easiest to identify and the most often considered potential conflicts[^icmje2026recommendations-e10]. Ioannidis also argues that conflicts of interest and prejudice may increase bias[^ioannidis2005why-e2]. Funding sources and conflicts of interest must both be declared in the paper[^wma2024helsinki-e10].

### Data sharing statements

Since July 1, 2018, clinical trial papers submitted to ICMJE journals must include a **data sharing statement**; trials that begin enrolling on or after January 1, 2019, must include a data sharing plan when they register[^icmje2026recommendations-e22]. The statement must say whether deidentified individual participant data, including data dictionaries, will be shared ("undecided" is not an acceptable answer); what data will be shared; whether the protocol and statistical analysis plan will be available; when the data will become available and for how long; and on what terms they can be accessed[^icmje2026recommendations-e23]. CONSORT 2025 also added a new "open science" section that brings registration, protocol, data sharing, and conflicts of interest together in one place in the report[^hopewell2025consort-e5].

## Research methods common in this field

Clinical medicine has no method pages of its own; the methods it commonly uses are cross-field ones: [Randomized controlled trials](../../06_methods/randomized_controlled_trial.md), [Observational epidemiology](../../06_methods/observational_epidemiology.md), and [Systematic reviews and meta-analysis (health research)](../../06_methods/meta_analysis.md). Each design in one sentence:

| Design | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| Randomized controlled trial | Participants are randomly assigned to different interventions and their outcomes compared. When appropriately designed, conducted, analyzed, and reported, it is generally regarded as the highest-quality evidence for evaluating healthcare interventions[^hopewell2025consort-e2] |
| Cohort study | Record a group of people's exposures (whether they smoke, for example), follow them over time, and see whether outcomes occur[^vandenbroucke2007strobe-e1] |
| Case-control study | Compare past exposures of people who have a disease (cases) with people who do not (controls)[^vandenbroucke2007strobe-e2] |
| Cross-sectional study | Survey a sample at a single point in time, often to estimate the prevalence of exposures, risk factors, or disease[^vandenbroucke2007strobe-e3]; it is hard to establish that the exposure came before the disease[^vandenbroucke2007strobe-e4] |
| Case report | A report on the care of a single patient, a sample size of one[^gagnier2013care-e7]; it can generate hypotheses[^gagnier2013care-e1] but can rarely establish causality[^gagnier2013care-e6] |
| Systematic review and meta-analysis | A systematic review is a process of selecting existing studies according to a defined procedure; a meta-analysis statistically combines the results of several studies into a single effect size[^murad2016new-e7]. For how to do one, see [Systematic reviews and meta-analysis (health research)](../../06_methods/meta_analysis.md) and [Review studies](../../06_methods/review_study.md); the process carries over `[Our analysis]`; in clinical medicine, report according to PRISMA 2020[^page2021prisma-e1] |

**Randomized trials cannot answer every question.** Observational studies are better at detecting rare or late adverse effects, and better reflect how treatments work in everyday medical practice[^vonelm2007strobe-e1]. Case reports have their place too: the link between thalidomide and birth defects was first discovered through case reports[^gagnier2013care-e2].

In observational research, tell **bias** (a flaw in the information collected or in how participants were selected, producing a wrong association) apart from **confounding** (the association is real, but an unaccounted-for factor is related to both the exposure and the outcome, so it cannot be read as causal)[^vandenbroucke2007strobe-e5]. For how to deal with them, see [Observational epidemiology](../../06_methods/observational_epidemiology.md#step-5-handle-confounding-draw-a-causal-diagram-first-then-decide-what-to-adjust-for).

### The evidence pyramid: useful, but do not apply it mechanically

The traditional evidence pyramid ranks study designs: basic research and case series at the bottom, case-control and cohort studies in the middle, randomized controlled trials above them, and systematic reviews and meta-analyses at the very top[^murad2016new-e1].

But Murad and colleagues point out that design alone is not enough to judge risk of bias: methodological limitations, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, and other factors are independent of design and can affect the quality of evidence from any design[^murad2016new-e2].

- **A strong design does not guarantee strong evidence.** In their example, even five randomized trials should not be rated high-quality evidence when they have these problems[^murad2016new-e3].
- **A weak design can still give strong evidence.** The benefit of hip replacement has never been tested in randomized trials, yet the quality of that evidence was rated up[^murad2016new-e4].
- **A review is only as good as the studies it includes.** A meta-analysis of randomized trials at low risk of bias and a meta-analysis of observational studies at high risk of bias cannot be treated as equivalent[^murad2016new-e5]. Murad and colleagues therefore propose taking systematic reviews off the top of the pyramid and using them as a "lens" through which to view the other studies[^murad2016new-e6].

## Common strategies and standards of evidence

A **research strategy** is the main logic by which a project produces new knowledge, chosen from its central claim; see [Key concepts](../../00_start-here/key-concepts.md#research-strategy) and [Research strategies](../../05_research-strategies/README.md). The table below lists three strategies clinical medicine commonly uses. The grouping and the "What it looks like in clinical medicine" column are our own summary; our sources do not cover the other strategies `[Our analysis]`.

| Strategy | What it looks like in clinical medicine | Common evidence and standards | How to do it |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Causal explanation](../../05_research-strategies/explain_causality.md) | Evaluate whether an intervention improves health outcomes. Randomized trials that are appropriately designed, conducted, analyzed, and reported are generally regarded as the highest-quality evidence for evaluating healthcare interventions[^hopewell2025consort-e2] | Register before recruiting the first participant[^wma2024helsinki-e9]; report according to CONSORT 2025[^hopewell2025consort-e1]; with observational data, tell bias apart from confounding[^vandenbroucke2007strobe-e5] | [Randomized controlled trials](../../06_methods/randomized_controlled_trial.md), [Observational epidemiology](../../06_methods/observational_epidemiology.md) |
| [Description](../../05_research-strategies/observe_describe.md) | Cross-sectional studies estimate prevalence[^vandenbroucke2007strobe-e3]; case reports describe the care of a single patient[^gagnier2013care-e7] | In cross-sectional studies it is hard to establish that the exposure came before the disease[^vandenbroucke2007strobe-e4]; case reports can generate hypotheses but can rarely establish causality[^gagnier2013care-e1][^gagnier2013care-e6]; report according to STROBE[^vonelm2007strobe-e2] and CARE[^gagnier2013care-e5] respectively | [Observational epidemiology](../../06_methods/observational_epidemiology.md) |
| [Synthesis](../../05_research-strategies/synthesize.md) | Systematic reviews and meta-analyses[^murad2016new-e7] | Report according to PRISMA 2020[^page2021prisma-e1]; the strength of the evidence depends on the quality of the included studies, not just their design[^murad2016new-e5] | [Systematic reviews and meta-analysis (health research)](../../06_methods/meta_analysis.md) |

How to use this table `[Our analysis]`: start from your central claim to find the strategy. "Comparing two treatments" looks like [Comparative evaluation](../../05_research-strategies/compare_evaluate.md), but questions about the effects of interventions are usually framed with PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome)[^higgins2024cochrane-e5], and in clinical medicine they usually have to be designed to the standards of Causal explanation. When you cite levels of evidence, look at study quality, not just the name of the design; see [The evidence pyramid](#the-evidence-pyramid-useful-but-do-not-apply-it-mechanically) above.

## Advice for newcomers

1. **Put both ethics approval and trial registration in your timeline, before recruitment.** You need both, and neither substitutes for the other[^icmje2026recommendations-e18].
2. **Open the matching reporting guideline when you write the protocol.** Spelling out key methods such as the primary outcome in the protocol makes quiet changes after the fact less likely[^hopewell2025consort-e3]; you can write a trial protocol following SPIRIT[^hopewell2025consort-e4].
3. **Agree on authorship when the project starts.** Use the ICMJE's four criteria[^icmje2026recommendations-e4]; settle it early, especially in multicenter projects[^icmje2026recommendations-e6].
4. **If your participants are your own patients, have someone else obtain informed consent.** Ask a qualified person who is independent of that relationship to obtain consent[^wma2024helsinki-e8].
5. **Make sure your paper matches your registration record.** If anything changed, say so and explain what changed[^hopewell2025consort-e8].

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Registering the trial only at submission | Registration after the fact serves none of registration's purposes[^icmje2026recommendations-e20], and ICMJE journals may not consider the paper for publication[^icmje2026recommendations-e15] |
| Assuming educational programs or process improvements are not "clinical trials" | The ICMJE's definition of an intervention includes them[^icmje2026recommendations-e17] |
| Adding your advisor or department head as an honorary author | General supervision or acquiring funding alone does not qualify for authorship[^icmje2026recommendations-e7] |
| Obtaining informed consent from your own patients yourself | When there is a dependent relationship, an independent qualified person should obtain consent[^wma2024helsinki-e8] |
| Writing "undecided" in the data sharing statement | The ICMJE does not accept this answer[^icmje2026recommendations-e23] |
| Citing Ioannidis 2005 as having "proved that most research is false" | It is a model-based argument, not an empirical measurement `[Our analysis]` |

## Further reading

- **The Declaration of Helsinki (WMA, 2024 version)**: the foundational document of clinical research ethics. Read it before you do any research involving people.
- **The ICMJE Recommendations**: recommendations on authorship, conflicts of interest, trial registration, data sharing, and preprints, adopted by many medical journals. Read them before you submit, especially Part II and Section III.L.
- **CONSORT 2025, STROBE, PRISMA 2020, CARE**: for randomized trials, observational studies, systematic reviews, and case reports respectively. STROBE has a companion explanation paper (Vandenbroucke et al. 2007) worth reading alongside it.
- **Murad et al. 2016, New evidence pyramid**: very short; explains the limits of the evidence pyramid. Good for anyone who wants to understand "levels of evidence" properly.
- **Ioannidis 2005, Why Most Published Research Findings Are False**: on how flexibility in study design and conflicts of interest affect how credible conclusions are. Keep in mind as you read that it is a model-based argument.
