# Sociology: field overview

This page explains how research actually runs in sociology: the main approaches (analyzing large existing survey data, running your own survey, ethnography, in-depth interviews, comparative-historical research), where theory fits into a study, a publishing culture in which books and long journal articles coexist, the ethics of research with people, data archives and data reuse, and the debates on both the quantitative and qualitative sides about transparency and replication. For what applies in every field (choosing a topic, study design, writing, peer review, research ethics), see the chapters of each Part listed in [The research journey at a glance](../../00_start-here/research-journey.md) and [Throughout the project](../../08_throughout/README.md); this page does not repeat them. For the research strategies sociology commonly uses and the evidence each one relies on, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence).

Our sources are mainly the ethics documents of the American Sociological Association and the British Sociological Association, one US data archive, the submission policies of two sociology journals, the tenure requirements of one US university sociology department, and reviews and editorials by a few US sociologists. When this page describes the requirements of an association or a journal, it means **the practice of that organization, in that country**, not a rule that holds worldwide; in other countries and institutions, local rules come first. Also, "5.9 Other social sciences" in the field classification of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) does not have its own field overview. If you work in one of those interdisciplinary areas, read this page first, then the neighboring overviews for [Psychology](../psychology/README.md), [Economics](../economics/README.md), and [History and archaeology](../history-archaeology/README.md). `[Our analysis]`

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- explain what data each of sociology's main approaches relies on, and what each has to watch out for;
- know what the professional associations require on informed consent, confidentiality, and protecting vulnerable groups when you run surveys, interviews, or ethnography;
- use data archives to find and cite data, and know how to archive your own data;
- understand why quantitative and qualitative sociologists keep arguing about "transparency and replication";
- avoid the mistakes newcomers commonly make.

## How research works in this field

### The main approaches

Sociology uses many research methods. The grouping below is our own summary, not a formal classification `[Our analysis]`:

| Approach | Roughly what you do | What to watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Analyzing large existing survey data (secondary analysis) | Use data that others collected and archived publicly to answer your own question. Freese and Peterson point out that most quantitative social science uses data the authors did not collect themselves, but that were built as large public resources for the research community[^freese2017replication-e11] | A long chain of steps separates the data source from your analysis, and the original data can themselves contain errors. When someone questioned the conclusion that "Americans are becoming more isolated," it turned out that the survey center supplying the data had miscoded 41 people as having "no one to confide in"[^freese2017replication-e4] |
| Designing and fielding your own questionnaire | See "Research methods common in this field" below | Sampling and question wording; if you want to compare across years, keep the item wording unchanged (see "longitudinal studies" below) `[Our analysis]` |
| Ethnography / fieldwork | Spend a long time in a community or organization, observing, participating, and taking notes `[Our analysis]` | The British Sociological Association warns that in long-term fieldwork, participants can easily forget they are being studied[^bsa2017statement-e4]; Freese and Peterson point out that field notes lose most of their value once they leave the person who wrote them[^freese2017replication-e20] |
| In-depth interviews | Hold long, open-ended interviews with a set of people and draw out meanings and mechanisms from them `[Our analysis]` | For how to choose interviewees and when to stop, see "Research methods common in this field" below |
| Comparative-historical research | Compare a small number of countries, regions, or periods to explain some social change `[Our analysis]` | For standards of judgment, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence) below; for how to work in archives, see the [History and archaeology page](../history-archaeology/README.md#working-in-an-archive) |

### Where theory fits

A sociology paper doesn't just report "what we found"; it also has to say what the finding means for understanding society more generally `[Our analysis]`. Two journals' statements are worth looking at, but each speaks only for itself:

- The *American Sociological Review* (ASR), the flagship journal of the American Sociological Association, says in its submission guidelines that its articles typically combine strong research design and analytic methods, a framing of the question that speaks to a general audience, and **theoretical development that goes beyond any one subfield of sociology**[^asrsubmission-e1].
- The open-access journal *Sociological Science*, by contrast, says neither theory nor evidence should be privileged; papers that mainly solve empirical puzzles have the same standing as papers that mainly address conceptual puzzles[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e11].

Theory also determines how far your conclusions reach. Freese and Peterson's inference is that if general sociological propositions hold only under certain conditions, then empirical studies test not so much the proposition itself as **the scope within which it applies**[^freese2017replication-e14]. For a newcomer, this means: when you write your conclusions, say "for which people, in which period, under which conditions" they hold `[Our analysis]`.

### Long projects, with books and articles side by side

Freese and Peterson note that many research projects run for a long time, so you need to keep records that both your collaborators and your future self can understand[^freese2017replication-e8].

Sociology publishes both monographs and journal articles `[Our analysis]`. An example from one specific department: the tenure requirements of the Wesleyan University sociology department in the US, revised in 2015, state that the main body of work can be either a book or a series of articles[^wesleyan2015sociology-e1]; if it is a book, the department normally expects it to be published, or accepted for publication, by a reputable press[^wesleyan2015sociology-e2]. This is the rule of one department at one US university and can't be generalized to other schools. Our sources do not give figures on the relative share of books and articles in sociology; which form counts for more depends on your institution and your advisor.

### Publishing: two journals as examples

Our sources cover only two journals, and each point below says which journal's rule it is. Our sources do not give overall figures on article length or review times for sociology journals; before submitting, go by what your target journal publishes.

- **Length.** ASR articles are limited to 15,000 words, including the main text, references, and footnotes, but not tables and figures[^asrsubmission-e2]. *Sociological Science* instead asks for a high ratio of new insight to word count and discourages excessive citation and unnecessary literature reviews[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e10].
- **One journal at a time, anonymized review (ASR's rules).** The American Sociological Association considers it unethical to submit a manuscript to another journal while it is under review at ASR[^asrsubmission-e3]. ASR uses anonymous peer review: when citing your own work, write "Smith (1992) concluded...," not "I concluded (Smith 1992)..."[^asrsubmission-e4]. ASR will not consider a manuscript it has previously rejected[^asrsubmission-e7].
- **Review may take several rounds.** This is *Sociological Science*'s description of the "dominant model" in sociology journals: a majority (sometimes all) of the reviewers must approve before publication, which leads to multiple rounds of revision and ever-longer review times[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e1]. It is the view of a journal that deliberately takes a different path, not a statistical finding. Its own practice: no "revise and resubmit," and a decision within 30 days of submission[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e2].

### Authorship

The American Sociological Association's code of ethics states that in sociology, **author order by default reflects relative contribution**; other practices exist and can be explained in a footnote or the acknowledgments[^asa2018code-e12]. When collaborative work is mainly based on a student's thesis or dissertation, the student is usually listed as first author[^asa2018code-e13]. This is a US association's position; for conventions in other countries and institutions, follow local rules and your advisor's advice `[Our analysis]`.

## How this field differs from others

| | Sociology | For comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Where quantitative data come from | Quantitative social science mostly uses large datasets collected by others and built as public resources[^freese2017replication-e11] | For experimental and data norms in psychology, see the [Psychology page](../psychology/README.md) |
| What kind of sample matters | More concern with population samples or historical cases; results are valuable even if they apply only to the target population[^freese2017replication-e13] | Experimental psychology often uses deliberately unrepresentative samples to study general psychological processes[^freese2017replication-e13] |
| First question when results disagree | In Freese and Peterson's impression, sociologists first look at how the two studies' **samples** differ[^freese2017replication-e12] | Psychologists first look at how the two studies' **designs** differ[^freese2017replication-e12] |
| Publication format | Journal articles and monographs side by side `[Our analysis]`; for example, ASR articles are limited to 15,000 words[^asrsubmission-e2] | Most areas of computer science publish mainly in conference proceedings; see the [Computer science page](../computer-science/README.md#conferences-are-the-main-publication-venue) |

Freese and Peterson write that many sociologists (themselves included) take pride in sociology being "the most undisciplined social science," and in the creativity this brings[^freese2017replication-e21]. Methodological diversity is a defining feature of sociology, and it also means that no single set of rules can easily govern all research `[Our analysis]`.

## Norms and reporting standards in this field

### Ethics: the ASA code and the BSA statement

This section rests on two documents: the 2018 Code of Ethics of the American Sociological Association (ASA), and the 2017 Statement of Ethical Practice of the British Sociological Association (BSA). They are norms written by associations for their members; they are not law and do not replace your institution's ethics review. In other countries, national law and your institution's ethics committee come first `[Our analysis]`. The BSA statement also reminds researchers that each country's laws on data protection, human rights, copyright, and defamation affect how research is carried out and how data are stored and published[^bsa2017statement-e13]. It says plainly that it does not provide a "recipe" for resolving ethical dilemmas; researchers must make their own judgments based on core principles[^bsa2017statement-e1]. For general requirements, see [Research integrity and ethics](../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#4-when-research-involves-people-or-could-harm-others-protect-people-first); this section covers only what is distinctive in sociology.

- **Informed consent is a process, not a one-time signature.** The ASA code says no one may take part in research without their own informed consent or that of a legally authorized representative (except where the code provides otherwise)[^asa2018code-e1]. In research with repeated contact, such as panel surveys and ethnography, you should periodically remind participants what they agreed to and that they can withdraw at any time, and tell them when research procedures change[^asa2018code-e2]. The BSA statement likewise says consent in fieldwork may need to be renegotiated over time[^bsa2017statement-e4].
- **Entering through a gatekeeper still requires each person's consent.** When you gain access to a research site through a gatekeeper, such as the head of an organization, the BSA statement requires you still to obtain informed consent directly from the participants, while also taking the gatekeeper's interests into account[^bsa2017statement-e5].
- **Promise only the confidentiality you can deliver.** The ASA code requires you to find out first which laws and rules limit confidentiality, and to tell participants about those limits as early as possible[^asa2018code-e3]; the BSA statement says not to make unrealistic promises of confidentiality[^bsa2017statement-e6]. The BSA statement also notes (for the UK) that research data given in confidence have no legal privilege and can be subpoenaed by a court, and participants should be told this[^bsa2017statement-e9]; laws differ from country to country, so follow local rules `[Our analysis]`. When studying illegal or potentially stigmatized behavior, the ASA code requires you to be clear about the limits of confidentiality and to submit the study for ethics review[^asa2018code-e14].
- **Vulnerable groups and children.** For vulnerable groups, the ASA code requires you to respect their dignity and not presume they are unable to give consent themselves[^asa2018code-e5]; even when parental consent is waived, the child's own willingness to take part must be respected[^asa2018code-e6]. The BSA statement asks for extra care when participants are especially vulnerable because of age, disability, or physical or mental health[^bsa2017statement-e10]; in addition to parental consent, the child's own consent should be sought[^bsa2017statement-e11].
- **Covert research only as an exception.** The ASA code says researchers should generally identify themselves as researchers; concealment is allowed only when the research could not be done otherwise, the added risk is no more than minimal, and an ethics review board or similar body has approved it[^asa2018code-e7]. The BSA statement points out that covert methods violate the principle of informed consent and may invade privacy; observing people in non-public settings without telling them is acceptable only when the necessary data cannot be obtained any other way[^bsa2017statement-e3].
- **Not everything online is public.** The ASA code says not all information online counts as public, and research in online spaces with access restrictions needs an informed consent process[^asa2018code-e8]; when analyzing large-scale data generated by online activity, you must not try to re-identify individuals[^asa2018code-e4].
- **Mind power relations and your own safety.** Sociologists study people with relatively little power and also people with more power than themselves; research relationships often involve gaps in power and status[^bsa2017statement-e2]. The BSA statement also asks you to consider the safety of researchers themselves (including research assistants) when designing a study[^bsa2017statement-e12].

### Problems specific to ethnography: pseudonyms, altered details, and letting others check

To protect confidentiality, fieldwork often uses pseudonyms, and even alters details so that people and places can't be recognized; this greatly limits others' ability to check the facts[^freese2017replication-e16]. One example: in an ethnography of medical error, Bosk changed the gender of the only female resident among the people he studied, which made it impossible for others to examine whether gender affected the interactions[^freese2017replication-e17]. The BSA statement notes that participants whose combination of characteristics makes them easy to recognize are often hard to disguise without changing the data beyond recognition; sometimes composite or partly fictionalized cases can be used instead[^bsa2017statement-e7]. If you use such techniques, say so in your methods section `[Our analysis]`.

> **Where sources disagree: should ethnographers share their data the way quantitative researchers do?**
>
> In 2024 the journal *Frontiers in Sociology* ran a set of articles on ethnography, and three US sociologists, Jerolmack, Murphy, and Reyes, wrote a one-page editorial for it summarizing the positions. Every scholar's view below is as reported in that editorial; we have not read the original works:
> - **Share and verify**: some scholars (the editorial cites Lubet 2018) argue that ethnographers must name their sources, fact-check, and even share raw data to earn readers' trust[^jerolmack2024ethnography-e3]. And according to Freese and Peterson's review, Desmond in 2016 hired an independent, publicly named fact-checker to review confidential materials[^freese2017replication-e18].
> - **Sharing harms participants and researchers**: other scholars (the editorial cites Reyes 2018 and Stuart 2020) warn that open science requirements may further marginalize two kinds of scholars: those who study vulnerable populations (for whom data transparency is dangerous), and those without the resources to pay for things like fact-checkers[^jerolmack2024ethnography-e4]. In the same collection, Khan and colleagues argue that in research on college students' sexual behavior, so much would have to be redacted that the remaining data would be meaningless[^jerolmack2024ethnography-e8].
> - **Middle ground**: the editorial mentions proposals for partial disclosure of people or places, sharing coding schemes, and providing supplementary data in online appendices[^jerolmack2024ethnography-e5].
>
> The editorial's authors think there is no one-size-fits-all answer to these debates[^jerolmack2024ethnography-e7]. For a newcomer, the practical step is: before you seek consent and ethics review, decide what you will and won't make public later, and explain your reasons in the paper `[Our analysis]`.

### Data archives: finding, citing, and depositing data

This section uses one data archive as its example: the Inter-university Consortium for Political and Social Research (ICPSR), based at the University of Michigan in the US. According to its website (we read the 2025 pages), it is an international consortium of more than 810 academic and research institutions, holding more than 350,000 research files in the social and behavioral sciences[^icpsrdata-e1]. ICPSR's requirements below bind only people who use its data or deposit data with it. Other major repositories listed by Freese and Peterson include the Open Science Framework, openICPSR, and Harvard Dataverse; they say authors are usually urged to deposit data in such independent repositories run by professionals, rather than on personal websites[^freese2017replication-e6]. Other countries have their own social science data archives; follow local practice `[Our analysis]`.

**Using other people's data:**

- **Cite the dataset itself.** ICPSR requires a data citation to include the author, title, distributor, date, version, and a persistent identifier (such as a DOI)[^icpsrdata-e4]; ASR also requires references to datasets to include a persistent identifier[^asrsubmission-e6]. After publishing work that uses ICPSR data, you must send the bibliographic details to ICPSR[^icpsrdata-e5].
- **Acknowledge the original data collectors.** The ASA code requires you to explicitly acknowledge the researchers who originally collected the data[^asa2018code-e10].
- **Whether secondary analysis needs ethics review depends on your institution.** Some institutions require review; others exempt data from pre-approved sources such as ICPSR[^icpsrdata-e9].
- **Record the data version.** For example, *Sociological Science* requires that when you analyze a public dataset without including the raw data, the replication package must give the official data URL, the release version or date of the data used (in case the provider later corrects it), and the code used to extract the variables[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e6].

**Depositing your own data:**

- **Build sharing into your plan.** The ASA code treats sharing data and related documentation as part of the research plan; the exceptions are when proprietary agreements restrict it, or when it is impossible to share while protecting participants' confidentiality (for example, detailed information in field notes or ethnographic interviews)[^asa2018code-e9].
- **Mention sharing in the consent form.** ICPSR's pages remind you that a consent form should explain how confidentiality will be protected without unduly restricting future data sharing[^icpsrdata-e8]. The BSA statement requires that when data may be shared, you discuss possible uses with participants and obtain their consent[^bsa2017statement-e8]; the ASA code requires you to explain how later researchers will maintain confidentiality[^asa2018code-e15].
- **Beware of indirect identifiers.** Variables such as detailed geography, detailed occupation, and exact event dates do not reveal identity on their own, but in combination they can identify a respondent[^icpsrdata-e6]. Where removing them would seriously damage the data's analytic value, ICPSR may release the data only as a "restricted-use" version under controlled conditions[^icpsrdata-e7].
- **Documentation matters as much as data.** ICPSR requires the deposit to be enough for others to understand the data independently, including the codebook, data collection instruments such as questionnaires, a README, and so on[^icpsrdata-e2][^icpsrdata-e3]. For general practice in organizing materials, see [Organizing research materials](../../08_throughout/organizing_materials.md#2-raw-data-is-read-only-everything-else-can-be-regenerated) in Throughout the project.

### Transparency and replication in quantitative research

Two US sociologists, Freese and Peterson, wrote in a 2017 review that discussions of replicability in medicine and the other social sciences were changing research practice, while sociologists had largely been absent[^freese2017replication-e1]. They counted: up to the time of writing, the two flagship sociology journals had published a total of 27 articles with "replication" in the title over their entire history, and only one in the past 25 years[^freese2017replication-e2]. They divide "replication" in quantitative research into four kinds[^freese2017replication-e3]:

1. **Verifiability**: with the same data and the same analysis, do you get the same results?
2. **Robustness**: under a different but reasonable analytic specification, does the conclusion still hold?
3. **Repeatability**: if you collect new data following the original procedures, do you observe it again?
4. **Generalizability**: with different methods or settings, is the conclusion consistent?

They point out that sociology has mostly treated "making data available for verification" as an ethical matter, handled through individual requests. They cite a 2015 report by Young and Horvath: when graduate students in a class exercise tried to verify 53 sociological results, more than half of the authors declined to provide data or code[^freese2017replication-e5]. They also worry that published results are only the "best-looking" of many analytic choices, whether because analyses were changed repeatedly until they supported the desired hypothesis, or because hypotheses formed after seeing the results were written up as if formed in advance[^freese2017replication-e9]. For details and remedies, see the [Psychology page](../psychology/README.md#where-it-went-wrong-p-hacking-and-harking).

**What changed afterward (one journal as an example)**: *Sociological Science* requires that, for submissions received after April 1, 2023, papers using statistical or computational methods must deposit a replication package (code, plus data where law and ethics permit) before publication[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e3]. If the data can't be made public, the paper must explain why in the main text, and the code must still be public[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e4]. It recommends at least indicating which code produces which table and figure[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e7], but does not check whether the package actually reproduces the results[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e9]. Papers that test hypotheses with experimental data the authors collected themselves must also say whether the study was preregistered[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e8]. These are the rules of this one journal; our sources do not cover other sociology journals, so go by your target journal.

Even when a journal doesn't require it, the ASA code says that once results are published, you must allow other responsible researchers to verify them, while protecting participants' confidentiality[^asa2018code-e11].

### Transparency in qualitative research: the debate continues

Freese and Peterson argue that qualitative social science is first and foremost an interpretive enterprise, and that the term "replication" may simply not fit qualitative research[^freese2017replication-e15]. *Sociological Science*'s replication policy accordingly does not require replication packages for studies based on interviews or participant observation, but encourages sharing materials such as interview guides and coding schemes where possible[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e5]. The 2024 editorial likewise notes that academic gatekeepers increasingly ask for field notes and interview transcripts to be shared[^jerolmack2024ethnography-e1], but it is unclear whether positivist standards of verification can be applied to ethnography[^jerolmack2024ethnography-e2].

## Common strategies and standards of evidence

A **research strategy** is the main logic by which a project produces new knowledge; see [Key concepts](../../00_start-here/key-concepts.md#research-strategy) and [Research strategies](../../05_research-strategies/README.md). The table below lists only the strategies our sources support. The grouping and summaries are our own; each standard represents only the journal, institution, or authors (mostly American) cited in its footnote `[Our analysis]`.

| Strategy | What it looks like in sociology | Common evidence and standards | How to do it |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Description](../../05_research-strategies/observe_describe.md) (quantitative) | Using population samples to describe a population and how it changes; results are valuable even if they apply only to the target population[^freese2017replication-e13] | Say for which population, period, and conditions the conclusion holds[^freese2017replication-e14]; if you want to measure change, don't change the measure[^freese2017replication-e10]; cite the dataset itself[^icpsrdata-e4]; *Sociological Science* requires replication packages for statistical papers[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e3] | [Questionnaire surveys](../../06_methods/questionnaire_survey.md), [Longitudinal studies](../../06_methods/longitudinal_study.md), [Data science research](../../06_methods/data_science.md) |
| [Description](../../05_research-strategies/observe_describe.md) (qualitative) | Ethnography and in-depth interviews, concerned with motives and meanings[^freese2017replication-e15] | "Replication" may not be the right term for judging qualitative research[^freese2017replication-e15]; some advocate iterating case by case until "saturation"[^freese2017replication-e19]; pseudonyms and altered details have to be weighed against others' ability to check[^freese2017replication-e16]; *Sociological Science* does not require a replication package but encourages sharing interview guides and coding schemes[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e5] | [Case study and ethnography](../../06_methods/case_study.md), [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md), [Grounded theory](../../06_methods/grounded_theory.md) |
| [Causal explanation](../../05_research-strategies/explain_causality.md) | Testing hypotheses with experimental data you collected yourself[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e8] | State whether the study was preregistered[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e8]; check whether the conclusion survives a different but reasonable analytic specification[^freese2017replication-e3]; don't report only the "best-looking" specification[^freese2017replication-e9] | [Controlled experiments](../../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md), [Process tracing and comparative case studies](../../06_methods/process_tracing.md) |
| [Causal explanation](../../05_research-strategies/explain_causality.md) (comparative-historical research) | Comparing the histories of a few societies to generalize causal conclusions[^mahoney2005clarifying-e1], on topics such as revolutions, welfare states, and democracy and authoritarianism[^mahoney2005clarifying-e4] | Select cases to build approximate controlled comparisons[^skocpol1980uses-e2]; conclusions hold only within stated scope conditions[^mahoney2005clarifying-e2]; details after the table | [Process tracing and comparative case studies](../../06_methods/process_tracing.md), [Archival research](../../06_methods/archival_research.md) |

How to use this table `[Our analysis]`: first find your strategy from your central claim, then read the matching strategy page and method page. Sociology's methods are diverse (see [How this field differs from others](#how-this-field-differs-from-others)); within the same strategy, quantitative and qualitative work have different standards for "enough," so go by your target journal's requirements.

The "comparative-historical research" row rests on two methodological pieces by US scholars: Skocpol and Somers's 1980 journal article, and Mahoney's 2005 short piece in the newsletter of the American Political Science Association's qualitative methods section. They represent the authors' views, not association norms.

- **Why only "approximate" controls are possible.** Macro-level phenomena like these involve too many variables and too few cases[^skocpol1980uses-e2], so perfectly controlled comparisons are impossible[^skocpol1980uses-e5].
- **Two comparative designs.** One takes several cases that share an outcome and differ in other respects, but share a hypothesized cause (the method of agreement)[^skocpol1980uses-e3]. The other contrasts cases in which both the outcome and the cause are present with "negative" cases in which both are absent but which are otherwise as similar as possible (the method of difference); Skocpol and Somers consider the latter more powerful when used on its own[^skocpol1980uses-e4]. For how to select cases purposefully, and why you shouldn't pick only cases with the same outcome, see [Process tracing and comparative case studies](../../06_methods/process_tracing.md#step-8-when-comparing-select-cases-purposively).
- **How far conclusions reach.** Causal arguments of this kind are mainly inductive and can't easily be extended beyond the cases studied[^skocpol1980uses-e8]; Mahoney likewise says these generalizations are bounded by "scope conditions"[^mahoney2005clarifying-e2]. With only two or three cases, inference rests mainly on within-case analysis, not on comparison across cases[^mahoney2005clarifying-e3].
- **Method can't replace theory.** Method alone does not guarantee a reliable conclusion[^skocpol1980uses-e7].

Not all comparative-historical research is causal explanation. Skocpol and Somers distinguish at least three uses; the table covers only "macro-causal analysis," the one closest to hypothesis testing[^skocpol1980uses-e1], which is a powerful tool for criticizing and rejecting false theories[^skocpol1980uses-e6]. In their view, the "contrast-oriented" approach can be theoretically very misleading: almost any theme can be brought to bear on the case material without being explicitly tested, and without being openly acknowledged as a proto-theory[^skocpol1980uses-e9]. So whichever approach you take, state your explanation explicitly and say which cases can test it `[Our analysis]`.

## Research methods common in this field

Sociology has no method pages of its own. The common methods map onto the cross-field method pages as follows; see also [Oral history](../../06_methods/oral_history.md) (the mapping and the notes on differences are our own summary `[Our analysis]`; the footnotes give the basis):

- **Secondary analysis of large survey data**: most quantitative social science uses data collected by others[^freese2017replication-e11]. For general practice, see [Data science research](../../06_methods/data_science.md). The difference: the focus is on understanding how the data were collected and coded, and on citing the dataset itself[^icpsrdata-e4].
- **Questionnaire surveys**: see [Questionnaire surveys](../../06_methods/questionnaire_survey.md). The difference: sociology cares more about population samples[^freese2017replication-e13]; for the basic concepts of sampling, see [Decide what you are studying](../../02_do-the-project/01_question-to-evidence.md#3-decide-what-you-are-studying-unit-of-analysis-and-population).
- **Longitudinal studies and panel surveys**: see [Longitudinal studies](../../06_methods/longitudinal_study.md). The difference: the US General Social Survey keeps the wording of its core items unchanged year after year[^freese2017replication-e10]; in panel surveys you should periodically remind participants what they agreed to[^asa2018code-e2].
- **In-depth interviews, ethnography and participant observation, grounded theory**: see [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md), [Case study and ethnography](../../06_methods/case_study.md), and [Grounded theory](../../06_methods/grounded_theory.md). The difference: in long-term fieldwork, consent has to be renegotiated over time[^bsa2017statement-e4], and the trade-offs around pseudonyms and altered details are sharper[^freese2017replication-e16]; the boundaries and ethics of online fieldwork are still unsettled[^jerolmack2024ethnography-e6].
- **Mixed methods, survey experiments**: see [Mixed methods](../../06_methods/mixed_methods.md) (for example, using survey data to find patterns, then interviews to explain the mechanisms) and [Controlled experiments](../../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md); for preregistration, see the [Psychology page](../psychology/README.md#what-changed-afterward).
- **Comparative-historical research**: comparing the histories of a few societies to find the causes of macro-level outcomes; for standards of judgment, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence) above. For how to do it, see [Process tracing and comparative case studies](../../06_methods/process_tracing.md) and [Archival research](../../06_methods/archival_research.md).

## Advice for newcomers

1. **Before using existing data, read the codebook and how the data were collected.** Errors can arise at the survey organization's end[^freese2017replication-e4]; record the version or release date of the data you use[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e6].
2. **Decide what will happen to the data when you seek consent.** State the limits of confidentiality[^asa2018code-e3] and how the data might be shared[^bsa2017statement-e8][^icpsrdata-e8].
3. **In fieldwork, treat consent as an ongoing process.** Periodically remind participants that the research is ongoing and that they can withdraw[^asa2018code-e2][^bsa2017statement-e4].
4. **Record every analytic choice from day one**, and make sure your code maps onto every table and figure[^sociologicalsciencepolicy-e7]. For general practice, see [Organizing your research materials](../../08_throughout/organizing_materials.md).
5. **State in your paper under what conditions the data are available.** There can be good reasons not to make data public, but readers should know whether the study can be independently verified[^freese2017replication-e7].
6. **Talk to your advisor early about authorship and the form your work will take.** For conventions, see [Authorship](#authorship) and [books and articles side by side](#long-projects-with-books-and-articles-side-by-side) above; whether to write a book or articles depends on how your institution evaluates work.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Promising participants "complete confidentiality" | Confidentiality has legal and regulatory limits that you must find out and disclose in advance[^asa2018code-e3]; the BSA statement says not to make unrealistic promises of confidentiality[^bsa2017statement-e6] |
| Writing only "data will be used for this study only" on the consent form | This wording may prevent you from sharing or archiving the data later `[Our analysis]`; ICPSR warns that consent forms should not unduly restrict future sharing[^icpsrdata-e8] |
| Assuming data are anonymous once names are removed | Geography, occupation, exact dates, and similar variables can still identify a respondent in combination[^icpsrdata-e6] |
| Using a public dataset but citing only the paper that introduced it, not the dataset itself | ICPSR requires citations to include elements such as the version and a persistent identifier[^icpsrdata-e4] |
| Entering a site through the organization's head and treating that as consent from all employees | The BSA statement requires consent directly from the participants themselves[^bsa2017statement-e5] |
| Lurking in online groups to observe, assuming anything online is fair game | Online spaces with access restrictions need an informed consent process[^asa2018code-e8] |
| Altering participants' details without considering how this affects the analysis | The altered detail (such as gender) may be exactly the factor others want to test[^freese2017replication-e17] |

## Further reading

- **ASA Code of Ethics (2018)**: the provisions on informed consent, confidentiality, data sharing, and authorship; this US association's requirements for its members.
- **BSA Statement of Ethical Practice (2017)**: about 10 pages, and concrete on fieldwork, gatekeepers, covert research, and the limits of confidentiality.
- **Freese & Peterson 2017, "Replication in Social Science"**: a sociological account of the four kinds of "replication" in quantitative research, and of why qualitative research is different (a free SocArXiv preprint is available).
- **Jerolmack, Murphy & Reyes 2024 editorial**: one page that lays out the positions in the debate over transparency in ethnography, with pointers to the articles in the same collection (open access).
- **ICPSR's pages on depositing data and data confidentiality**: check against them directly when you prepare a deposit, write a consent form, or handle identifying information.
- **Sociological Science replication policy**: one journal's very specific requirements for replication packages, which you can use as a checklist for organizing your own materials `[Our analysis]`.
