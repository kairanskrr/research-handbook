# Education: field overview

This page explains how education research actually runs. Most of it happens in schools and classrooms, so you deal with principals, teachers, students, and parents. Methods range from randomized controlled trials to classroom ethnography. On the ethics side, you often have to deal with minors and with teachers researching their own students. Standards of evidence, how to interpret effect sizes, and replication also have conventions specific to this field. For what applies in every field, see the chapters listed in [The research journey at a glance](../../00_start-here/research-journey.md) and [Throughout the project](../../08_throughout/README.md); this page does not repeat them. For the research strategies the field commonly uses and the evidence each one relies on, see [Common strategies and standards of evidence](#common-strategies-and-standards-of-evidence). For what overlaps with psychology (preregistration, statistical power, measurement), see the [Psychology page](../psychology/README.md). The rules and guidance cited here come from the US (the American Educational Research Association and the Institute of Education Sciences of the US Department of Education) and the UK (the British Educational Research Association and the Education Endowment Foundation, a UK charity), and each one names whose rule it is. If you do research in another country, follow local law, your institution, and the schools you work with.

**After reading this page, you should be able to:**

- know which approvals you need before you can do research in a school, and why you plan your timeline backward from the school calendar;
- explain what to watch for in consent and power relationships when you study minors or your own students;
- know what the reporting standards and standards of evidence commonly used in education research each emphasize;
- interpret effect sizes on a scale that fits real education research;
- avoid the mistakes newcomers commonly make.

## How research works in this field

### Most research happens in schools: get past the gatekeepers first

Participants in education research are often students and teachers in schools. The ethical guidelines of the British Educational Research Association (BERA) remind you that before you contact participants directly, you should consider whether to approach a "gatekeeper" first, and whether to comply with the school's own ethical approval and safeguarding procedures; the guidelines say this is usually required[^bera2024ethical-e2]. A school leader can agree on behalf of staff and students to take part, but you still need to give each participant information and support[^bera2024ethical-e3]. If you do the research under a university or other educational institution, you also need to pass your own institution's ethics review first[^bera2024ethical-e25]. BERA also asks you to consider how much burden the research places on participants' lives and workloads, especially for groups that are over-researched[^bera2024ethical-e23].

### Plan your timeline backward from the school calendar

The Education Endowment Foundation (EEF) is a charity that funds randomized controlled trials in schools in England. What follows comes from its 2022 recruitment guidance, written for the projects it funds, and applies only to EEF-funded trials. Other countries have different school calendars and exam schedules, so treat it as a reference only:

- **Recruitment is slow, and it is a major risk.** The EEF says recruiting schools takes a lot of time and is often one of the biggest risks to a trial: if you cannot recruit enough schools, the sample is too small to detect an effect and the trial may not go ahead[^eef2022recruitment-e1]. It is not unusual to contact dozens of schools for every school you recruit[^eef2022recruitment-e5].
- **Avoid the busy seasons.** Recruiting is especially hard just before and during the national exams in England; projects that need a lot of teacher time are harder to get schools to agree to at the end of the school year[^eef2022recruitment-e3]. For most projects that start delivery in September, the EEF typically expects recruitment to start in January of the previous school year[^eef2022recruitment-e4].
- **Leave time for approvals and notifications.** Ethics review usually takes 3–6 weeks, depending on the institution[^eef2022recruitment-e7]; the information for parents is usually sent out by the school at the start of the autumn term or at the end of the summer term[^eef2022recruitment-e9].
- **Finish pretesting before randomization.** Otherwise schools allocated to the control group may refuse to be tested, and once people know their allocation the data may also be biased[^eef2022recruitment-e11].
- **Make clear that schools are signing up for an evaluation.** A school may be allocated to the control group, and it has to cooperate with the evaluation whichever group it is in[^eef2022recruitment-e6]. Control schools have little contact with the program, so they are harder to keep in the study until the posttest[^eef2022recruitment-e13].

A rough calculation from this: from starting recruitment in January to starting delivery in September is about 8 months; delivering the intervention for one school year and posttesting at the end of the year is about another 10 months; together that is about 18 months, before any analysis or writing. This is our own estimate from the EEF's milestones, not something the EEF says; projects with shorter delivery will take less time.

### A single study often covers only one segment of the "evidence pipeline"

In 2013 the Institute of Education Sciences (IES) of the US Department of Education and the US National Science Foundation (NSF) jointly published the *Common Guidelines for Education Research and Development*, which divide education research into six types. In the simplest view, they form a "pipeline" of evidence: from foundational and exploratory research, to designing and developing interventions, to testing whether promising interventions work[^ies2013common-e1]. The table below lists five of them (foundational research is left out):

| Type | What it does |
|---|---|
| Early-stage or exploratory research | Examines relationships between important constructs in education to lay the groundwork for future interventions; these relationships are usually correlational, not causal[^ies2013common-e3] |
| Design and development research | Designs and develops an intervention iteratively, based on existing theory and evidence[^ies2013common-e4]; includes collecting feasibility data and running a pilot study[^ies2013common-e8] |
| Efficacy research | Tests an intervention under "ideal" conditions, for example with more support from the developer than usual[^ies2013common-e5] |
| Effectiveness research | Tests it under the routine conditions of the settings it is meant for[^ies2013common-e6] |
| Scale-up research | Tests it across a wide range of populations and settings, without substantial involvement of the developer[^ies2013common-e7] |

The guidelines themselves say that real research is more complex and less sequential than a "pipeline"[^ies2013common-e2]. What this gives a newcomer: first work out which segment your study is in, and keep your conclusions within what that segment can show. For example, a correlation found in exploratory research cannot be written up as "practice X raises achievement".

### Many methods, more than one set of quality standards

The reporting standards of the American Educational Research Association (AERA) give examples: some studies make causal inferences, such as estimating the effect of reducing class size on achievement; others describe what happens in a setting and how people make sense of it, such as how early literacy is taught in a particular kindergarten[^aera2006standards-e4]. The BERA guidelines say concepts such as "reliability," "validity," and "trustworthiness" can be understood in different but equally legitimate ways[^bera2024ethical-e20], and the guidelines themselves do not favor any method[^bera2024ethical-e21]. For how to do and write up research by the standards of the method you chose, see the Part 2 chapter section [Choose a method for each piece of evidence](../../02_do-the-project/02_choose-method.md#2-choose-a-method-for-each-piece-of-evidence).

### Publishing: journals, society conferences, and working papers

- **Journals and society conferences.** For example, BERA holds a large international conference every year and publishes peer-reviewed journals[^bera2024ethical-e22]; in 2013 AERA decided to launch an open-access journal, AERA Open[^makel2014facts-e10].
- **Working papers.** Research aimed at education policy can first go into a working paper series, such as the open-access EdWorkingPapers[^edworkingpapersabout-e1]: papers are made public before formal publication for discussion and have not been peer reviewed[^edworkingpapersabout-e2]; the series mainly takes research that matters for education policy and that the authors plan eventually to submit to a journal[^edworkingpapersabout-e3].
- **Does a conference paper count as a publication? (AERA in the US as an example.)** AERA does not publish conference proceedings; presenters at its annual meeting can choose to put their papers in its open-access online paper repository[^aerapublicationsfaq-e1]. For annual meeting papers in the repository, authors keep the copyright and can submit them to journals after the meeting[^aerapublicationsfaq-e2]. So presenting at the AERA annual meeting is not the same as publishing in a journal; whether your institution counts a conference presentation as an output depends on its own rules.
- **How long does review take? (A cross-field study, for reference only.)** In a study published in 2013, Björk and Solomon drew a stratified random sample of 2,700 papers from 135 journals in the Scopus citation database[^bjork2013publishing-e1]; for each journal they took the last 20 papers published in 2012, and the journals were roughly sorted into nine groups such as social sciences and engineering, with no separate group for education[^bjork2013publishing-e2]. "Submission to acceptance" reflects the time for review and revision, and "acceptance to publication" reflects the production process and the queue for publication[^bjork2013publishing-e3]; for the social sciences group, these two stages averaged 6.2 months and 7.9 months[^bjork2013publishing-e6]. Differences between journals within a group were much larger than differences between groups[^bjork2013publishing-e5], so for how long your target journal actually takes, go by the figures the journal publishes.
- **Authorship.** BERA in the UK: in research done jointly by academics and schoolteachers, everyone who made a substantial contribution should be listed as a co-author[^bera2024ethical-e18]; seniority does not decide who is first author, and you can order by contribution or agree on alphabetical order[^bera2024ethical-e19]. AERA's code of ethics in the US: authorship order follows relative contribution, regardless of status[^aera2011code-e11]; on a multi-authored paper based mainly on a student's dissertation, the student is usually first author[^aera2011code-e12]. For general practice, see [Sorting out authorship and credit](../../08_throughout/collaboration_and_advisors.md#6-authorship-discuss-it-at-the-start-and-adjust-as-you-go) in Throughout the project.

## How this field differs from others

The table below is our synthesis of the sources cited in it.

| | Education | Computer science, psychology (see the [Computer science page](../computer-science/README.md) and the [Psychology page](../psychology/README.md)) |
|---|---|---|
| Where research happens | Schools and classrooms, after getting past the gatekeepers[^bera2024ethical-e2] | Computer science mostly studies systems themselves; psychology mostly works in the lab |
| Who does the research | Besides university researchers, practicing teachers who do research for a degree or to improve their teaching[^bera2024ethical-e1] | Mainly researchers in universities and companies |

| Time constraints | Set by the school calendar and exam season[^eef2022recruitment-e3] | In computer science, set by conference deadlines |

## Norms and reporting standards in this field

### Ethics: minors, gatekeepers, and studying your own students

For general principles, see [When research involves people, protect them first](../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#4-when-research-involves-people-or-could-harm-others-protect-people-first) in Throughout the project. This page draws on two society documents: BERA's *Ethical Guidelines for Educational Research* (5th edition, 2024, UK), written for everyone who does education research, including teachers doing research for a degree or to improve their teaching[^bera2024ethical-e1]; and AERA's *Code of Ethics* (2011, US), whose first aim is to protect the individuals and groups involved in research[^aera2011code-e1]. The main points:

- **Parental consent plus the child's assent.** AERA's code: research with children requires the consent of a parent or legal guardian[^aera2011code-e2], and also the child's own **assent**, to the extent the child is capable of giving it[^aera2011code-e3]; waiving these consents requires approval from an institutional review board (the US term for an ethics committee)[^aera2011code-e4]. The BERA guidelines likewise use "assent" for agreement from people below the legal age of consent[^bera2024ethical-e4], and ask you to design information materials around participants' age, reading ability, and attention span[^bera2024ethical-e5].
- **Opt-in consent, or "taking part by default unless you opt out"?** BERA generally prefers opt-in consent, and allows opt-out in some circumstances, but you need to check local law and custom[^bera2024ethical-e7]. The law makes a big difference: the EEF guidance points out that under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), a data protection regulation, opt-out is not a valid legal basis for processing personal data[^eef2022recruitment-e8], but you must still give parents the chance to withdraw their child's data[^eef2022recruitment-e9]; students aged 13 and over exercise the right to object to their data being processed themselves[^eef2022recruitment-e10].
- **When someone in the class does not consent to observation.** BERA: if a student or parent actively refuses, you must respect that and work out a practical solution[^bera2024ethical-e8]; when the research analyzes individuals' roles within a group, people who have not consented cannot be included[^bera2024ethical-e9]. AERA: obtain consent before making audio or video recordings[^aera2011code-e13].
- **Studying your own students.** BERA lists "the researcher is the participants' teacher" as a situation that needs careful handling, and asks you to reassure students as far as possible that not taking part is completely fine[^bera2024ethical-e12]. AERA's code asks you to prefer participants with whom you have no teacher–student or supervisory relationship; when you cannot avoid it, take special care that consent is voluntary[^aera2011code-e7], and protect students from any disadvantage if they decline or withdraw[^aera2011code-e6]; the researcher's authority in itself puts pressure on people[^aera2011code-e8]. BERA's suggestions include being transparent about your role as a researcher and bringing an independent third party into the research process[^bera2024ethical-e11]. One workable approach is to have a colleague collect the consent forms, and only find out who consented after grading is finished.
- **Fairness to the control group.** BERA: an intervention may be seen as a benefit that only one group gets and the control group does not; one way to ease this is to offer the control group any intervention shown to be effective once the trial is over[^bera2024ethical-e13].
- **Confidentiality has exceptions.** BERA: if you learn of abuse or similar, you may have a legal duty to report it[^bera2024ethical-e14]; this exception has to be written into the information sheet and consent form in advance[^bera2024ethical-e15].
- **Report all results, and share data.** BERA requires you to report all results of interventions and evaluations, including negative ones[^bera2024ethical-e16]; AERA treats data sharing as standard practice[^aera2011code-e9]. For general practice, see [Research integrity and ethics](../../08_throughout/integrity_and_ethics.md#2-be-honest-with-the-evidence-dont-fool-yourself-report-all-results).

### Reporting standards: AERA's standards for reporting empirical research

In 2006 AERA adopted the *Standards for Reporting on Empirical Social Science Research in AERA Publications*, which apply to AERA's own publications and cover both quantitative and qualitative research[^aera2006standards-e1]. There are two overarching principles: conclusions must be warranted, and reporting must be transparent[^aera2006standards-e3]. It is not a checklist; a given standard may legitimately not apply to a particular study[^aera2006standards-e2]. We picked out the points most relevant to education research:

- **Say how you got into the field.** Describe how you gained access to the school, how you selected participants and obtained consent, how you built trust, what role you played as a researcher, and whether these relationships changed over time[^aera2006standards-e5]; the relationship between researcher and participants affects the data you collect[^aera2006standards-e16].
- **Describe both the intervention and the comparison group clearly**[^aera2006standards-e6].
- **Report what went wrong during the study**: attrition, missing data, ceiling or floor effects, tests not administered according to standard procedures, suspected cheating, and so on[^aera2006standards-e7].
- **Quantitative results**: report an effect size for each key result[^aera2006standards-e8] together with its uncertainty (standard error or confidence interval)[^aera2006standards-e9], and explain in words what the effect means for the research question[^aera2006standards-e10].
- **Qualitative results**: give supporting evidence for each claim and report important counterexamples[^aera2006standards-e11]; clearly mark speculation that goes beyond the evidence[^aera2006standards-e12].
- **Scope of generalization**: if you want to generalize your conclusions, say clearly to what[^aera2006standards-e13].
- **Ethics**: state that the study was approved by an institutional review board[^aera2006standards-e14]. Even without names, identities can be revealed indirectly[^aera2006standards-e15], for example by writing "the only bilingual pilot primary school in the city".

### Standards of evidence: how the What Works Clearinghouse rates studies

The What Works Clearinghouse (WWC), run by the Institute of Education Sciences of the US Department of Education, collects existing education research, assesses its quality, and summarizes the evidence that meets its standards[^wwc2022handbook-e1]. Its ratings mainly ask whether the results can support a causal inference[^wwc2022handbook-e16]. This is a US standard; even if you are not in the US, it spells out "what kind of intervention study is convincing". The following is based on version 5.0 of its handbook (2022):

- **Three ratings**: Meets WWC Standards Without Reservations, Meets WWC Standards With Reservations, and Does Not Meet WWC Standards[^wwc2022handbook-e2]. The top rating goes only to studies with a strong design that were well carried out[^wwc2022handbook-e3]; "with reservations" means causes other than the intervention cannot be fully ruled out[^wwc2022handbook-e4].
- **Only four designs are reviewed**: randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental designs, regression discontinuity designs, and single-case designs[^wwc2022handbook-e5].
- **A quasi-experiment can at best meet standards "with reservations."** The authors must show that the two groups were equivalent before the intervention (for example, on pretest scores), with statistical adjustment where needed[^wwc2022handbook-e6]; a randomized trial at high risk of bias because the sample's composition changed can also reach "with reservations" at best[^wwc2022handbook-e7].
- **Attrition.** A participant who was assigned to a group but ends up with no outcome data counts as attrition[^wwc2022handbook-e8]; that includes a student who is absent on the day of the posttest[^wwc2022handbook-e9]. With high attrition, the people who remain may be too different from the original sample[^wwc2022handbook-e10].
- **"One class versus one class" doesn't work.** When the intervention or comparison group has only one teacher, one class, or one school, the WWC treats it as a confounding factor[^wwc2022handbook-e11]: you cannot tell whether the effect came from the intervention or from that teacher or that class. Assigning students within the same school to the two groups is fine[^wwc2022handbook-e12]. If the two groups come from different school years, time can also become a confounding factor[^wwc2022handbook-e13].
- **Be careful with researcher-made tests.** The WWC is concerned that effect sizes from tests made by the intervention's developer or the researchers are less informative for decision-makers and teachers than effect sizes from independent tests[^wwc2022handbook-e14].
- **A similar approach in the UK.** The EEF also gives the trials it funds a "padlock" rating that shows how secure the findings are, and this rating is strongly affected by attrition from the outcome data[^eef2022recruitment-e16].

### Preregistration: education has its own registry

A 2018 IES blog post introduced REES, the Registry of Efficacy and Effectiveness Studies: a registry for studies of causal effects in education[^ies2018rees-e1], where you can register randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental designs, regression discontinuity designs, and single-case designs[^ies2018rees-e3]. An entry includes basic study information and a pre-analysis plan[^ies2018rees-e4], and every change is time-stamped and made public[^ies2018rees-e5]. In its grant application guidance for fiscal year 2019, IES recommended that applicants describe their preregistration plans[^ies2018rees-e2]; BERA in the UK also recommends registering evaluation studies in advance[^bera2024ethical-e16]. For how to write a preregistration and what to do when you deviate from the plan, see the [Psychology page](../psychology/README.md#what-changed-afterward) and the Part 2 chapter section [Be clear whether you are testing or exploring](../../02_do-the-project/01_question-to-evidence.md#5-be-clear-whether-you-are-testing-or-exploring).

### Effect sizes: don't judge education interventions by Cohen's benchmarks

An **effect size**, in its most basic form, is the difference between two group means divided by the standard deviation[^kraft2019interpreting-e22], so it is expressed in "standard deviations." The following is based on Kraft's 2019 working paper (this is the only version we read):

- **Cohen's benchmarks do not fit education interventions.** The commonly used Cohen benchmarks are 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, and 0.8 large[^kraft2019interpreting-e2]. They come from a handful of small social psychology lab experiments in the 1960s, mostly with undergraduates[^kraft2019interpreting-e3]. An effect that counts as "small" by these benchmarks is already large compared with most field interventions[^kraft2019interpreting-e1]. One consequence of using them is underpowered studies[^kraft2019interpreting-e4].
- **The numbers in practice.** Kraft compiled 1,942 effect sizes from 747 randomized controlled trials in education with standardized test outcomes[^kraft2019interpreting-e15]: the median was 0.10 standard deviations[^kraft2019interpreting-e16], and a quarter were zero or negative[^kraft2019interpreting-e17]. Among them, the 49 trials (139 effect sizes) funded by the US Department of Education and required to be preregistered had a median of only 0.03[^kraft2019interpreting-e19]. He also cites a 2019 study by Lortie-Forgues and Inglis: 141 randomized controlled trials funded by IES in the US and the EEF in the UK had an average effect size of only 0.06[^kraft2019interpreting-e5].
- **The new scale he proposes** (only for causal studies from pre-K through grade 12 with academic achievement as the outcome): less than 0.05 is small, 0.05 to less than 0.20 is medium, and 0.20 or more is large[^kraft2019interpreting-e15].
- **Study features change effect sizes.** Researcher-made tests closely aligned with the intervention often give effect sizes 2–4 times those on US state standardized tests[^kraft2019interpreting-e8]; testing right after the intervention gives larger effects than testing several months later[^kraft2019interpreting-e9]; small trials targeted at specific groups give larger effects than interventions for everyone[^kraft2019interpreting-e10]; in his data, studies with more than 2,000 students had effects several times smaller than studies with 100 or fewer (median 0.03 vs 0.24)[^kraft2019interpreting-e18]; the effect of "offering the intervention" is smaller than the effect of "actually receiving it"[^kraft2019interpreting-e12]. He recommends computing effect sizes with standard deviations at the individual student level wherever possible[^kraft2019interpreting-e11].
- **Converting is not interpreting; also look at cost and scale.** Converting an effect size into "the equivalent of so many months of learning" only changes the unit[^kraft2019interpreting-e6]. When effects are similar, the cheaper intervention deserves more attention[^kraft2019interpreting-e13]; that effects shrink when small programs are scaled up is one of the most consistent findings in education research[^kraft2019interpreting-e14].

What this means in practice: when you calculate sample size, do not assume an effect like 0.5; when you read about an education intervention with "an effect size of 0.8," first check whether it was a small sample, a researcher-made test, or a test given right after the intervention. For how to justify your sample size, see [Statistical power and sample size](../psychology/README.md#statistical-power-and-sample-size) on the Psychology page.

### Replication studies are rare

In 2014 Makel and Plucker published an analysis in *Educational Researcher* (we read only the abstract and AERA's press release). According to the abstract, they searched the entire publication history of the top 100 education journals ranked by 5-year impact factor and found that only 0.13% of articles were replications; when the original study and the replication had no authors in common, the replication was markedly less likely to succeed[^makel2014facts-e1][^makel2014facts-e3]. The authors list biases against replication in submission, funding, peer review, hiring, and promotion[^makel2014facts-e9], and recommend that journals create a dedicated article type for replications and that replication be built into student training[^makel2014facts-e7].

## Common strategies and standards of evidence

A **research strategy** is the main logic by which a project produces new knowledge; see [Key concepts](../../00_start-here/key-concepts.md#research-strategy) and [Research strategies](../../05_research-strategies/README.md). The table below lists the four strategies this page's sources support. The grouping and the summaries are our own; each standard represents only the institution in its footnote (mostly in the US or UK).

| Strategy | What it looks like in education | Common evidence and standards | How to do it |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Causal explanation](../../05_research-strategies/explain_causality.md) | Test whether a practice has an effect, for example by estimating the effect of reducing class size on achievement[^aera2006standards-e4] | The US WWC's ratings mainly ask whether the results can support a causal inference[^wwc2022handbook-e16], and it reviews only four designs[^wwc2022handbook-e5]; no group can consist of just one class or one teacher[^wwc2022handbook-e11]. See [Standards of evidence](#standards-of-evidence-how-the-what-works-clearinghouse-rates-studies) and [Effect sizes](#effect-sizes-dont-judge-education-interventions-by-cohens-benchmarks) above | [Field experiments](../../06_methods/field_experiment.md), [Quasi-experiments and causal identification](../../06_methods/quasi_experiment.md), [Controlled experiments](../../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md) |
| [Building and improving](../../05_research-strategies/build_improve.md) | Design and refine a teaching intervention over several rounds in real classrooms together with teachers, while distilling design principles[^anderson2012design-e1]; IES and NSF call this "design and development research"[^ies2013common-e4] | There is general agreement that it should produce both an effective intervention and useful theory[^easterday2014design-e1]; most such studies do not produce effect sizes that demonstrate "what works"[^anderson2012design-e9], so showing that something works takes a later efficacy or effectiveness study | [Design-based research](practices/design_based_research.md) |
| [Description](../../05_research-strategies/observe_describe.md) | Describe what happens in a setting and how people make sense of it, for example how early literacy is taught in a particular kindergarten[^aera2006standards-e4]; exploratory research examines relationships between constructs, which are usually correlational[^ies2013common-e3] | AERA's reporting standards: say how you got into the field and what role you played as a researcher[^aera2006standards-e5]; give evidence for each claim and report important counterexamples[^aera2006standards-e11] | [Case study and ethnography](../../06_methods/case_study.md), [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md), [Questionnaire surveys](../../06_methods/questionnaire_survey.md) |
| [Synthesis](../../05_research-strategies/synthesize.md) | Collect existing education research, assess its quality, and summarize the evidence that meets the standards, as the US WWC does[^wwc2022handbook-e1] | The WWC gives three ratings based on design and execution[^wwc2022handbook-e2]; it treats effect sizes from tests made by the developer or the authors with more caution[^wwc2022handbook-e14] | [Review studies](../../06_methods/review_study.md), [Systematic reviews and meta-analysis](../../06_methods/meta_analysis.md) |

How to use this table:

- **First work out where your study sits in the [evidence pipeline](#a-single-study-often-covers-only-one-segment-of-the-evidence-pipeline).** Early-stage exploratory research is mostly description; design and development research is building and improving; efficacy, effectiveness, and scale-up research are causal explanation.
- **One project often combines several strategies.** Design-based research, for example, usually uses mixed methods[^anderson2012design-e10]. In that case, pick the main strategy by your central claim, and hold the other parts to the standards of their own strategies.

## Research methods common in this field

The method specific to this field is [Design-based research](practices/design_based_research.md). The other methods are shared with other fields that study people: read the relevant method page first, then note the differences in the table below:

| Research method | Method page | What to watch for in education |
|---|---|---|
| Randomized controlled trials (including randomizing whole classes or schools) | [Field experiments](../../06_methods/field_experiment.md), [Controlled experiments](../../06_methods/controlled_experiment.md) | Some assign groups such as whole schools[^wwc2022handbook-e15]; finish pretesting before randomization[^eef2022recruitment-e11] |
| Quasi-experiments (for example, comparing schools that did and did not adopt a practice) | [Quasi-experiments and causal identification](../../06_methods/quasi_experiment.md) | You must show the two groups were equivalent before the intervention[^wwc2022handbook-e6]; avoid one class versus one class[^wwc2022handbook-e11] |
| Classroom observation, case study, and ethnography | [Case study and ethnography](../../06_methods/case_study.md) | How to handle students who do not consent[^bera2024ethical-e8][^bera2024ethical-e9]; report how you got into the field[^aera2006standards-e5] |
| Interviews (with teachers, students, parents) | [Interview studies](../../06_methods/interview_study.md) | Interviewing children requires parental consent plus the child's assent[^aera2011code-e2][^aera2011code-e3]; legal exceptions to confidentiality[^bera2024ethical-e14] |
| Questionnaire surveys and scales | [Questionnaire surveys](../../06_methods/questionnaire_survey.md), [Scale development and measurement studies](../../06_methods/scale_development.md) | Mind the burden on over-researched groups[^bera2024ethical-e23] |
| Action research, practitioner research (teachers studying their own classrooms) | [Action research](../../06_methods/action_research.md) | Power relationships created by the dual role[^bera2024ethical-e10]; inform the school's leadership[^bera2024ethical-e24] |
| Replication studies | [Replication studies](../../06_methods/replication.md) | Very rare in education journals; less likely to succeed when the authors do not overlap with the original study's[^makel2014facts-e1][^makel2014facts-e3] |

**"Action research" means different things in the two places.** On the method page, action research is a researcher working with an organization and personally driving a change. According to Anderson and Shattuck, in education's action research the educator is both researcher and teacher[^anderson2012design-e4], and it is usually done by the teacher alone[^anderson2012design-e5]. This means the "researchers teaming up with practitioners" approach described on the method page is closer to design-based research in education.

---

## Advice for newcomers

1. **Plan your timeline around the school calendar most of a year ahead.** For projects starting delivery in September, the EEF expects recruitment to start in January of the previous school year[^eef2022recruitment-e4]; build exam season, the end of the school year, and ethics review into your plan[^eef2022recruitment-e3][^eef2022recruitment-e7].
2. **Find the gatekeepers first, then the participants; the principal's agreement is not everyone's agreement.**[^bera2024ethical-e2][^bera2024ethical-e3]
3. **When you study your own students, keep consent completely separate from grades and assessment.** Let students know that not taking part has no consequences at all[^bera2024ethical-e12][^aera2011code-e6].
4. **Use independent standardized tests for your main outcomes where possible, with researcher-made tests as a supplement.**[^wwc2022handbook-e14][^kraft2019interpreting-e8]
5. **Calculate sample size from realistic effect sizes.** In the 747 education randomized controlled trials Kraft compiled, the median effect size was 0.10 standard deviations[^kraft2019interpreting-e16]; if you plan for 0.5, your study is likely to be underpowered[^kraft2019interpreting-e4].

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| One experimental class versus one control class | The WWC treats a group with only one class or one teacher as a confounding factor[^wwc2022handbook-e11] |
| Randomizing first, pretesting afterward | Control schools may refuse to be tested, and once people know their allocation the data may also be biased[^eef2022recruitment-e11] |
| Using Cohen's benchmarks to call 0.1 standard deviations "trivial" | An effect that counts as "small" by Cohen's benchmarks is already large compared with most field interventions[^kraft2019interpreting-e1] |
| Reporting only a large effect on a researcher-made test | Effect sizes on researcher-made tests are often 2–4 times those on standardized state tests[^kraft2019interpreting-e8], and may be less informative than independent tests[^wwc2022handbook-e14] |
| Writing up an "effect size" from a correlational study as a causal effect | Be clear whether it is correlational or causal[^kraft2019interpreting-e7] |
| Not recording or reporting attrition and missed tests | With high attrition, the people who remain may be too different from the original sample[^wwc2022handbook-e10]; AERA's standards require you to report it[^aera2006standards-e7] |
| Promising "complete confidentiality" in the consent form | If you learn of abuse or similar, you may have to disclose it, and this exception has to be stated in advance[^bera2024ethical-e15] |
| Not finding out what the control group normally does | Especially with targeted interventions, control students often get other extra support too[^eef2022recruitment-e14] |

## Further reading

The reasons for each recommendation are our own view.

- **BERA, *Ethical Guidelines for Educational Research*, 5th edition (2024, UK)**: written for both researchers and practicing teachers; covers consent, assent, and studying your own students.
- **AERA *Code of Ethics* (2011, US), sections 13–15**: informed consent, research with children, data sharing, and authorship.
- **AERA, *Standards for Reporting on Empirical Social Science Research* (2006, US)**: read it before you write your paper and keep it at hand; it covers both quantitative and qualitative research.
- **WWC *Procedures and Standards Handbook*, version 5.0 (US)**: if you want to study whether an intervention works, read Chapters I and III first to know what reviewers will pick at.
- **EEF, recruitment and retention guidance (2022, UK)**: practical details of running trials in schools; written for EEF-funded projects, so read it as a reference elsewhere.
- **IES and NSF, *Common Guidelines for Education Research and Development* (2013, US)**: helps you work out which segment of the evidence pipeline your study is in.
- **Kraft, *Interpreting Effect Sizes of Education Interventions* (2019 working paper)**: five guidelines and a new scale for interpreting effect sizes in education.
- **Anderson & Shattuck 2012; Makel & Plucker 2014**: the first explains what design-based research is and where it gets hard; the second covers the state of replication in education and how to improve it.
