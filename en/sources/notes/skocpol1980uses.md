# Skocpol & Somers 1980: The Uses of Comparative History in Macrosocial Inquiry

- Source: `skocpol1980uses`, [original](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0010417500009282)
- Version: Comparative Studies in Society and History 22(2), pp. 174-197, 1980; the publisher, Cambridge University Press, offers the full-text PDF free on its website. Both authors were at Harvard University at the time, and an early version was presented at the "Methods of Historical Sociology" session of the 1979 American Sociological Association annual meeting. Locations give journal page numbers. This is the two authors' account of comparative-historical methods, not a professional society's standard.

### E1

- Location: p. 175
- Quote: "Notwithstanding such attempts at homogenization, there are, in fact, at least three distinct logics-in-use of comparative history." / "One of them, which we shall label comparative history as macro-causal analysis, actually does resemble multivariate hypothesis-testing."
- Key point: Comparative history is actually used in at least three distinct ways; one of them, called "macro-causal analysis," does resemble multivariate hypothesis testing.

### E2

- Location: p. 182
- Quote: "This third variant of comparative history is, indeed, a kind of multivariate analysis to which scholars turn in order to validate causal statements about macro-phenomena for which, inherently, there are too many variables and not enough cases. Macro-analytic comparative historians proceed by selecting or referring to aspects of historical cases in order to set up approximations to controlled comparisons."
- Key point: Macro-causal analysis is used to test causal claims about macro-level phenomena, which inherently involve "too many variables, not enough cases"; researchers select aspects of historical cases to approximate "controlled comparisons."

### E3

- Location: p. 183
- Quote: "On the one hand, Macro-analysts can try to establish that several cases having in common the phenomenon to be explained also have in common the hypothesized causal factors, although the cases vary in other ways that might have seemed causally relevant."
- Key point: One design (Mill's "method of agreement"): several cases all show the phenomenon to be explained and all have the hypothesized cause, while differing in other seemingly relevant ways.

### E4

- Location: p. 183
- Quote: "On the other hand, Macro-analysts can contrast cases in which the phenomenon to be explained and the hypothesized causes are present to other ("negative") cases in which the phenomenon and the causes are both absent, although they are as similar as possible to the "positive" cases in other respects." / "Taken alone, this second approach is more powerful for establishing valid causal associations than is the "Method of Agreement."
- Key point: Another design (Mill's "method of difference"): contrast cases where both the phenomenon and the cause are present with "negative" cases where both are absent but which are otherwise as similar as possible; used on its own, it is more powerful than the method of agreement.

### E5

- Location: pp. 193–194
- Quote: "The problem is that perfectly controlled comparisons are never really feasible." / "and history rarely, if ever, provides exactly the cases needed for controlled comparisons."
- Key point: Perfectly controlled comparisons are never achievable; history almost never provides exactly the cases a controlled comparison needs.

### E6

- Location: p. 194
- Quote: "Moreover, macro-causal analysis remains a powerful tool for criticizing and invalidating mistaken theories."
- Key point: Macro-causal analysis is still a powerful tool for criticizing and rejecting false theories.

### E7

- Location: p. 195
- Quote: "The point is that the method itself is no substitute for theory, and cannot in itself guarantee reliable and valid conclusions."
- Key point: Method by itself cannot substitute for theory, nor can it alone guarantee that conclusions are reliable and valid.

### E8

- Location: p. 195
- Quote: "The answer is that, because they are largely inductively established, comparative-historical causal arguments cannot be readily generalized beyond the cases actually discussed."
- Key point: Causal arguments in comparative history are reached mainly by induction and cannot readily be generalized beyond the cases actually discussed.

### E9

- Location: p. 193
- Quote: "But the Contrast-oriented approach can also be theoretically very misleading. For virtually any themes can be brought to bear upon the case materials without being put to any explicit test and without being openly identified as a proto-theory."
- Key point: "Contrast-oriented" comparative history can be theoretically misleading: almost any theme can be fitted onto the case material, without being explicitly tested and without openly acknowledging that it is in fact a rudimentary theory.
