# Columbia Public Health 2023: Content Analysis

- Source: `columbia2023contentanalysis`, [web page](https://www.publichealth.columbia.edu/research/population-health-methods/content-analysis)
- Version: a public methods page in the Population Health Methods series of Columbia University's Mailman School of Public Health; page metadata shows it was last modified on March 30, 2023. Written for public health researchers. Locations give the page's headings. Note: the page says 80% is generally an acceptable level of reliability without saying which index; Lombard et al. and Krippendorff both advise against relying on percent agreement alone, so the guide does not use that sentence.

### E1

- Location: Overview
- Quote: "Content analysis is a research tool used to determine the presence of certain words, themes, or concepts within some given qualitative data (i.e. text). Using content analysis, researchers can quantify and analyze the presence, meanings, and relationships of such certain words, themes, or concepts."
- Key point: Content analysis determines whether certain words, themes or concepts appear in a text, and lets researchers quantify and analyze their presence, meanings and relationships.

### E2

- Location: Overview
- Quote: "Researchers can then make inferences about the messages within the texts, the writer(s), the audience, and even the culture and time of surrounding the text."
- Key point: From this, researchers can make inferences about the messages in the text, its writers, its audience, and even the culture and time around it.

### E3

- Location: Description
- Quote: "Sources of data could be from interviews, open-ended questions, field research notes, conversations, or literally any occurrence of communicative language (such as books, essays, discussions, newspaper headlines, speeches, media, historical documents)."
- Key point: Data can come from interviews, open-ended questions, field notes, conversations, or any communicative language, such as books, discussions, headlines, speeches, media and historical documents.

### E4

- Location: Types of Content Analysis
- Quote: "There are two general types of content analysis: conceptual analysis and relational analysis. Conceptual analysis determines the existence and frequency of concepts in a text. Relational analysis develops the conceptual analysis further by examining the relationships among concepts in a text."
- Key point: There are two broad types: conceptual analysis finds whether and how often concepts appear in a text; relational analysis goes further and examines the relationships among them.

### E5

- Location: Conceptual Analysis, General steps, step 1
- Quote: "Decide the level of analysis: word, word sense, phrase, sentence, themes"
- Key point: First decide the level of analysis: word, word sense, phrase, sentence or theme.

### E6

- Location: Conceptual Analysis, General steps, step 2
- Quote: "Decide how many concepts to code for: develop a pre-defined or interactive set of categories or concepts. Decide either: A. to allow flexibility to add categories through the coding process, or B. to stick with the pre-defined set of categories."
- Key point: Decide which concepts to code: either allow new categories to be added during coding, or stick with a predefined set.

### E7

- Location: Conceptual Analysis, General steps, step 3
- Quote: "Decide whether to code for existence or frequency of a concept. The decision changes the coding process."
- Key point: Decide whether to code whether a concept appears or how often it appears; the choice changes the coding process.

### E8

- Location: Conceptual Analysis, General steps, steps 4–5
- Quote: "What level of implication is to be allowed? Words that imply the concept or words that explicitly state the concept?" / "Develop rules for coding your texts. After decisions of steps 1-4 are complete, a researcher can begin developing rules for translation of text into codes. This will keep the coding process organized and consistent."
- Key point: Decide how much implication to allow (only explicit mentions, or implied ones too), then write rules for translating text into codes so coding stays organized and consistent.

### E9

- Location: Conceptual Analysis, General steps, step 6
- Quote: "Decide what to do with irrelevant information: should this be ignored (e.g. common English words like “the” and “and”), or used to reexamine the coding scheme in the case that it would add to the outcome of coding?"
- Key point: Decide in advance what to do with content irrelevant to the research question: ignore it, or use it to re-examine the coding scheme.

### E10

- Location: Relational Analysis
- Quote: "Select text for analysis carefully by balancing having enough information for a thorough analysis so results are not limited with having information that is too extensive so that the coding process becomes too arduous and heavy to supply meaningful and worthwhile results."
- Key point: When selecting texts, balance having enough material for a thorough analysis against having so much that coding becomes unmanageable.

### E11

- Location: Reliability and Validity, Validity, Conclusions
- Quote: "Software can obtain an accurate count of that word's occurrence and frequency, but not be able to produce an accurate accounting of the meaning inherent in each particular usage."
- Key point: Software can count exactly how often a word occurs but cannot tell what each use means (for example, "mine" as a pronoun, an explosive, or a place to dig ore).

### E12

- Location: Disadvantages of Content Analysis
- Quote: "Is often devoid of theoretical base, or attempts too liberally to draw meaningful inferences about the relationships and impacts implied in a study" / "Tends too often to simply consist of word counts" / "Often disregards the context that produced the text, as well as the state of things after the text is produced"
- Key point: Common weaknesses of content analysis: little theoretical grounding, over-reaching inferences, too often mere word counts, and neglect of the context that produced the text.
