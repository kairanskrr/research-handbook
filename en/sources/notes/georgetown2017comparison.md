# Georgetown Law Writing Center 2017: How to Craft an Effective Case Comparison

- Source: `georgetown2017comparison`, [Original PDF (Writing Center, Georgetown University Law Center, US; free)](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/How-to-Craft-an-Effective-Case-Comparison.pdf)
- Version: Handout from the Writing Center at Georgetown University Law Center, prepared by Michel Djandji in 2017. Written for **US law students**. Locations are given as handout section numbers.

### E1

- Location: Opening
- Quote: "In the United States, like in any common law system, legal reasoning relies on the basic assumption that similar cases should be decided similarly."
- Key point: In the United States, as in any common law system, legal reasoning rests on the basic assumption that similar cases should be decided similarly.

### E2

- Location: I. When to Use a Case Comparison
- Quote: "Similar facts between the past case and the present case lead to a similar outcome (analogizing); dissimilar facts between the past case and the present case lead to a different outcome (distinguishing)."
- Key point: Similar facts between a past case and the present case lead to a similar outcome (analogizing); dissimilar facts lead to a different outcome (distinguishing).

### E3

- Location: II. Dissecting a Case Comparison
- Quote: "A similarity or difference is legally significant if it relates to a term in the governing rule of law or a principle implicated by the rule or its application in this case."
- Key point: A similarity or difference is legally significant only if it relates to a term in the governing rule or to a principle implicated by the rule.

### E4

- Location: II, footnote 4
- Quote: "For example, a writer cannot argue that because the precedential case has fact x and the current case does not have fact x, the outcome of the current case is not the outcome of the precedential case; this is mistaken negation."
- Key point: A common logical error, mistaken negation: you cannot argue that because the precedent has fact x and the current case does not, the current case must come out differently.

### E5

- Location: III. What To Do with Unfavorable Facts
- Quote: "These instances are called counterexamples, and a legal writer must be prepared to anticipate and rebut them."
- Key point: Precedents with similar facts but the opposite outcome are called counterexamples; a legal writer must anticipate and rebut them.
