# Müller et al. 2020: A Guideline for Life Cycle Assessment of Carbon Capture and Utilization

- Source: `muller2020lca`, [original](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2020.00015/full), DOI: 10.3389/fenrg.2020.00015
- Version: Frontiers in Energy Research, vol. 8, article 15, 2020, open access under a Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY) license. It is a condensed version of the life cycle assessment part of the 2018 *Techno-Economic Assessment & Life-Cycle Assessment Guidelines for CO2 Utilization* (Global CO2 Initiative et al.), written for carbon capture and utilization (CCU) technologies. The web page has no page numbers; locations give section headings. Accessed 2026-10-06.

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "The guideline has been development in a collaborative process involving over 40 experts and builds upon existing LCA standards and guidelines."
- Key point: The guideline was developed collaboratively by over 40 experts and builds on existing life cycle assessment (LCA) standards and guidelines.

### E2

- Location: General Introduction to Life Cycle Assessment
- Quote: "Life cycle assessment is a methodology to account for the environmental impacts of a product or service throughout its entire life cycle"
- Key point: Life cycle assessment is a method for accounting for the environmental impacts of a product or service over its entire life cycle.

### E3

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "However, the reduction of environmental impacts cannot be taken for granted: high energetic co-reactants such as hydrogen (Sternberg and Bardow, 2016) or epoxide are often needed to activate the chemically inert CO2"
- Key point: Using CO2 as a feedstock does not automatically reduce environmental impacts, because high-energy co-reactants such as hydrogen are often needed to activate the inert CO2.

### E4

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "As a result, LCA studies on CCU technologies are often not comparable, e.g., because of differences in functional units, i.e., the relative basis for which environmental impacts are assessed, or system boundaries."
- Key point: LCA studies of carbon capture and utilization are often not comparable because they use different functional units (the reference basis for calculating environmental impacts) or system boundaries.

### E5

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "For example, for CO2-based methanol, LCA studies in the literature reported cradle-to-gate carbon footprints between −1.7 and +9.7 kg of CO2, eq per kg of methanol."
- Key point: For example, for CO2-based methanol, published studies report cradle-to-gate carbon footprints between −1.7 and +9.7 kg CO2-eq per kg of methanol.

### E6

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "However, harmonization of assumptions regarding supply of electricity and CO2 reduced the variation such that all carbon footprints had the same sign and could be clearly distinguished from fossil-based methanol."
- Key point: After harmonizing assumptions about electricity and CO2 supply, the variation shrank so that all carbon footprints had the same sign and could be clearly distinguished from fossil-based methanol.

### E7

- Location: General Introduction to Life Cycle Assessment (studies at low technology readiness levels)
- Quote: "In contrast, low TRL processes usually have higher energy demand or solvent consumption because of not yet established heat integration and/or process optimization. At the same time, low TRL processes lack auxiliary processes such as product purification steps after reaction. Thus, LCA studies on lab-scale processes can both under- or over-estimate environmental impacts."
- Key point: Processes at low technology readiness levels (TRL) lack heat integration and optimization, so their energy and solvent demand is higher, and they lack auxiliary steps such as downstream purification; an LCA based on laboratory scale may therefore over- or underestimate environmental impacts.

### E8

- Location: General Introduction to Life Cycle Assessment (studies at low technology readiness levels)
- Quote: "In early development stages, LCA is most useful to identify hot spots for environmental improvement via contribution followed by a sensitivity analysis."
- Key point: In early development stages, LCA is most useful for identifying environmental "hot spots," followed by a sensitivity analysis.

### E9

- Location: Scope Definition › Defining Functional Units for CCU Technologies
- Quote: "Instead, the functional unit shall be defined so that the technical performance in the defined application of the products becomes comparable, e.g., compare detergents based on the washing performance and not based on mass."
- Key point: When the new product and the conventional product differ in chemical structure, define the functional unit by technical performance in the actual application, e.g., compare detergents by washing performance, not by mass.

### E10

- Location: Life Cycle Interpretation (interpreting carbon neutrality and negative emissions)
- Quote: "However, the negative LCA results does only reflect a comparison. In particular, negative LCA results do not necessarily imply that the CCU product is carbon neutral or even has negative emissions over its life cycle."
- Key point: A negative LCA result only reflects a comparison; it does not necessarily mean the product is carbon neutral or carbon negative over its life cycle.

### E11

- Location: Reporting
- Quote: "The main report shall report all assumptions, data for calculation, methods, results and limitations as transparently and detailed as possible."
- Key point: The main report shall state all assumptions, calculation data, methods, results, and limitations as transparently and in as much detail as possible.
