# US FDA 1997: Design Control Guidance for Medical Device Manufacturers (withdrawn)

- Source: `fda1997designcontrol`, [original (Internet Archive snapshot)](https://web.archive.org/web/20251214094548/https://www.fda.gov/media/116573/download)
- Version: Guidance issued on March 11, 1997 by the Center for Devices and Radiological Health of the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), explaining the design control requirements of the old 21 CFR 820.30. The FDA website returns 404 to scripts, so this excerpt was read from an Internet Archive snapshot of 2025-12-14. **This guidance was withdrawn in 2026 when the new Quality Management System Regulation took effect** (see `aabb2026qmsr`); it is cited here only as historical material for understanding the logic of design controls. Locations give the section name and paragraph subheading.

### E1

- Location: Section C. Design Input, "Research and Development" paragraph
- Quote: "Some manufacturers have difficulty in determining where research ends and development begins." / "It may be reasonable to develop a rapid prototype to explore the feasibility of an idea or design approach, for example, prior to developing design input requirements. But manufacturers should avoid falling into the trap of equating the prototype design with a finished product design."
- Key point: The line between research and development is hard to draw. Building a rapid prototype to explore whether an idea is feasible before writing design input requirements is reasonable, but the prototype design must not be treated as the finished design.

### E2

- Location: Section C. Design Input, "Research and Development" paragraph
- Quote: "Prototypes at this stage lack safety features and ancillary functions necessary for a finished product, and are developed under conditions which preclude adequate consideration of product variability due to manufacturing."
- Key point: Prototypes at this stage lack the safety features and ancillary functions a finished product needs, and cannot adequately account for the product variation that comes with volume manufacturing.

### E3

- Location: Section C. Design Input, "Concept Documents versus Design Input" paragraph
- Quote: "Even the simplest of terms can have enormous design implications." / "Thus, a concept document may be the starting point for development, but it is not the design input requirement."
- Key point: Even the simplest term (the guidance's example is "portable") can have enormous design implications; a concept document may be the starting point for development, but it is not the design input requirements.

### E4

- Location: Section C. Design Input, "Responsibility for Design Input Development" paragraph
- Quote: "Medical terminology is appropriate in requirements when the developers and reviewers are familiar with the language, but it is often preferable to translate the concepts into engineering terms at the requirements stage to minimize miscommunication with the development staff."
- Key point: Medical terminology is fine in requirements when developers and reviewers are familiar with it, but translating concepts into engineering language at the requirements stage often reduces misunderstandings with developers.

### E5

- Location: Section C. Design Input, opening discussion
- Quote: "If essential requirements are not identified until validation, expensive redesign and rework may be necessary before a design can be released to production."
- Key point: If essential requirements are not identified until validation, expensive redesign and rework may be needed.

### E6

- Location: Introduction, note on the design control diagram
- Quote: "In practice, feedback paths would be required between each phase of the process and previous phases, representing the iterative nature of product development."
- Key point: The diagram shows sequential phases ("waterfall" style), but in practice feedback paths are needed between phases, reflecting the iterative nature of product development.

### E7

- Location: Introduction, note on the design control diagram
- Quote: "As the figure illustrates, design validation encompasses verification and extends the assessment to address whether devices produced in accordance with the design actually satisfy user needs and intended uses."
- Key point: Design validation encompasses verification and goes further, assessing whether devices produced according to the design actually meet user needs and intended uses.

### E8

- Location: Section B. Design and Development Planning
- Quote: "For example, if clinical trials are anticipated, there may be tasks associated with appropriate regulatory requirements."
- Key point: If clinical trials are anticipated, the plan should include the associated regulatory tasks.

### E9

- Location: Section F. Design Verification, "Types of Verification Activities" paragraph
- Quote: "The basis of verification is a three-pronged approach involving tests, inspections, and analyses."
- Key point: Verification rests on three basic approaches: tests, inspections, and analyses.
