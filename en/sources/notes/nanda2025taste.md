# Nanda 2025: My Research Process: Understanding and Cultivating Research Taste

- Source: `nanda2025taste`, [original](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/Ldrss6o3tiKT6NdMm)
- Version: AI Alignment Forum, 2025-05-01, part 3 of a series. The author works on machine learning interpretability; only the parts that hold across fields are excerpted here. After HTML tags were stripped, bolded words in the original are followed by an extra space before the next punctuation mark (e.g., "a mentor ,"); quotes reproduce the extracted text as is.

### E1

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "While I believe research taste is very real, incredibly valuable, and a key differentiator I look for, I don't think it's mystical or innate. Talent plays an important role, but taste is largely learned, and with the right mindset you can learn faster."
- Key point: Research taste is real and important, but it is not mystical or innate; it is mostly learned.

### E2

- Location: What is research taste?
- Quote: "Research is full of key decisions that will affect the future of the project, without an obvious way to find the right answer: from choosing the research problem itself, to identifying which anomalies are and are not worth exploring, distinguishing an experiment that will be compelling from one that’ll have inconclusive results, etc."
- Key point: The author defines taste broadly: not only choosing a topic but every key decision without an obvious right answer during a project, such as which anomalies to chase and which experiment would be more convincing.

### E3

- Location: Where does taste come from?
- Quote: "If you're new to research, feeling like you lack "taste" is completely normal and expected. You don't need perfect judgment to start."
- Key point: If you are new to research, feeling that you lack taste is completely normal; you don't need perfect judgment to start.

### E4

- Location: How to learn it?
- Quote: "The core problem is you just don't get that much data [...] Generally the shorter a feedback loop is the more data you will get."
- Key point: The core reason taste is hard to learn is that you get little feedback; the shorter the feedback loop, the more "data" you get.

### E5

- Location: How to learn it?
- Quote: "To get more data the easiest way is to lean on sources of supervised data: ideally a mentor , or seeing what worked in papers . You can also get more from each data point - analyse it in detail before setting the feedback, predict your mentor’s answers before they give them , etc."
- Key point: Ways to speed up: lean on mentors and papers; before each piece of feedback, predict what your mentor will say.

### E6

- Location: Decomposing Research Taste › Conviction & Confidence
- Quote: "Confidence doesn't mean correctness . Generally people reach the level of having conviction far before they reach the level of having correct intuitions"
- Key point: Confidence does not mean correctness; people usually reach conviction long before they have correct intuitions.

### E7

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "Try to write down prior predictions , and why you think an idea is good, pursue it, and reflect on what happened ."
- Key point: Write down your predictions and why you think an idea is good beforehand, then reflect on the outcome afterward.

### E8

- Location: Cultivating Research Taste › Getting more data
- Quote: "Especially early on, projects where you can test intermediate hypotheses or get partial results relatively quickly can accelerate learning more than moonshots with year-long feedback loops."
- Key point: Early on, favor projects that yield intermediate results relatively quickly; you learn faster than from a "moonshot" with a year-long feedback cycle.

### E9

- Location: Conclusion: Patience and Process
- Quote: "It's perfectly normal, and indeed expected, to rely heavily on external guidance (like mentors or established research directions) early in your career."
- Key point: Relying heavily on mentors or established research directions early in your career is normal and expected.
