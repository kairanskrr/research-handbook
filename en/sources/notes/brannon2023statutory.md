# Brannon 2023: Statutory Interpretation: Theories, Tools, and Trends (Congressional Research Service report R45153)

- Source: `brannon2023statutory`, [Original (full report as publicly republished on EveryCRSReport.com)](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R45153.html); official page https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R45153 (script access was refused, so the public republication was read)
- Version: Congressional Research Service report R45153 by Valerie C. Brannon (Legislative Attorney), first issued 2018; the version read is the update of March 10, 2023. It covers how **US federal courts** interpret acts of Congress. Congressional Research Service reports are US federal government works and free to read. Locations are given as the report's section titles.

### E1

- Location: Summary
- Quote: "Proponents of the two main theories of statutory interpretation—purposivism and textualism—disagree about how judges can best adhere to this ideal of legislative supremacy. While purposivists argue that courts should prioritize interpretations that advance the statute's purpose, textualists maintain that judges should primarily confine their focus to the statute's text."
- Key point: The two main theories of US statutory interpretation: purposivism favors interpretations that advance the statute's purpose; textualism holds that judges should focus mainly on the statutory text.

### E2

- Location: Summary
- Quote: "Most frequently, judges rely on five types of interpretive tools. Although both purposivists and textualists may use any of these five tools, a judge's theory of statutory interpretation may influence the order in which these tools are applied and how much weight is given to each tool."
- Key point: Judges most often rely on five types of interpretive tools; both camps may use any of them, but a judge's theory of interpretation can affect the order in which the tools are applied and how much weight each gets.

### E3

- Location: Summary
- Quote: "First, judges often begin by looking to the ordinary meaning of the statutory text" / "Second, judges interpret specific provisions by looking to their broader statutory context" / "Third, judges may turn to the canons of construction , which are presumptions about how courts ordinarily read statutes." / "Fourth, judges may look to a statute's legislative history , or the record of Congress's deliberations when enacting a law." / "Finally, a judge might consider statutory implementation : the way a law has been applied in the past, or might be applied in the future."
- Key point: The five tools: the ordinary meaning of the text; the broader statutory context and structure; the canons of construction (presumptions about how courts usually read statutes); legislative history (the record of Congress's deliberations when enacting the law); and the statute's implementation (how it has been applied in the past and may be applied in the future).

### E4

- Location: Summary
- Quote: "Nonetheless, judges may disagree about what a word's ordinary meaning is, or whether a particular statutory term may instead be a term of art"
- Key point: Judges may disagree about a word's ordinary meaning, or about whether a term is a term of art with a specialized meaning.

### E5

- Location: Summary
- Quote: "Some judges have suggested certain forms of legislative history are more reliable than others, with committee reports providing stronger evidence of Congress's understanding of the law than individual floor statements from non-sponsors, for example."
- Key point: Some judges treat some forms of legislative history as more reliable than others; for example, committee reports are stronger evidence of Congress's understanding than floor statements by non-sponsors.

### E6

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "Even a statutory provision that at first appears unambiguous can engender significant difficulties when applied in the real world."
- Key point: Even a provision that at first appears unambiguous can create significant difficulties when applied in the real world.

### E7

- Location: Canons of Construction — Justifications: Disrepute, Rehabilitation, and Empirical Studies
- Quote: "He compiled a table of “thrusts” and “parries” that purported to demonstrate that for every canon, there was an opposing canon on the same point." / "parries are truly contradictory, arguing that many of his pairs instead represent two halves of one rule, the thrust giving the general rule, and the parry, the exception or condition."
- Key point: The American scholar Llewellyn compiled a table claiming that every canon has an opposing canon; other scholars and judges question whether the pairs really contradict each other, arguing that many are two halves of one rule: a general rule and its exception.
