# Pryor: Guidelines on Writing a Philosophy Paper

- Source: `pryor2012writing`, [original](https://www.jimpryor.net/teaching/guidelines/writing.html)
- Version: Writing guide on the personal website of Jim Pryor (philosophy teacher at New York University, US); the page says it was last updated September 6, 2012. Written for students in undergraduate philosophy courses (5-6 page course papers). Locations give subheadings.

### E1

- Location: What Does One Do in a Philosophy Paper?
- Quote: "A philosophy paper consists of the reasoned defense of some claim"
- Key point: A philosophy paper is the reasoned defense of some claim.

### E2

- Location: What Does One Do in a Philosophy Paper?
- Quote: "Your paper must offer an argument. It can't consist in the mere report of your opinions, nor in a mere report of the opinions of the philosophers we discuss."
- Key point: The paper must offer an argument; merely reporting your opinions, or the opinions of the philosophers discussed, is not enough.

### E3

- Location: What Does One Do in a Philosophy Paper?
- Quote: "It usually begins by putting some thesis or argument on the table for consideration. Then it goes on to do one or two of the following:"
- Key point: A philosophy paper usually puts a thesis or argument on the table, then does one or two things with it (see E4).

### E4

- Location: What Does One Do in a Philosophy Paper?
- Quote: "Criticize that argument; or show that certain arguments for the thesis are no good"
- Key point: One option: criticize the argument, or show that certain arguments for the thesis are no good. The same list includes defending it against criticism, offering reasons for it, giving counterexamples, comparing two opposing views, discussing the thesis's implications, and revising the thesis in light of objections.

### E5

- Location: What Does One Do in a Philosophy Paper?
- Quote: "A good philosophy paper is modest and makes a small point; but it makes that point clearly and straightforwardly, and it offers good reasons in support of it"
- Key point: A good philosophy paper is modest and makes a small point, but makes it clearly and straightforwardly, with good reasons.

### E6

- Location: What Does One Do in a Philosophy Paper?
- Quote: "People very often attempt to accomplish too much in a philosophy paper. The usual result of this is a paper that's hard to read, and which is full of inadequately defended and poorly explained claims."
- Key point: People often try to do too much in one paper; the result is hard to read and full of poorly defended, poorly explained claims.

### E7

- Location: Early Stages / Make an outline
- Quote: "I find that making an outline is at least 80% of the work of writing a good philosophy paper."
- Key point: Pryor finds that making an outline is at least 80% of the work of writing a good philosophy paper.

### E8

- Location: Write a Draft / Use simple prose
- Quote: "Don't shoot for literary elegance. Use simple, straightforward prose. Keep your sentences and paragraphs short."
- Key point: Don't aim for literary elegance; use simple, direct prose with short sentences and paragraphs.

### E9

- Location: Make the structure of your paper obvious
- Quote: "If you say "P. Thus Q." then you are claiming that P is a good reason to accept Q. You had better be right."
- Key point: Writing "P. Thus Q." claims that P is a good reason to accept Q; use connectives correctly.

### E10

- Location: Use plenty of examples and definitions
- Quote: "You should always make it clear how you understand these notions, even if they are familiar from everyday discourse."
- Key point: Make clear how you understand the key notions in your argument, even everyday ones.

### E11

- Location: Don't vary your vocabulary just for the sake of variety
- Quote: "If you call something "X" at the start of your paper, call it "X" all the way through."
- Key point: Call the same thing by the same name throughout; don't vary the word for variety's sake.

### E12

- Location: Presenting and assessing the views of others
- Quote: "Make sure you understand exactly what the position you're criticizing says. Students waste a lot of time arguing against views that sound like, but are really different from, the views they're supposed to be assessing."
- Key point: Make sure you understand exactly what the position you criticize says; students often attack a view that sounds similar but is actually different.

### E13

- Location: Presenting and assessing the views of others
- Quote: "But don't treat the philosopher or the views you're discussing as stupid. If they were stupid, we wouldn't be looking at them."
- Key point: Don't treat the philosopher or view you are discussing as stupid.

### E14

- Location: Presenting and assessing the views of others
- Quote: "Try to figure out what reasonable position the philosopher could have had in mind, and direct your arguments against that."
- Key point: Figure out what reasonable position the philosopher could have had in mind, and argue against that.

### E15

- Location: Presenting and assessing the views of others
- Quote: "In your paper, you always have to explain what a position says before you criticize it."
- Key point: Always explain what a position says before you criticize it.

### E16

- Location: Anticipate objections
- Quote: "Don't be afraid of mentioning objections to your own thesis. It is better to bring up an objection yourself than to hope your reader won't think of it."
- Key point: Don't be afraid to raise objections to your own thesis; better to raise them yourself than hope the reader won't think of them.

### E17

- Location: Anticipate objections
- Quote: "Of course, there's often no way to deal with all the objections someone might raise; so concentrate on the ones that seem strongest or most pressing."
- Key point: You can't answer every objection; concentrate on the strongest and most pressing.

### E18

- Location: What happens if you're stuck?
- Quote: "Your paper doesn't always have to provide a definite solution to a problem, or a straight yes or no answer to a question."
- Key point: A paper need not offer a definite solution or a straight yes-or-no answer.

### E19

- Location: What happens if you're stuck?
- Quote: "It's okay to change your thesis to one you can defend."
- Key point: If you find a problem in your argument you can't fix, you may change your thesis to one you can defend.

### E20

- Location: Rewrite, and Keep Rewriting
- Quote: "You should count on writing many drafts of your paper. At least 3 or 4!!"
- Key point: Expect to write many drafts, at least three or four.

### E21

- Location: Minor Points / Beginning your paper
- Quote: "Don't begin with a sentence like "Down through the ages, mankind has pondered the problem of..." There's no need to warm up to your topic."
- Key point: Don't warm up with openers like "Down through the ages, mankind has pondered..."; get to the point in the first sentence.

### E22

- Location: Minor Points / Beginning your paper
- Quote: "Dictionaries aren't good philosophical authorities."
- Key point: Dictionaries are not good philosophical authorities; don't open with a dictionary definition.

### E23

- Location: How You'll Be Graded
- Quote: "We do not judge your paper by whether we agree with its conclusion."
- Key point: A paper is not judged by whether the grader agrees with its conclusion (but by how well it argues).

### E24

- Location: Minor Points / Secondary readings
- Quote: "You shouldn't need to use these secondary readings when writing your papers."
- Key point: In Pryor's undergraduate courses, course papers should not need secondary readings beyond the assigned texts. (This applies only to course papers.)

### E25

- Location: Presenting and assessing the views of others
- Quote: "Sometimes you'll need to argue for your interpretation of X's view, by citing passages which support your interpretation. It is permissible for you to discuss a view you think a philosopher might have held, or should have held, though you can't find any direct evidence of that view in the text. When you do this, though, you should explicitly say so."
- Key point: Sometimes you need to cite passages to support your interpretation of a philosopher's view; you may discuss a view the philosopher might or should have held without direct textual evidence, but you must say so explicitly.

### E26

- Location: What Does One Do in a Philosophy Paper?
- Quote: "You should assume that your audience does not already accept your position; and you should treat your paper as an attempt to persuade such an audience. Hence, don't start with assumptions which your opponents are sure to reject."
- Key point: Assume your audience does not yet accept your position and treat the paper as an attempt to persuade them; so don't start from assumptions your opponents would reject.

### E27

- Location: What happens if you're stuck?
- Quote: "Many excellent philosophy papers don't offer straight yes or no answers. Sometimes they argue that the question needs to be clarified, or that certain further questions need to be raised. Sometimes they argue that certain assumptions of the question need to be challenged."
- Key point: Many excellent papers give no yes-or-no answer; instead they argue that the question needs clarifying, that further questions must be raised, or that some of its assumptions should be challenged.

### E28

- Location: What happens if you're stuck?
- Quote: "Sometimes they argue that certain answers to the question are too easy, that is, they won't work."
- Key point: You can also argue that certain answers are too easy and won't work.

### E29

- Location: Presenting and assessing the views of others
- Quote: "Then ask yourself: Are X's arguments good ones? Are his assumptions clearly stated? Are they plausible? Are they reasonable starting-points for X's argument, or ought he have provided some independent argument for them?"
- Key point: When evaluating someone's view, ask: Are the arguments good? Are the assumptions clearly stated? Are they plausible? Are they reasonable starting points, or should they have been argued for separately?

### E30

- Location: Be concise, but explain yourself fully
- Quote: "Formulate the central problem or question you wish to address at the beginning of your paper, and keep it in mind at all times. Make it clear what the problem is, and why it is a problem."
- Key point: State the central problem or question at the start of the paper and keep it in mind throughout; make clear what the problem is and why it is a problem.

### E31

- Location: Make an outline
- Quote: "It also helps ensure that you're in a position to say what your main argument or criticism is, before you sit down to write a full draft of your paper. When students get stuck writing, it's often because they haven't yet figured out what they're trying to say."
- Key point: An outline ensures you can state your main argument or criticism before drafting; getting stuck often means you haven't yet worked out what you want to say.

### E32

- Location: Quotations
- Quote: "If the quoted passage contains an argument, reconstruct the argument in more explicit, straightforward terms."
- Key point: If a quoted passage contains an argument, reconstruct it in more explicit, straightforward terms.

### E33

- Location: Anticipate objections
- Quote: "For instance, if you object to some philosopher's view, don't assume he would immediately admit defeat. Imagine what his comeback might be. How would you handle that comeback?"
- Key point: When you object to a philosopher, don't assume they would immediately concede; imagine their comeback and how you would handle it.

### E34

- Location: Your paper should do some philosophical work
- Quote: "Philosopher X assumes A and argues from there to B. B seems unattractive to me. Philosopher X just assumes A and doesn't give any argument for it. I don't think A is true. So I can just reject A and thereby avoid B."
- Key point: A common weak pattern: X assumes A and argues to B; I dislike B; X gives no argument for A; I don't think A is true; so I reject A.

### E35

- Location: Your paper should do some philosophical work
- Quote: "She could have argued that B doesn't really follow from A, after all. Or she could have presented reasons for thinking that A is false. Or she could have argued that assuming A is an illegitimate move to make in a debate about whether B is true."
- Key point: More interesting options: argue that B doesn't actually follow from A, give reasons to think A is false, or argue that assuming A is illegitimate in a debate about B.

### E36

- Location: Discuss the issues with others
- Quote: "It's even more valuable to talk to each other about what you want to argue in your paper. When you have your ideas worked out well enough that you can explain them to someone else, verbally, then you're ready to sit down and start making an outline."
- Key point: Talk with others about what you want to argue; once you can explain it to someone out loud, you are ready to outline.

### E37

- Location: Rewrite, and Keep Rewriting
- Quote: "Reading the paper out loud can help you notice holes in your reasoning, digressions, and unclear prose."
- Key point: Reading the paper aloud helps you notice holes in your reasoning, digressions, and unclear prose.

### E38

- Location: Rewrite, and Keep Rewriting
- Quote: "When you're revising a draft, it's much more important to work on the draft's structure and overall clarity, than it is to clean up a word or a phrase here or there."
- Key point: When revising, structure and overall clarity matter far more than tweaking a word here and there.

### E39

- Location: Be concise, but explain yourself fully
- Quote: "And he's mean, so he's not going to read your paper charitably. (For example, if something you say admits of more than one interpretation, he's going to assume you meant the less plausible thing.)"
- Key point: Imagine a "mean" reader: if a sentence admits more than one interpretation, he assumes you meant the less plausible one.

### E40

- Location: Make the structure of your paper obvious
- Quote: "make it explicit when you're reporting your own view and when you're reporting the views of some philosopher you're discussing. The reader should never be in doubt about whose claims you're presenting in a given paragraph."
- Key point: Make explicit which views are your own and which you are reporting; the reader should never be unsure whose claim a passage presents.

### E41

- Location: What happens if you're stuck?
- Quote: "It's perfectly okay to say that their strengths and weaknesses seem to be roughly equally balanced. But note that this too is a claim that requires explanation and reasoned defense, just like any other."
- Key point: You may say two positions seem about equally balanced, but that too is a claim that needs reasoned defense.

### E42

- Location: Rewrite, and Keep Rewriting
- Quote: "Make sure every sentence in your draft does useful work. Get rid of any which don't."
- Key point: Make every sentence do useful work; cut those that don't.

### E43

- Location: How You'll Be Graded
- Quote: "Is the structure of your paper clear? For instance, is it clear what parts of your paper are expository, and what parts are your own positive contribution?"
- Key point: Make the structure clear: which parts are exposition and which are your own positive contribution.

### E44

- Location: Use plenty of examples and definitions
- Quote: "So everything turns here on what the author means by "person." The author should be explicit about how he is using this notion."
- Key point: (Example: "a fetus is a person; killing a person is wrong; so killing a fetus is wrong.") Everything turns on what "person" means; the author must be explicit about how he uses the notion.

### E45

- Location: What happens if you're stuck?
- Quote: "If there's some problem with your argument which you can't fix, try to figure out why you can't fix it. It's okay to change your thesis to one you can defend."
- Key point: If your argument has a problem you can't fix, first work out why; you may change your thesis to one you can defend.

### E46

- Location: Presenting and assessing the views of others
- Quote: "Use your imagination. Try to figure out what reasonable position the philosopher could have had in mind, and direct your arguments against that."
- Key point: Imagine the reasonable position your opponent could hold, and argue against that.

### E47

- Location: Make the structure of your paper obvious
- Quote: "Don't throw in a "thus" or a "therefore" to make your train of thought sound better-argued than it really is."
- Key point: Don't throw in "thus" or "therefore" to make your train of thought sound better argued than it is.

### E48

- Location: Rewrite, and Keep Rewriting
- Quote: "If you can, show your draft to your friends or to other students in the class, and get their comments and advice."
- Key point: If you can, show your draft to friends or classmates and get their comments.

### E49

- Location: What happens if you're stuck?
- Quote: "If something in a view you're examining is unclear to you, don't gloss it over. Call attention to the unclarity. Suggest several different ways of understanding the view."
- Key point: If something in a view you examine is unclear, don't gloss over it; point out the unclarity and suggest several ways of understanding the view.
