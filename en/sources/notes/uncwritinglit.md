# University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill Writing Center: Literature (Fiction)

- Source: `uncwritinglit`, [original](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/literature/)
- Version: Free Writing Center handout (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0); accessed October 2026

### E1

- Location: Demystifying the process
- Quote: "literary analyses (or papers that offer an interpretation of literary texts) rely on the assumption that stories, poems, and plays must mean something."
- Key point: Literary analysis rests on the assumption that works mean something that can be interpreted.

### E2

- Location: Demystifying the process
- Quote: "To say that there is no one answer is not to say that anything we decide to say about a literary text is valid, interesting, or valuable. Interpretations of literature are often opinions, but not all opinions are equal."
- Key point: Having no single answer does not mean anything goes; some interpretations are better than others.

### E3

- Location: Demystifying the process
- Quote: "use careful reasoning to explain how that evidence relates to the main points of the interpretation."
- Key point: A good interpretation uses careful reasoning to explain how the evidence relates to its main points.

### E4

- Location: Demystifying the process
- Quote: "avoid the obvious (in other words, it won’t argue a conclusion that most readers could reach on their own from a general knowledge of the story)"
- Key point: A good interpretation avoids the obvious.

### E5

- Location: 2. Explore potential topics
- Quote: "A problem, on the other hand, is something that bugs you or that doesn’t seem to add up."
- Key point: A "problem" is something in the text that bugs you or doesn't add up, and it can be a source of topics.

### E6

- Location: 4. Write out a working thesis
- Quote: "A changing thesis statement is a good sign that you’re starting to say more interesting and complex things on your subject."
- Key point: A changing working thesis is a good sign.

### E7

- Location: 5. Make an extended list of evidence
- Quote: "As you make your notes keep track of page numbers so you can quickly find the passages in your book again"
- Key point: Keep track of page numbers as you take notes.

### E8

- Location: 9. Interpret your evidence
- Quote: "If you’re quoting passages without interpreting them, you’re not demonstrating your reasoning skills or helping the reader."
- Key point: Quoting without interpreting is not making an argument.

### E9

- Location: General hints
- Quote: "Your thesis should be able to answer the brutal question “so what?”"
- Key point: A thesis should answer "so what?"

### E10

- Location: General hints
- Quote: "Literature scholars have identified connections between literature and the following: economics, family dynamics, education, religion, mortality, law, politics, sexuality, history, psychology, the environment, technology, animality, citizenship, and migration, among others."
- Key point: Literature scholars connect literature with larger issues such as economics, religion, law, politics, history, and the environment.

### E11

- Location: General hints
- Quote: "Avoid going into a topic with a preconceived notion of what you’ll find."
- Key point: Don't go into a topic with a preconceived conclusion.

### E12

- Location: 2. Explore potential topics
- Quote: "A pattern can be the recurrence of certain kinds of imagery, vocabulary, formal elements (like rhyme and meter), or events."
- Key point: A "pattern" is the recurrence of imagery, vocabulary, formal elements, or events.

### E13

- Location: 2. Explore potential topics
- Quote: "chances are good that other people will have been perplexed by these moments as well and will be interested to see how you make sense of it all."
- Key point: What puzzles you has probably puzzled others too, and they will want to see how you make sense of it.

### E14

- Location: 3. Select a topic with a lot of evidence
- Quote: "narrow down your list by identifying how much evidence or how many specific details you could use to investigate each potential issue."
- Key point: Narrow down candidate topics by how much evidence and how many specific details you could use for each.

### E15

- Location: 3. Select a topic with a lot of evidence
- Quote: "Don’t launch into a topic without considering all the options first because you may end up with a topic that seemed promising initially but that only leads to a dead end."
- Key point: Compare the options before you start, or you may pick a topic that looks promising at first but leads to a dead end.

### E16

- Location: 5. Make an extended list of evidence
- Quote: "you also want to avoid the temptation to arrive at definite conclusions about your topic."
- Key point: While gathering evidence, resist jumping to definite conclusions.

### E17

- Location: 5. Make an extended list of evidence
- Quote: "These other examples might provide a context or some useful contrasts that could illuminate my evidence relating to Victor."
- Key point: Passages not directly about your topic can still provide context or contrasts that illuminate your core evidence.

### E18

- Location: 6. Select your evidence
- Quote: "First, select the facts which bear the closest relation to your thesis statement. Second, choose the pieces of evidence you’ll be able to say the most about."
- Key point: Two criteria for choosing evidence: it bears the closest relation to your thesis, and you can say the most about it.

### E19

- Location: 7. Refine your thesis
- Quote: "This step and the previous step (selecting evidence) are actually best done at the same time, since selecting your evidence and defining the focus of your paper depend upon each other."
- Key point: Selecting evidence and revising the thesis are best done at the same time, because each depends on the other.

### E20

- Location: 4. Write out a working thesis; 7. Refine your thesis
- Quote: "Victor Frankenstein’s tragic ambition was fueled by a faulty education." / "Victor Frankenstein’s education in unnaturally isolated environments fosters his tragic ambition."
- Key point: The handout's example: the working thesis moves from "a faulty education fueled his tragic ambition" to the more specific "education in unnaturally isolated environments fosters his tragic ambition."

### E21

- Location: 8. Organize your evidence
- Quote: "The ideas that tie these clusters of evidence together can then become the claims that you’ll make in your paper."
- Key point: Group similar pieces of evidence into clusters; the ideas that tie each cluster together become the claims of your paper.

### E22

- Location: General hints, Make your thesis relevant to your readers
- Quote: "Avoid writing a paper that identifies a pattern in a story but doesn’t quite explain why that pattern leads to an interesting interpretation."
- Key point: Don't just identify a pattern; explain why that pattern leads to an interesting interpretation.

### E23

- Location: General hints
- Quote: "Avoid going into a topic with a preconceived notion of what you’ll find. Be prepared to challenge your own ideas about what gender, race, or class mean in a particular text."
- Key point: Don't go into a topic with a preconceived conclusion; be prepared to challenge your own ideas about what concepts such as gender, race, or class mean in a particular text.

### E24

- Location: General hints, Make your thesis specific
- Quote: "A paper about education in general in Frankenstein will probably not get my reader’s attention as much as a more specific topic about the impact of the learning environment on the main character."
- Key point: A paper on "education" in general is weaker than one on something specific, such as "the impact of the learning environment on the main character."

### E25

- Location: 5. Make an extended list of evidence
- Quote: "A good interpretive paper does not need to cover all elements of the story"
- Key point: A good interpretive paper does not need to cover every element of the work.

### E26

- Location: Demystifying the process
- Quote: "To say that there is no one answer is not to say that anything we decide to say about a literary text is valid, interesting, or valuable."
- Key point: Having no single answer does not mean any claim is valid.

### E27

- Location: 5. Make an extended list of evidence
- Quote: "even just reaching a higher state of confusion is a good indicator that you’re treating your topic in a complex way"
- Key point: Getting more confused as you read can be a sign that you are engaging deeply with your topic.
