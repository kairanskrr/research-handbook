# Collier 2011: Understanding Process Tracing

- Source: `collier2011understanding`, [PS: Political Science & Politics 44(4): 823–830 (free PDF on the Cambridge University Press website)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/183A057AD6A36783E678CB37440346D1/S1049096511001429a.pdf/div-class-title-understanding-process-tracing-div.pdf)
- Version: The publisher's version of record, free to download from the Cambridge University Press website (no login needed). Locations give journal page numbers. The scanned version on the Berkeley website has a garbled text layer and was not used.

### E1

- Location: Introduction, p. 823
- Quote: "it is defined as the systematic examination of diagnostic evidence selected and analyzed in light of research questions and hypotheses posed by the investigator."
- Key point: Process tracing is the systematic examination of diagnostic evidence, selected and analyzed in light of the researcher's research questions and hypotheses.

### E2

- Location: Teaching Exercises, pp. 823–824
- Quote: "This new formulation of process tracing is accompanied by online teaching exercises" / "The Sherlock Holmes story "Silver Blaze" (posted online with the exercises) serves as the basis for an exercise, and also as a running example in parts of the following presentation."
- Key point: The article comes with online teaching exercises; the Sherlock Holmes story "Silver Blaze" is both an exercise and the running example in the text.

### E3

- Location: Descriptive Inference, p. 824
- Quote: "the descriptive component of process tracing begins not with observing change or sequence, but rather with taking good snapshots at a series of specific moments."
- Key point: The descriptive part of process tracing begins not with observing change or sequence but with taking good snapshots at a series of specific moments.

### E4

- Location: Causal Inference, p. 825
- Quote: "Careful, analytically informed specification of hypotheses is essential both in selecting and interpreting pieces of evidence, and in weighing them against one another."
- Key point: Careful, theoretically informed specification of hypotheses is essential for selecting, interpreting, and weighing pieces of evidence.

### E5

- Location: Causal Inference, p. 825
- Quote: "The distinctions in table 1 are a useful heuristic, but should not be taken rigidly. The decision to treat a given piece of evidence as the basis for one of the four tests can depend on the researcher's prior knowledge, the assumptions that underlie the study, and the specific formulation of the hypothesis."
- Key point: The four tests are a useful heuristic, not a rigid scheme; which test a piece of evidence counts as depends on the researcher's prior knowledge, assumptions, and the exact wording of the hypothesis.

### E6

- Location: Straw-in-the-Wind Tests, p. 826
- Quote: "Straw-in-the-wind tests thus provide neither a necessary nor a sufficient criterion for accepting or rejecting a hypothesis, and they only slightly weaken rival hypotheses."
- Key point: Straw-in-the-wind tests are neither necessary nor sufficient and only slightly weaken rival hypotheses.

### E7

- Location: Hoop Tests, p. 826
- Quote: "The hypothesis must "jump through the hoop" to remain under consideration, but passing the test does not by itself affirm the hypothesis." / "Hoop tests do not confirm a hypothesis, but they can eliminate it."
- Key point: A hypothesis must "jump through the hoop" to stay under consideration, but passing does not by itself confirm it; hoop tests cannot confirm a hypothesis, but they can eliminate it.

### E8

- Location: Smoking-Gun Tests, p. 827
- Quote: "a suspect who is caught holding a smoking gun is presumed guilty. However, those with no smoking gun may not be innocent. In other words, this provides a sufficient but not necessary criterion for accepting the causal inference."
- Key point: A suspect caught holding a smoking gun is presumed guilty, but someone without one is not necessarily innocent; the smoking-gun test is a sufficient but not necessary criterion.

### E9

- Location: Doubly Decisive Tests, p. 827
- Quote: "single tests that accomplish this are rare in social science, but this leverage may be achieved by combining multiple tests, which together support one explanation and eliminate all others."
- Key point: Single doubly decisive tests are rare in social science, but combining several tests can together support one explanation and eliminate the others.

### E10

- Location: Doubly Decisive Tests, p. 827
- Quote: "Put simply, when the investigator has eliminated all plausible alternatives, the remaining scenario must be the correct one."
- Key point: Elimination: once you have ruled out all plausible alternatives, the remaining explanation must be the correct one.

### E11

- Location: Conclusion, p. 828
- Quote: "Doubts may arise as to which causal-inference test is appropriate. The analysis may face standard problems of missing variables. Measurement error can be an issue, and probabilistic relationships are harder to address than in quantitative research."
- Key point: Process tracing has serious problems of its own: which test applies can be in doubt, variables may be missing, measurement error occurs, and probabilistic relationships are harder to handle than in quantitative research.

### E12

- Location: Conclusion, pp. 828–829
- Quote: "It can therefore be productive to start with a good narrative or with a timeline that lists the sequence of events. One can then explore the causal ideas embedded in the narratives, consider the kinds of evidence"
- Key point: It can help to start with a good narrative or a timeline of events, then draw out the causal ideas in it and consider what evidence you would need.
