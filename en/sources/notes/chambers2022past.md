# Chambers & Tzavella 2022: The past, present and future of Registered Reports

- Source: `chambers2022past`, [Original PDF (Cardiff University repository, author accepted manuscript)](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/145541/1/Chambers%26Tzavella2021-MS.pdf)
- Version: Published in Nature Human Behaviour 6:29–42. We use the author accepted manuscript; locations give the section and the (approximate) manuscript page.

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "Registered Reports are a form of empirical publication in which study proposals are peer reviewed and pre-accepted before research is undertaken."
- Key point: Registered Reports are a publication format in which the study proposal is peer reviewed and pre-accepted before the research is done.

### E2

- Location: Introduction, manuscript p. 3
- Quote: "proposals that are favourably assessed receive in principle acceptance (IPA), which commits the journal to publishing the final paper regardless of whether the hypotheses are supported, provided the authors adhere to their approved protocol and interpret the results in line with the evidence."
- Key point: A proposal that passes review receives in-principle acceptance (IPA): as long as the authors follow the approved protocol and interpret the results in line with the evidence, the journal publishes the paper whether or not the hypotheses are supported.

### E3

- Location: Present, Bias control paragraph, manuscript p. 7
- Quote: "a recent analysis of 296 hypotheses published across 127 RRs in different fields found that 60% of RRs report null results, approximately five times greater than the rate in regular articles" / "In psychology this difference is even more striking, with a new study finding that just 4% of regular articles failed to confirm the first hypothesis compared with 56% for RRs"
- Key point: Registered Reports report null (non-significant) results far more often than regular articles. In psychology, only 4% of regular articles fail to confirm their first hypothesis, against 56% of Registered Reports.

### E4

- Location: Present, Bias control paragraph, manuscript p. 7
- Quote: "it is possible that authors, knowing that their study will be published regardless of whether their hypotheses are supported, might employ the RR format to test riskier hypotheses."
- Key point: The authors caution that the gap may not all come from removing bias; authors may also use Registered Reports to test riskier hypotheses.

### E5

- Location: Present, Limitations and drawbacks paragraph, manuscript p. 12
- Quote: "they are particularly well suited to hypothesis-driven studies and are not designed to improve the robustness or transparency of purely exploratory science"
- Key point: Registered Reports suit hypothesis-driven studies and are not designed for purely exploratory research.

### E6

- Location: Present, Stage 1 delay and bureaucratic tennis paragraph, manuscript p. 13
- Quote: "the Stage 1 review time typically adds a period of several months between submitting a Stage 1 manuscript and the commencement of the research."
- Key point: Stage 1 review typically delays the start of the research by several months, which is an obstacle for people on short contracts or with funding that requires data collection to start right away.

### E7

- Location: Present, paragraph on the accessibility of Registered Reports, manuscript p. 6
- Quote: "the often substantial sample sizes needed for RRs to achieve minimum levels of statistical power required by many journals, combined with the time taken for Stage 1 review (see Limitations and drawbacks), could act as a deterrent"
- Key point: Many journals set a minimum statistical power for Registered Reports, which often means large samples; together with the time Stage 1 review takes, this can deter researchers with limited resources.

### E8

- Location: Box 1. Top tips for authors, manuscript p. 13
- Quote: "A well-justified sampling plan that meets journal requirements (e.g. 90% statistical power) should be formulated"
- Key point: Advice to authors: formulate a well-justified sampling plan that meets the journal's requirements, e.g., 90% statistical power.
