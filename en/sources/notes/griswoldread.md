# Griswold: How to Read an Engineering Research Paper

- Source: `griswoldread`, [Original](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~wgg/CSE210/howtoread.html)
- Version: UCSD CSE 210 course web page, year to be confirmed

### E1

- Location: Main text, "What are the motivations for this work?"
- Quote: "The people problem is the benefits that are desired in the world at large; for example some issue of quality of life, such as saved time or increased safety. The second part is the technical problem, which is: why doesn't the people problem have a trivial solution?"
- Key point: When reading a paper, look first for the motivation, which has two layers: the "people problem" (the benefits desired in the world) and the "technical problem" (why the people problem has no trivial solution).

### E2

- Location: Main text, "What are the motivations for this work?"
- Quote: "Finally, the motivation and statement of the problem are distilled into a research question, the question that the paper sets out to answer. This might be more focused than the problem stated at the outset. Oftentimes, one or more of these elements are not explicitly stated, making your job more difficult."
- Key point: The motivation and problem are distilled into a research question; these elements are often not stated explicitly, so the reader has to dig them out.

### E3

- Location: Main text, "What is the work's evaluation of the proposed solution?"
- Quote: "An idea alone is usually not adequate for publication of a research paper. This is the concrete engagement of the research question. What argument, implementation, and/or experiment makes the case for the value of the ideas?"
- Key point: Ask what argument, implementation, or experiment the paper uses to make the case for the value of its ideas.

### E4

- Location: Main text, "What is your analysis of the identified problem, idea and evaluation?"
- Quote: "Is this a good idea? What flaws do you perceive in the work? What are the most interesting points made? What are the most controversial ideas or points made?"
- Key point: Form your own judgment: is this a good idea, what flaws does it have, and what are its most interesting and most controversial points?

### E5

- Location: Main text, "What is your take-away message from this paper?"
- Quote: "Sum up the main implication of the paper from your perspective. This is useful for very quick review and refreshing your memory. It also forces you to try to identify the essence of the work."
- Key point: Sum up the paper's main implication from your own perspective; this helps quick review later and forces you to identify the essence of the work.

### E6

- Location: Main text, how to read a paper
- Quote: "I recommend that you keep a notebook on all the papers you read, or mark-up the papers themselves. [...] In practice, you are not done reading a paper until you can answer all the questions."
- Key point: Keep notes or mark up every paper you read; you are not done reading until you can answer all the questions above.

### E7

- Location: Main text, how to read a paper
- Quote: "If you find that filling out this form doesn't work for you, you can try writing a 250 word abstract of the paper--not rewriting the abstract at the front of the paper, but your abstract, capturing the above five issues from your perspective."
- Key point: You can write a 250-word abstract of your own for the paper: not a rewrite of its abstract, but your own summary of the motivation, solution, evaluation, and other key points.

### E8

- Location: Main text, how to read a paper
- Quote: "Taking time to writing down questions you have about the paper will often surface thoughts that were not initially articulated. Perhaps the paper was vague on key issues, or ignored issues that you think are important."
- Key point: Writing down your questions about a paper often surfaces thoughts you had not articulated, such as the paper being vague on key issues or ignoring important ones.
