# Percie du Sert et al. 2020: Reporting animal research: Explanation and elaboration for the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0

- Source: `perciedusert2020reporting`, [original](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.3000411)
- Version: PLoS Biology 18(7): e3000411, 2020, open access, read 2026-10-06. The item-by-item explanation document for ARRIVE 2.0. Locations give item numbers.

### E1

- Location: Item 1. Study design, 1a
- Quote: "1a. The groups being compared, including control groups. If no control group has been used, the rationale should be stated."
- Key point: State which groups were compared, including control groups; if there was no control group, give the rationale.

### E2

- Location: Item 1a, Explanation
- Quote: "Negative controls are used to determine whether a difference between groups is caused by the intervention (e.g., wild-type animals versus genetically modified animals, placebo versus active treatment, sham surgery versus surgical intervention). Positive controls can be used to support the interpretation of negative results or determine if an expected effect is detectable."
- Key point: Negative controls show whether a difference between groups is caused by the intervention (e.g., wild-type vs. genetically modified animals, placebo vs. active treatment, sham surgery vs. surgery); positive controls help interpret negative results or confirm that the expected effect can be detected.

### E3

- Location: Box 1. Glossary, Experimental unit
- Quote: "Experimental unit: Biological entity subjected to an intervention independently of all other units, such that it is possible to assign any two experimental units to different treatment groups."
- Key point: The experimental unit is the biological entity subjected to an intervention independently of all other units, such that any two units could be assigned to different treatment groups.

### E4

- Location: Item 1b, Explanation
- Quote: "Conflating experimental units with subsamples or repeated measurements can lead to artificial inflation of the sample size. For example, measurements from 50 individual cells from a single mouse represent n = 1 when the experimental unit is the mouse."
- Key point: Treating subsamples or repeated measurements as experimental units artificially inflates the sample size; for example, if the experimental unit is the mouse, 50 cells measured from one mouse still give n = 1.

### E5

- Location: Item 1b, Explanation
- Quote: "Reporting n = 50 in this case is an example of pseudoreplication. It underestimates the true variability in a study, which can lead to false positives and invalidate the analysis and resulting conclusions"
- Key point: Reporting n = 50 in this case is pseudoreplication: it underestimates the true variability, which can lead to false positives and invalidate the analysis and conclusions.

### E6

- Location: Item 1b, Explanation
- Quote: "However, the experimental unit may be the cage or the litter (e.g., a diet administered to a whole cage, or a treatment administered to a dam and investigated in her pups), or it could be part of the animal"
- Key point: The experimental unit is not always a single animal; it can be a cage, a litter, or part of an animal's body.

### E7

- Location: Item 4. Randomisation, Explanation
- Quote: "Selecting an animal ‘at random’ (i.e., haphazardly or arbitrarily) from a cage is not statistically random, as the process involves human judgement."
- Key point: Picking an animal "at random" (haphazardly) from a cage is not statistically random, because it involves human judgment.

### E8

- Location: Item 5. Blinding, item text and Explanation
- Quote: "Describe who was aware of the group allocation at the different stages of the experiment (during the allocation, the conduct of the experiment, the outcome assessment, and the data analysis)." / "Researchers often expect a particular outcome and can unintentionally influence the experiment or interpret the data in such a way as to support their preferred hypothesis. Blinding is a strategy used to minimise these subjective biases."
- Key point: State who knew the group allocation at each stage (allocation, conduct of the experiment, outcome assessment, data analysis). Researchers often expect a particular result and may unintentionally influence the experiment or the interpretation of data; blinding reduces this subjective bias.

### E9

- Location: Item 1a, Explanation (second half)
- Quote: "Selectively excluding some experimental groups (for example, because the data are inconsistent or conflict with the narrative of the paper) is misleading and should be avoided."
- Key point: Selectively excluding experimental groups because their data are inconsistent or conflict with the paper's narrative is misleading and should be avoided.

### E10

- Location: Item 1b, Explanation
- Quote: "Within a design, biological and technical factors will often be organised hierarchically, such as cells within animals and mitochondria within cells, or cages within rooms and animals within cages."
- Key point: Biological and technical factors are often nested hierarchically in a design: cells within animals and mitochondria within cells, or animals within cages and cages within rooms.

### E11

- Location: Item 1a, Explanation
- Quote: "Negative controls are used to determine whether a difference between groups is caused by the intervention" / "Positive controls can be used to support the interpretation of negative results or determine if an expected effect is detectable."
- Key point: Negative controls show whether a difference between groups is caused by the intervention; positive controls help interpret negative results or confirm that the experiment can detect the expected effect.

### E12

- Location: Item 13. Objectives, Explanation
- Quote: "A typical exploratory study may measure multiple outcomes and look for patterns in the data or relationships that can be used to generate hypotheses. It may also be a pilot study, which aims to inform the design or feasibility of larger subsequent experiments."
- Key point: An exploratory study measures multiple outcomes and looks for patterns in the data to generate hypotheses; it can also be a pilot study that informs the design and feasibility of a larger follow-up experiment.

### E13

- Location: Item 13. Objectives, Explanation
- Quote: "an experiment designed to detect a hypothesised effect will likely need to be analysed with inferential statistics, and a statistical estimation of the sample size will need to be performed a priori" / "Hypothesis-testing studies also have a predefined primary outcome measure"
- Key point: An experiment testing a hypothesis needs inferential statistics, an a priori sample size estimate, and a predefined primary outcome measure.

### E14

- Location: Item 2b, Explanation
- Quote: "For any type of experiment, it is crucial to explain how the sample size was determined." / "Low power has three effects: first, within the experiment, real effects are more likely to be missed; second, when an effect is detected, this will often be an overestimation of the true effect size"
- Key point: For any experiment, explain how the sample size was determined. Low statistical power means real effects are more likely to be missed, and effects that are detected are often overestimated.

### E15

- Location: Item 2b, Box 3 and Explanation
- Quote: "Sample size calculation is based on a mathematical relationship between the following parameters: effect size, variability, significance level, power, and sample size." / "Consultation with a statistician is recommended, especially when the experimental design is complex or unusual."
- Key point: Sample size calculation rests on the mathematical relationship among effect size, variability, significance level, power, and sample size; for complex or unusual designs, consult a statistician.

### E16

- Location: Item 2b, Explanation
- Quote: "When the objective of the preliminary study is primarily logistic or operational (e.g., to improve procedures and equipment), the number of animals needed is generally small. In such cases, power calculations are not appropriate" / "Pilot studies alone are unlikely to provide adequate data on variability for a power calculation for future experiments."
- Key point: A pilot study aimed mainly at improving procedures and equipment usually needs few animals and is not suited to a power calculation; pilot studies alone are also unlikely to give enough variability data for powering later experiments.

### E17

- Location: Item 3a, Explanation
- Quote: "To ensure scientific rigour, the criteria should be defined before the experiment starts and data are collected" / "Criteria for excluding samples or data include failure to meet quality control standards, such as insufficient sample volumes, unacceptable levels of contaminants, poor histological quality, etc."
- Key point: Define inclusion and exclusion criteria before the experiment starts and data are collected; criteria for excluding samples or data include failing quality control standards, such as insufficient sample volume, unacceptable contamination, or poor-quality tissue sections.

### E18

- Location: Item 3a, Explanation
- Quote: "At the very least, these criteria should be documented in a laboratory notebook and reported in manuscripts, explicitly stating that the criteria were defined before any data"
- Key point: At the very least, record exclusion criteria in the lab notebook and report them in the paper, stating explicitly that they were defined before data collection.

### E19

- Location: Item 3b, Explanation
- Quote: "In some instances, it may be scientifically justifiable to remove outlying data points from an analysis, such as obvious errors in data entry or measurement with readings that are outside a plausible range." / "providing the reasoning for removing data points enables the distinction to be made between responsible data cleaning and data manipulation"
- Key point: Removing outlying data points is sometimes scientifically justified, e.g., obvious data entry errors or readings outside a plausible range; stating the reason lets readers distinguish responsible data cleaning from data manipulation.

### E20

- Location: Item 3b, Explanation
- Quote: "Explicitly state when built-in models in statistics packages have been used to remove outliers"
- Key point: State explicitly when built-in outlier tests in statistics packages were used to remove data.

### E21

- Location: Item 4, Box 4
- Quote: "Blocking is a method of controlling natural variation among experimental units. This splits up the experiment into smaller subexperiments (blocks), and treatments are randomised to experimental units within each block" / "This takes into account nuisance variables that could potentially bias the results (e.g., cage location, day or week of procedure)."
- Key point: A blocked design splits the experiment into smaller subexperiments (blocks) and randomizes treatments within each block, to control nuisance variables that could cause bias, such as cage position or the day or week of testing.

### E22

- Location: Item 4b, Explanation
- Quote: "Strategies to report include standardising (keeping conditions the same, e.g., all surgeries done by the same surgeon), randomising (e.g., the sampling or measurement order), and blocking or counterbalancing"
- Key point: Ways to reduce systematic differences between groups: standardizing (keeping conditions the same), randomizing (e.g., the order of sampling or measurement), and blocking or counterbalancing.

### E23

- Location: Item 5, Explanation
- Quote: "If this is not possible for every stage of an experiment (see Box 5), it should always be possible to conduct at least some of the stages blind. This has implications for the organisation of the experiment and may require help from additional personnel" / "Blinding is especially important during outcome assessment, particularly if there is a subjective element (e.g., when assessing behavioural changes or reading histological slides)"
- Key point: If not every stage can be blinded, at least some stages always can be, which may require help from others; blind outcome assessment is especially important when it involves subjective judgment, such as reading tissue sections.

### E24

- Location: Item 5, Explanation
- Quote: "There are, however, compelling data from systematic reviews showing that nonblinded outcome assessment leads to the treatment effects being overestimated, and the lack of bias-reducing measures such as randomisation and blinding can contribute to as much as 30%–45% inflation of effect sizes"
- Key point: Data from systematic reviews show that lacking bias-reducing measures such as randomization and blinding can inflate effect sizes by as much as 30%-45%.

### E25

- Location: Item 6b, Explanation
- Quote: "Studies that claim to test a hypothesis but do not specify a predefined primary outcome measure or those that change the primary outcome measure after data were collected (also known as primary outcome switching) are liable to selectively report only statistically significant results" / "Registering a protocol in advance protects the researcher against concerns about selective outcome reporting (also known as data dredging or p-hacking)"
- Key point: Studies that claim to test a hypothesis without a predefined primary outcome, or that switch the primary outcome after data collection, invite reporting only the significant results; registering the protocol in advance protects against suspicion of selective outcome reporting (also called data dredging or p-hacking).

### E26

- Location: Item 7a, Explanation
- Quote: "The statistical analysis methods implemented will reflect the goals and the design of the experiment; they should be decided in advance before data are collected"
- Key point: Statistical analysis methods should reflect the goals and design of the experiment and be decided before data collection.

### E27

- Location: Item 10a, Explanation
- Quote: "If a complete experiment is repeated on a different day or under different conditions, report the results of all repeats rather than selecting data from representative experiments."
- Key point: If a complete experiment was repeated on different days or under different conditions, report the results of all repeats, not data picked from "representative experiments."

### E28

- Location: Item 9a, Explanation
- Quote: "Provide enough detail to enable others to replicate the methods and highlight any quality assurance and quality control used" / "When available, cite the Research Resource Identifier (RRID) for reagents and tools used"
- Key point: Describe methods in enough detail for others to replicate them and state any quality assurance and quality control used; cite the Research Resource Identifier (RRID) for reagents and tools that have one.

### E29

- Location: Item 1a, Explanation
- Quote: "A pilot study, such as one designed to test the feasibility of a procedure, might also not require a control group."
- Key point: A pilot study testing the feasibility of a procedure may not need a control group.

### E30

- Location: Item 6b, Explanation
- Quote: "For exploratory studies, it is not necessary to identify a single primary outcome, and often multiple outcomes are assessed"
- Key point: Exploratory studies need not identify a single primary outcome and often assess several.

### E31

- Location: Item 7a, Explanation
- Quote: "Factors deemed to affect the variability of the outcome measure should also be handled in the analysis, for example, as a blocking factor (e.g., batch of reagent or experimenter)"
- Key point: Factors that may affect the variability of the outcome (e.g., reagent batch, experimenter) should also be handled in the analysis, for example as blocking factors.

### E32

- Location: Item 12b, Explanation
- Quote: "Include information on the rationale for choosing a particular species and explain how the outcome measures assessed are relevant to the condition under study and how the model was validated. Stating that an animal model is commonly used in the field is not appropriate, and a well-considered, detailed rationale should be provided."
- Key point: Explain why you chose the species, how the outcome measures relate to the condition under study, and how the model was validated; saying "this model is commonly used in the field" is not enough; give a considered, specific rationale.

### E33

- Location: Item 12a, Explanation
- Quote: "Scientific background information for an animal study should demonstrate a clear evidence gap and explain why an in vivo approach was warranted. Systematic reviews of the animal literature provide the most convincing evidence that a research question has not been conclusively addressed, by showing the extent of current evidence within a field of research. They can also inform the choice of animal model by providing a comprehensive overview of the models used along with their benefits and limitations"
- Key point: The background should show a clear evidence gap and why an in vivo approach is needed; systematic reviews of the animal literature best show that the question is unresolved and also help you choose a model, since they list the models used and their strengths and weaknesses.

### E34

- Location: Box 3. Information used in a power calculation
- Quote: "The effect size is estimated as a biologically relevant change in the primary outcome measure between the groups under study. This can be informed by similar studies and involves scientists exploring what magnitude of effect would generate interest and would be worth taking forward into further work. In preclinical studies, the clinical relevance of the effect should also be taken into consideration."
- Key point: Set the effect size as a biologically relevant change in the primary outcome, informed by similar studies and by thinking through how large an effect would be worth pursuing; in preclinical research, also consider whether the effect is clinically meaningful.

### E35

- Location: Box 3
- Quote: "A target power between 80% and 95% is normally deemed acceptable, which entails a risk of false negative between 5% and 20%."
- Key point: Target power is usually set between 80% and 95%, corresponding to a 5%-20% risk of a false negative.

### E36

- Location: Box 3
- Quote: "Pilot studies alone are unlikely to provide adequate data on variability for a power calculation for future experiments. Systematic reviews and previous studies are more appropriate sources of information on variability"
- Key point: Pilot studies alone rarely give adequate variability data to size later experiments; systematic reviews and previous studies are better sources.

### E37

- Location: Item 2b, Explanation
- Quote: "Sample sizes that are too small (i.e., underpowered studies) produce inconclusive results, whereas sample sizes that are too large (i.e., overpowered studies) raise ethical issues over unnecessary use of animals and may produce trivial findings that are statistically significant but not biologically relevant"
- Key point: Samples that are too small give inconclusive results; samples that are too large use more animals than needed, raising ethical issues, and may yield statistically significant but biologically meaningless results.

### E38

- Location: Item 2b, Explanation
- Quote: "Low power has three effects: first, within the experiment, real effects are more likely to be missed; second, when an effect is detected, this will often be an overestimation of the true effect size; and finally, when low power is combined with publication bias, there is an increase in the false positive rate in the published literature"
- Key point: Low power has three consequences: real effects are more likely to be missed, detected effects are often overestimated, and, combined with publication bias, false positives increase in the published literature.

### E39

- Location: Box 3
- Quote: "If no power calculation was used to determine the sample size, state this explicitly and provide the reasoning that was used to decide on the sample size per group. Regardless of whether a power calculation was used or not, when explaining how the sample size was determined take into consideration any anticipated loss of animals or data"
- Key point: If no power calculation was done, say so and give the reasoning for the sample size per group; either way, account for expected losses of animals or data when setting the sample size.

### E40

- Location: Box 3
- Quote: "For example, the number of animals needed for antibody or tissue production is determined by the amount required and the production ability of an individual animal." / "When the objective of the preliminary study is primarily logistic or operational (e.g., to improve procedures and equipment), the number of animals needed is generally small. In such cases, power calculations are not appropriate"
- Key point: For some studies an a priori power calculation is inappropriate, e.g., animals used to produce antibodies or tissue, whose number depends on how much is needed; pilot studies mainly for working out procedures or testing equipment usually need few animals and are not suited to power analysis either.

### E41

- Location: Item 4, Explanation
- Quote: "Selecting an animal ‘at random’ (i.e., haphazardly or arbitrarily) from a cage is not statistically random, as the process involves human judgement." / "Inferential statistics based on nonrandomised group allocation are not valid"
- Key point: Grabbing an animal "at random" from a cage is not statistically random, because it involves human judgment; without randomization, inferential statistics are not valid.

### E42

- Location: Item 4, Explanation
- Quote: "Examples of appropriate randomisation methods include online random number generators (e.g., https://www.graphpad.com/quickcalcs/randomize1/) or a function like Rand() in spreadsheet software such as Excel, Google Sheets, or LibreOffice."
- Key point: Suitable randomization methods include online random number generators or a function like Rand() in spreadsheet software.

### E43

- Location: Box 4. Considerations for the randomisation strategy
- Quote: "All animals/samples are simultaneously randomised to the treatment groups without considering any other variable. This strategy is rarely appropriate, as it cannot ensure that comparison groups are balanced for other variables that might influence the result of an experiment." / "This splits up the experiment into smaller subexperiments (blocks), and treatments are randomised to experimental units within each block"
- Key point: Simple randomization ignores other variables and is rarely appropriate, because it cannot ensure groups are balanced on other variables that may affect the outcome; block randomization splits the experiment into smaller subexperiments (blocks) and randomizes treatments within each block.

### E44

- Location: Box 4
- Quote: "If blocking factors are used in the randomisation, they should also be included in the analysis."
- Key point: If blocking factors were used in randomization, include them in the analysis too.

### E45

- Location: Box 4, Examples of nuisance variables
- Quote: "Time or day of the experiment" / "Litter, cage, or fish tank" / "Investigator or surgeon—different level of experience in the people administering the treatments, performing the surgeries, or assessing the results may result in varying stress levels in the animals or duration of anaesthesia"
- Key point: Nuisance variables that can be built into the randomization strategy include time or day of the experiment; litter, cage, or fish tank; and investigator or surgeon (differing experience may change animals' stress levels or anesthesia duration).

### E46

- Location: Item 4b, Explanation
- Quote: "For example, rodents in cages at the top of the rack may be exposed to higher light levels, which can affect stress" / "Strategies to report include standardising (keeping conditions the same, e.g., all surgeries done by the same surgeon), randomising (e.g., the sampling or measurement order), and blocking or counterbalancing (e.g., position of animal cages or tanks on the rack)"
- Key point: For example, rodents in cages at the top of the rack get more light, which can affect stress; controls include standardizing (e.g., one surgeon does all surgeries), randomizing (e.g., sampling or measurement order), and blocking or counterbalancing (e.g., cage position on the rack).

### E47

- Location: Item 5, Explanation
- Quote: "If this is not possible for every stage of an experiment (see Box 5), it should always be possible to conduct at least some of the stages blind. This has implications for the organisation of the experiment and may require help from additional personnel—for example, a surgeon to perform interventions, a technician to code the treatment syringes for each animal, or a colleague to code the treatment groups for the analysis."
- Key point: If not every stage can be blinded, at least some stages always can; this needs organizing and may require help from others, e.g., a surgeon performs the intervention, a technician codes each animal's syringe, and a colleague codes the groups for analysis.

### E48

- Location: Box 5, During allocation
- Quote: "Allocation concealment refers to concealing the treatment to be allocated to each individual animal from those assigning the animals to groups, until the time of assignment. Together with randomisation, allocation concealment helps minimise selection bias"
- Key point: Allocation concealment means that, until the moment of assignment, the person allocating animals does not know which treatment the next animal will receive; together with randomization, it reduces selection bias.

### E49

- Location: Box 5, During the outcome assessment
- Quote: "Blinding is especially important during outcome assessment, particularly if there is a subjective element (e.g., when assessing behavioural changes or reading histological slides)" / "If the person assessing the outcome cannot be blinded to the group allocation (e.g., obvious phenotypic or behavioural differences between groups), some, but not all, of the sources of bias could be mitigated by sending data for analysis to a third party"
- Key point: Blinding is especially important during outcome assessment, particularly when it has a subjective element (e.g., assessing behavioral changes or reading histological slides); when the assessor cannot be blinded (e.g., groups have obviously different phenotypes), having a third party analyze the data can reduce some bias.

### E50

- Location: Box 5, During the data analysis
- Quote: "This type of blinding is often neglected but is important, as the analyst makes many semisubjective decisions such as applying data transformation to outcome measures, choosing methods for handling missing data, and handling outliers." / "Data can be coded prior to analysis so that the treatment group cannot be identified before analysis is completed."
- Key point: Blinding during analysis is often neglected, yet the analyst makes many semi-subjective decisions (data transformations, missing values, outliers); code the data before analysis and reveal the groups only afterward.

### E51

- Location: Item 3, Explanation
- Quote: "To ensure scientific rigour, the criteria should be defined before the experiment starts and data are collected"
- Key point: Define inclusion and exclusion criteria before the experiment starts and data are collected.

### E52

- Location: Item 3a, Explanation
- Quote: "At the very least, these criteria should be documented in a laboratory notebook and reported in manuscripts, explicitly stating that the criteria were defined before any data was collected."
- Key point: At the very least, record these criteria in the lab notebook and state in the paper that they were defined before any data were collected.

### E53

- Location: Item 3b, Explanation
- Quote: "Reporting exclusions and attritions provides valuable information to other investigators evaluating the results or who intend to repeat the experiment or test the intervention in other species. It may also provide important safety information for human trials (e.g., exclusions related to adverse effects)."
- Key point: Reporting exclusions and attrition helps others evaluate the results, repeat the experiment, or test the intervention in other species, and may give important safety information for human trials (e.g., animals excluded because of adverse effects).

### E54

- Location: Item 6b, Explanation
- Quote: "In a hypothesis-testing experiment, the primary outcome measure answers the main biological question. It is the outcome of greatest importance, identified in the planning stages of the experiment and used as the basis for the sample size calculation"
- Key point: In a hypothesis-testing experiment, the primary outcome measure answers the main question; it is identified at the planning stage and used to calculate the sample size.

### E55

- Location: Item 6a, Explanation
- Quote: "Failure to disclose all the outcomes that were measured introduces bias in the literature, as positive outcomes (e.g., those statistically significant) are reported more often"
- Key point: Failing to report every outcome measured biases the literature, because positive results are reported more often.

### E56

- Location: Item 13, Explanation
- Quote: "Knowing whether a study is exploratory or hypothesis-testing is critical to its interpretation." / "In contrast, exploratory research investigates many possible effects and is likely to yield more false positive results because some will be positive by chance."
- Key point: Whether a study is exploratory or hypothesis-testing determines how its results should be interpreted; exploratory research investigates many possible effects, some of which will be positive by chance, so it yields more false positives.

### E57

- Location: Item 13, Explanation
- Quote: "Hypothesis-testing studies may collect additional information for exploratory purposes; it is important to distinguish which hypotheses were prespecified and which originated after data inspection"
- Key point: Hypothesis-testing studies may also collect information for exploratory purposes, but must distinguish which hypotheses were prespecified and which arose after looking at the data.

### E58

- Location: Item 17a, Explanation
- Quote: "For hypothesis-testing studies, interpretations should be restricted to the primary outcome (see Item 6. Outcome measures). Exploratory results derived from additional outcomes should not be described as conclusive, as they may be underpowered and less reliable."
- Key point: Conclusions of hypothesis-testing studies should be restricted to the primary outcome; exploratory results from other outcomes may be underpowered and less reliable and should not be presented as conclusive.

### E59

- Location: Item 8a, Explanation
- Quote: "The species, strain, substrain, sex, weight, and age of animals are critical factors that can influence most experimental results" / "(e.g., a mouse referred to as an adult can vary in age from 6 to 20 weeks"
- Key point: Species, strain, substrain, sex, weight, and age affect most experimental results; an "adult mouse," for example, can be anywhere from 6 to 20 weeks old, so give the exact age.

### E60

- Location: Item 8b, Explanation
- Quote: "For example, animals of the same strain but from different sources, or animals obtained from the same source but at different times, may be genetically different"
- Key point: Animals of the same strain from different sources, or from the same source obtained at different times, may be genetically different.

### E61

- Location: Item 15, Explanation
- Quote: "The environment determines the health and wellbeing of the animals, and every aspect of it can potentially affect their behavioural and physiological responses, thereby affecting research outcomes"
- Key point: The housing environment determines animals' health and wellbeing, and every aspect of it can affect their behavioral and physiological responses, and hence the research outcomes.

### E62

- Location: Item 15, Explanation
- Quote: "If no environmental enrichment was provided, this should be clearly stated with justification. Similarly, scientific justification needs to be reported for withholding food and water and for singly housing animals"
- Key point: If no environmental enrichment was provided, say so and justify it; withholding food or water and single housing also need scientific justification.

### E63

- Location: Items 9a, 9b, Explanation
- Quote: "Animal handling can be a source of stress, and the specific method used (e.g., mice picked up by tail or in cupped hands) can affect research outcomes" / "Along with innate circadian rhythms, these can affect research outcomes such as behavioural, physiological, and immunological parameters"
- Key point: How animals are handled (e.g., picked up by the tail or in cupped hands) can cause stress and affect results; light cycles and the timing of dosing and measurement, together with animals' innate circadian rhythms, affect behavioral, physiological, and immune measures.

### E64

- Location: Item 14, Explanation
- Quote: "This includes ensuring that they have the relevant approval for their study from an appropriate ethics committee and/or regulatory body before the work starts."
- Key point: Authors must make sure they have approval from an appropriate ethics committee and/or regulatory body before the study starts.

### E65

- Location: Item 16c, Explanation
- Quote: "Humane endpoints are predetermined morphological, physiological, and/or behavioural signs that define the circumstances under which an animal will be removed from an experimental study."
- Key point: Humane endpoints are predetermined morphological, physiological, or behavioral signs that, once seen, mean the animal is removed from the experiment.

### E66

- Location: Item 19, Explanation
- Quote: "Registering a protocol before the start of the experiment enables researchers to demonstrate that the hypothesis, approach, and analysis were planned in advance and not shaped by data as they emerged; it enhances scientific rigour and protects the researcher against concerns about selective reporting of results"
- Key point: Registering the protocol before the experiment starts shows that the hypothesis, approach, and analysis were planned in advance rather than shaped by the data; this improves rigor and protects researchers from suspicions of selective reporting.

### E67

- Location: Item 19, Explanation
- Quote: "A protocol should consist of (1) the question being addressed and the key features of the research that is proposed, such as the hypothesis being tested, the primary outcome measure (if applicable), and the statistical analysis plan; and (2) the laboratory procedures to be used to perform the planned experiment."
- Key point: A protocol should include (1) the research question and key design features, such as the hypothesis, primary outcome measure, and statistical analysis plan, and (2) the procedures the experiment will use.

### E68

- Location: Item 19, Explanation
- Quote: "Other online resources include the Open Science Framework, which is suitable to deposit PHISPS (Population; Hypothesis; Intervention; Statistical Analysis Plan; Primary; Outcome Measure; Sample Size Calculation) protocols and provide researchers with the flexibility to embargo the preregistration, keep it from public view until the research is published"
- Key point: The Open Science Framework is suitable for depositing a protocol covering population, hypothesis, intervention, statistical analysis plan, primary outcome measure, and sample size calculation, and can keep it embargoed until the paper is published.

### E69

- Location: Item 19, Explanation
- Quote: "When there have been deviations from the protocol, describe the rationale for these changes in the publication so that readers can take this into account when assessing the findings."
- Key point: If you deviated from the protocol, explain the reasons in the paper so readers can take them into account when assessing the findings.

### E70

- Location: Item 18, Explanation
- Quote: "Similarly, human clinical trials initiated based on insufficient or misleading animal research evidence increase research waste and negatively influence the risk-benefit balance for research participants"
- Key point: Starting human clinical trials on insufficient or misleading animal evidence increases research waste and upsets the balance of risks and benefits for trial participants.

### E71

- Location: Item 8a, Explanation
- Quote: "Reporting the weight or the sex of the animals used may not feasible for all studies. For example, sex may be unknown for embryos or juveniles" / "If reporting these characteristics can be reasonably expected for the species used and the experimental setting but are not reported, provide a justification."
- Key point: Not every study can report weight or sex; for example, sex may be unknown for embryos or juveniles; if they could reasonably be reported for the species and experiment but were not, give the reason.

### E72
