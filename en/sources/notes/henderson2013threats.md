# Henderson et al. 2013: Threats to Validity in the Design and Conduct of Preclinical Efficacy Studies

- Source: `henderson2013threats`, [Original](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1001489)
- Version: PLoS Medicine 10(7): e1001489, 2013, open access, accessed 2026-10-06. A systematic review of 26 guidelines on the design of preclinical animal experiments. Locations are given as subheadings.

### E1

- Location: Abstract, Methods and Findings
- Quote: "From these, we identified 26 guidelines that met our eligibility criteria—most of which were directed at neurological or cerebrovascular drug development. Together, these guidelines offered 55 different recommendations. Some of the most common recommendations included performance of a power calculation to determine sample size, randomized treatment allocation, and characterization of disease phenotype in the animal model prior to experimentation."
- Key point: The review included 26 guidelines, mostly for neurological and cerebrovascular drug development, with 55 recommendations in total; the most common included using a power calculation to set sample size, randomly allocating treatments, and characterizing the disease phenotype of the animal model before the experiment.

### E2

- Location: Editors' Summary, Why Was This Study Done?
- Quote: "Internal threats to validity are factors that confound reliable inferences about treatment–effect relationships in preclinical research." / "Construct threats to validity arise when researchers mischaracterize the relationship between an experimental system and the clinical disease it is intended to represent." / "External threats to validity are unseen factors that frustrate the transfer of treatment–effect relationships from animal models to patients."
- Key point: Threats to internal validity are factors that confound inferences about treatment–effect relationships; threats to construct validity arise from mischaracterizing the relationship between the experimental system and the clinical disease it represents; threats to external validity are unknown factors that prevent a treatment–effect relationship from transferring from the animal model to patients.

### E3

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "For instance, a researcher might err in using only rotational behavior in animals to represent human parkinsonism—a condition with a complex clinical presentation including tremor and cognitive symptoms."
- Key point: Example: using only rotational behavior in animals to represent human Parkinson's disease is a mistake, because the human condition has a complex clinical presentation including tremor and cognitive symptoms.

### E4

- Location: Box 1. Construct Validity and Preclinical Research
- Quote: "First, preclinical researchers might use treatments, animal models, or outcome assessments that are poorly matched to the clinical setting, as when preclinical studies use an acute disease model to represent a chronic disease in human beings."
- Key point: One threat to construct validity is a mismatch between the treatment, model, or outcome assessment and the clinical setting, such as using an acute disease model to represent a chronic human disease.

### E5

- Location: Box 1
- Quote: "For example, researchers intending to represent intravenous drug administration can introduce a threat to construct validity if, when performing tail vein administration in rats, they inadvertently administer a drug subcutaneously."
- Key point: Another threat is a procedure done wrong, for example intending tail vein injection to represent intravenous administration but inadvertently injecting subcutaneously.

### E6

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "Responses in an inbred mouse, for example, may be particular to the strain, thus limiting generalizability to other mouse models or patients." / "Researchers often address threats to external validity by replicating treatment effects in multiple model systems, or using multiple treatment formulations."
- Key point: Responses in an inbred mouse may be specific to that strain and hard to generalize to other models or to patients; researchers often address threats to external validity by replicating treatment effects in multiple model systems.

### E7

- Location: Results, Threats to Internal Validity, Construct Validity, and External Validity
- Quote: "Nine concerned matching the procedures used in preclinical studies—such as timing of drug delivery—to those planned for clinical studies."
- Key point: Of 25 recommendations on construct validity, 9 concerned matching preclinical procedures (such as the timing of drug delivery) to those planned for clinical studies.

### E8

- Location: Results, Threats to Internal Validity, Construct Validity, and External Validity
- Quote: "The most common was the recommendation that researchers reproduce their treatment effects in more than one animal model type, followed closely by independent replication of experiments"
- Key point: The most common external validity recommendation was to reproduce treatment effects in more than one animal model type, followed by independent replication of experiments.

### E9

- Location: Editors' Summary, What Did the Researchers Do and Find?
- Quote: "The most commonly included recommendations of this type called for the use of power calculations to ensure that sample sizes are large enough to yield statistically meaningful results, random allocation of animals to treatment groups, and “blinding” of researchers who assess outcomes to treatment allocation. Among the 25 recommendations that addressed threats to construct validity, the most commonly included recommendations called for characterization of the properties of the animal model before experimentation and matching of the animal model to the human manifestation of the disease."
- Key point: The most common internal validity recommendations were power calculations, random allocation, and blinding of outcome assessors; the most common construct validity recommendations were characterizing the animal model before the experiment and matching the model to the human disease.

### E10

- Location: Discussion
- Quote: "Whether failure to implement the procedures described above explains the frequent discordance between preclinical effect sizes and those in clinical trials is unclear."
- Key point: Whether failure to implement these practices explains the frequent discordance between preclinical and clinical effect sizes is unclear.

### E11

- Location: Results, Guideline Characteristics
- Quote: "Most were derived from workshop discussions, and only three described a clear methodology for their development."
- Key point: Most of these guidelines came from workshop discussions; only three described a clear methodology for their development.

### E12

- Location: Discussion
- Quote: "Validity threats can be specific to disease domains, models, or intervention platforms. For instance, confounding of anesthetics with disease response presents a greater validity threat in cardiovascular preclinical studies than in cancer, since anesthetics can interact with cardiovascular function but rarely interfere with tumor growth."
- Key point: Validity threats can differ by disease domain, model, or intervention; for example, anesthetics interact with cardiovascular function but rarely with tumor growth, so confounding by anesthetics is a greater threat in cardiovascular studies.

### E13

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "First, they aim to demonstrate causal relationships between an investigational agent (treatment) and a disease-related phenotype or phenotype proxy (effect) in an animal model." / "For example, biased outcome assessment due to experimenter expectation can lead to spurious inferences about treatment response." / "The second aim of preclinical efficacy studies is to support generalization of treatment–effect relationships to human patients."
- Key point: Preclinical efficacy studies have two aims: first, to demonstrate a causal relationship between an investigational agent (treatment) and a disease-related phenotype (effect) in an animal model, where, for example, biased outcome assessment due to experimenter expectation can lead to spurious inferences; second, to support generalizing this treatment–effect relationship to patients.
