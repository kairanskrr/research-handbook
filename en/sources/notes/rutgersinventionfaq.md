# Rutgers Office for Research: Inventions & Commercialization FAQs

- Source: `rutgersinventionfaq`, [original](https://research.rutgers.edu/faculty-staff/inventions-commercialization/faqs)
- Version: Technology transfer FAQ page of the Office for Research at Rutgers University (US), read 2026-10-06. Locations give question titles. The content reflects US law and the university's own policy.

### E1

- Location: When should I submit an invention disclosure?
- Quote: "If an invention is disclosed to the Office for Research after it has been publicly disseminated in an enabling manner, then patent rights outside of the United States are not available."
- Key point: Once an invention has been publicly disclosed in an enabling way, a later application cannot obtain patent rights outside the United States.

### E2

- Location: When should I submit an invention disclosure?
- Quote: "Furthermore, if a patent application is not filed within one year of a public disclosure, U.S. rights can be lost as well."
- Key point: If no application is filed within one year of public disclosure, US patent rights can be lost too.

### E3

- Location: What if I want to publish my results?
- Quote: "Given sufficient notice, we will work with you to obtain the patent protection you need without delaying publication." / "Please be aware that the Office for Research will never prevent you from publishing or presenting your research."
- Key point: Given enough notice, the technology transfer office will work to file patents without delaying publication, and it promises never to prevent researchers from publishing or presenting.

### E4

- Location: What constitutes a public disclosure?
- Quote: "A public disclosure is a non-confidential communication from the inventor to the public of the existence of an invention. The disclosure must also be “enabling", meaning that it must give enough information to someone "of ordinary skill in the art" to practice the invention without undue experimentation."
- Key point: A "public disclosure" is a non-confidential communication of an invention to the public that is "enabling," i.e., detailed enough for a person of ordinary skill in the field to practice it without undue experimentation.

### E5

- Location: What constitutes a public disclosure? — Oral disclosures
- Quote: "if copies of presentation are distributed at a presentation in which an invention is disclosed, this is a public disclosure. However, even if there are no handouts, an audience member taking notes on the invention would constitute a public disclosure."
- Key point: Handing out copies at a presentation counts as public disclosure; even without handouts, an audience member taking notes can make it one. Conference talks, departmental seminars, and thesis defenses can all be public disclosures.

### E6

- Location: questions for industry partners
- Quote: "In general, universities are not the most ideal places to keep information confidential, as one of the main duties of researchers is to publish."
- Key point: Universities are not ideal places to keep information confidential, since one of researchers' main duties is to publish.
