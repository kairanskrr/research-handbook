# Schulman 2020: An Opinionated Guide to ML Research

- Source: `schulman2020opinionated`, [original](http://joschu.net/blog/opinionated-guide-ml-research.html)
- Version: Author's blog, 2020-01-24 (originally written December 2017). Written for machine learning; we excerpt only the parts that hold across fields.

### E1

- Location: opening Exercise
- Quote: "Think about what makes each one stand out—whether it's a groundbreaking result that changed your perspective on some problem; or an algorithmic idea that's reusable; or a deep insight about some recurring questions."
- Key point: Impressive work comes in roughly three kinds: a groundbreaking result that changes how people see a problem, a reusable method, or a deep insight into recurring questions.

### E2

- Location: Choosing Problems › Aim High, and Climb Incrementally Towards High Goals
- Quote: "Sometimes, people who are both exceptionally smart and hard-working fail to do great research. In my view, the main reason for this failure is that they work on unimportant problems."
- Key point: When people who are both smart and hard-working fail to do great research, the main reason is that they work on unimportant problems.

### E3

- Location: Choosing Problems › Honing Your Taste
- Quote: "Your ability to choose the right problems to work on is even more important than your raw technical skill. This taste in problems is something you’ll develop over time by watching which ideas prosper and which ones are forgotten."
- Key point: The ability to choose the right problems matters more than raw technical skill; this taste develops over time by watching which ideas prosper and which are forgotten.

### E4

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "Seek advice from experienced researchers on what to work on. There’s no shame in working on ideas suggested by other people. Ideas are cheap, and there are lots of them in the air. Your skill comes in when you decide which one to work on, and how well you execute on it."
- Key point: Ask experienced researchers what to work on; there is no shame in working on ideas suggested by others. Ideas are cheap and plentiful; your skill lies in choosing which to pursue and how well you execute.

### E5

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "What causes some ideas to get wide uptake, whereas others are forgotten?"
- Key point: One reflection question for developing taste: why do some ideas get wide uptake while others are forgotten?

### E6

- Location: Choosing Problems › Idea-Driven vs Goal-Driven Research
- Quote: "Idea-driven. Follow some sector of the literature. As you read a paper showing how to do X, you have an idea of how to do X even better. Then you embark on a project to test your idea."
- Key point: Idea-driven research: follow some part of the literature; when you read how to do X, you think of how to do X better, then set out to test it.

### E7

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "One major downside of idea-driven research is that there’s a high risk of getting scooped or duplicating the work of others. Researchers around the world are reading the same literature, which leads them to similar ideas."
- Key point: The main downside of idea-driven research is a high risk of getting scooped: everyone reads the same literature and arrives at similar ideas.

### E8

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "On the other hand, with goal-driven research, your goal will give you a perspective that’s differentiated from the rest of the community. It will lead you to ask questions that no one else is asking, enabling you to make larger leaps of progress."
- Key point: Goal-driven research (first set a concrete goal, then solve the problems on the way to it) gives you a perspective different from the rest of the community and leads you to ask questions no one else is asking.

### E9

- Location: Choosing Problems › Case Study of Goal-Driven Research
- Quote: "This example illustrates how choosing a different problem from the rest of the community can lead you to explore different ideas."
- Key point: The author did not follow the hot directions of the time and stuck with his own problem, which produced his most influential work; choosing a problem different from everyone else's leads you to different ideas.

### E10

- Location: Choosing Problems › Aim High, and Climb Incrementally Towards High Goals
- Quote: "When you embark on a research project, you should ask yourself: how large is the potential upside? Will this be a 10% improvement or a 10X improvement? I often see researchers take on projects that seem sensible but could only possibly yield a small improvement to some metric."
- Key point: Before starting a project, ask yourself how large the potential upside is: a 10% improvement or a 10X improvement? Many projects seem sensible but at best yield a small gain on some metric.

### E11

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "Incremental work (those 10% improvements) are most useful in the context of a larger goal that you are trying to achieve."
- Key point: Incremental improvements are most valuable when they serve a larger goal.

### E12

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "A method that slightly improves on the baseline better be very simple, otherwise no one will bother using it—not even you."
- Key point: A method that only slightly improves on the baseline had better be very simple, or no one will use it, not even you.

### E13

- Location: Making Continual Progress › When to Switch Problems
- Quote: "In my experience, switching problems too frequently (and giving up on promising ideas) is a more common failure mode than not switching enough."
- Key point: In the author's experience, switching problems too often (giving up on promising ideas) is a more common failure than not switching enough.

### E14

- Location: Choosing Problems › Idea-Driven vs Goal-Driven Research
- Quote: "I personally recommend goal-driven research for most people, and I’ve mostly followed this strategy myself."
- Key point: The author recommends goal-driven research for most people.

### E15

- Location: Idea-Driven vs Goal-Driven Research
- Quote: "You can wake up every morning and imagine achieving your goal—what the result would look like and how you would feel. That makes it easier to stick to a long-running research program with ups and downs."
- Key point: A clear goal makes it easier to stick with a long research program through its ups and downs.

### E16

- Location: Keep a Notebook, and Review It
- Quote: "I strongly advise you to keep a notebook, where you record your daily ideas and experiments."
- Key point: The author strongly advises keeping a research notebook that records your ideas and experiments every day.

### E17

- Location: Keep a Notebook, and Review It
- Quote: "Every 1 or 2 weeks, I do a review, where I read all of my daily entries and I condense the information into a summary."
- Key point: Every week or two, read through your daily entries and condense them into a summary, with sections such as experimental findings, insights, code progress, and next steps.

### E18

- Location: Keep a Notebook, and Review It
- Quote: "You also might look back at several months and realize that you’ve been jumping around between ideas too much—that you have a few half-finished projects but you didn’t follow any of these threads long enough to yield a notable result."
- Key point: A notebook helps you notice when you have been jumping between ideas and leaving a pile of half-finished projects.

### E19

- Location: When to Switch Problems
- Quote: "Sometimes the idea has the potential to work, but if you get one detail wrong, you’ll see no signs of life. But other ideas are simply doomed to fail no matter how hard you work on them."
- Key point: The hard part: some ideas fail only because one detail is wrong, while others are doomed no matter what you do, and the two are hard to tell apart.

### E20

- Location: When to Switch Problems
- Quote: "If your idea is quick to try and the potential upside is large, then go ahead and do it. But more commonly, your initial results on the new idea will be disappointing, and it’ll take a more sustained effort to yield significant results."
- Key point: If a new idea is quick to try and the potential upside is large, try it; but more often, initial results on the new idea are also disappointing and need sustained effort too.

### E21

- Location: When to Switch Problems
- Quote: "One strategy, which I haven’t tried personally but makes a lot of sense upon reflection, is to devote some fixed time budget to trying out new ideas that diverge from your main line of work."
- Key point: You can set aside a fixed time budget, such as one day a week, for new ideas that diverge from your main line of work.
