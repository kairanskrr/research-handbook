# Baylor University Institute for Oral History 2012: Introduction to Oral History (online workshop handouts)

- Source: `baylor2012introduction`, [original URL (dead)](https://www.baylor.edu/content/services/document.php/66420.pdf), read from Internet Archive snapshots of 2013 (`https://web.archive.org/web/2013id_/http://www.baylor.edu/content/services/document.php/<number>.pdf`, numbers 66419-66440)
- Version: "Workshop on the Web: Introduction to Oral History" by the Baylor University Institute for Oral History (US), one PDF page per topic, copyright page dated 2012. Read 2026-10-06. Locations give page titles. Note: the handouts date from 2012, so their advice on specific equipment and storage media (such as CDs) is outdated; this handbook cites only principles that do not depend on particular equipment.

### E1

- Location: Discovering oral history: What is it?
- Quote: "Oral history is a sound recording of historical information, obtained through an interview that preserves a person's life history or eyewitness account of a past experience"
- Key point: Oral history is a sound recording of historical information obtained through an interview, preserving a person's life history or eyewitness account of past experience.

### E2

- Location: Discovering oral history: What is it?
- Quote: "Two strengths distinguish oral history interviewing: 1) subjectivity, which allows interviewers to ask not only, What happened?, but also, How did you feel about what happened?, and 2) the partnership of co-creation, which invites narrators to interpret and analyze their personal experiences through their own points of view and in their own words."
- Key point: Oral history interviewing has two distinctive strengths: subjectivity, which lets you ask not only "What happened?" but "How did you feel about it?"; and co-creation, which invites narrators to interpret their experiences in their own perspective and words.

### E3

- Location: Understanding oral history: Why do it?
- Quote: "Eyewitnesses to events contribute various viewpoints and perspectives that fill in the gaps in documented history, sometimes correcting or even contradicting the written record."
- Key point: Eyewitnesses contribute varied viewpoints that fill gaps in documented history, sometimes correcting or even contradicting the written record.

### E4

- Location: Discovering oral history: What is it?
- Quote: "seek out people who may otherwise leave behind little or no material record for future generations and ask questions that may have never before been asked about a topic or event"
- Key point: Oral historians seek out people who may otherwise leave little or no written record and ask questions that may never have been asked.

### E5

- Location: Planning a project: Where to begin?
- Quote: "Make sure that oral history is the best way to gather the information you seek. Are there people you can reach who can and will tell you what you want to know?"
- Key point: First make sure oral history is the best way to get the information: are there people you can reach who can and will tell you what you want to know?

### E6

- Location: Planning a project: Where to begin?
- Quote: "Talk to the archivist of the depository to which you will donate your project. Ask what recording formats the depository accepts, what legal agreements are required, and what accompanying materials may be helpful"
- Key point: Talk to the archivist of the depository that will receive your project: what recording formats it accepts, what legal agreements it requires, and what accompanying materials it needs.

### E7

- Location: Focusing & researching a topic
- Quote: "can be readily researched" / "matches with available narrators" / "can be completed in the allotted time" / "has potential for providing otherwise undocumented information"
- Key point: Criteria for a topic include: it can be readily researched, it matches available narrators, it can be completed in the allotted time, and it may provide otherwise undocumented information.

### E8

- Location: Focusing & researching a topic
- Quote: "To create informed questions that prompt storytelling" / "To clarify names of people and places mentioned in an interview" / "Between interviews (to clarify and verify)" / "After an interview (for validity and accuracy)"
- Key point: Background research helps you ask questions that prompt storytelling and clarify names and places mentioned; research between interviews clarifies and verifies, and research after an interview checks validity and accuracy.

### E9

- Location: Selecting narrators
- Quote: "Aim for a representative sample of people who can share insights from various perspectives."
- Key point: Aim for a representative set of narrators who can share insights from various perspectives.

### E10

- Location: Selecting narrators
- Quote: "Whenever possible, visit with narrators before the actual recording session to assess the extent of their experience with the topic, their recall ability, and their physical stamina." / "You may conclude that several brief interviews will accomplish more than a single long one because they tire easily."
- Key point: Whenever possible, visit narrators before recording to assess their experience with the topic, recall, and stamina; for people who tire easily, several short interviews may accomplish more than one long one.

### E11

- Location: Selecting narrators
- Quote: "The presence of an additional person may discourage probing questions and inhibit candid responses."
- Key point: An additional person in the room may discourage probing questions and inhibit candid answers.

### E12

- Location: Creating an interview outline
- Quote: "Writing out specific questions may hinder candid responses, prevent your following up for details, and impose a rigid agenda on what should be an open, flexible exchange."
- Key point: Writing out questions word for word can hinder candid answers and follow-up, imposing a rigid agenda on what should be an open, flexible exchange; list topics as keywords instead.

### E13

- Location: Composing questions
- Quote: "Avoid loaded and leading questions that reveal your biases and suggest you have already formed an opinion of what the answer might be. Not: Wasn't Sandtown a poor, hard place to grow up in? But: Tell me how you felt about the place where you grew up."
- Key point: Avoid loaded and leading questions. Not "Wasn't Sandtown a poor, hard place to grow up in?" but "Tell me how you felt about the place where you grew up."

### E14

- Location: Composing questions
- Quote: "Ask one question at a time." / "Use the silent question. Keep quiet and wait."
- Key point: Ask one question at a time; use the "silent question": keep quiet and wait.

### E15

- Location: Getting the story
- Quote: "Give the narrator time to answer each question fully and finish her/his train of thought, then just sit quietly for a few moments. Chances are excellent that the narrator will think of something else to add. Silence is an integral, important part of the oral history interview process."
- Key point: Let the narrator finish, then sit quietly for a few moments; they will very likely add something. Silence is an important part of the interview.

### E16

- Location: Getting the story
- Quote: "Keep your feedback quiet, being aware that your sounds can override the narrator's voice during the recording." / "indicating neither agreement nor disagreement"
- Key point: Keep your feedback quiet, since your sounds can override the narrator's voice on the recording, and neutral, indicating neither agreement nor disagreement.

### E17

- Location: Getting the story
- Quote: "Rather than disrupt the narrator's train of thought by asking for spellings of unusual words, jot down a phonetic spelling and a clue to its place in the story, then after the interview ask for the correct spelling."
- Key point: Do not interrupt to ask how to spell unusual words; jot them down phonetically and ask for the spelling after the interview.

### E18

- Location: Getting the story
- Quote: "Give the narrator a chance to think through difficult subjects."
- Key point: Give the narrator time to think through difficult subjects.

### E19

- Location: Getting the story
- Quote: "Challenge accounts that you think may be inaccurate , but do not question the narrator's memory or honesty. If you feel you must, refer to other accounts or interpretations you know, asking the narrator for a response or clarification."
- Key point: You may challenge accounts you think are inaccurate, but do not question the narrator's memory or honesty; you can mention other accounts you know and ask for a response or clarification.

### E20

- Location: Getting the story
- Quote: "information or switching the recorder off and on. Assure the narrator that sensitive information may be restricted."
- Key point: Avoid "off the record" information and switching the recorder off and on; assure the narrator that sensitive information can be restricted.

### E21

- Location: Getting the story
- Quote: "Sixty to ninety minutes is a good average length for an interview. Concentration diminishes if the interview becomes lengthy."
- Key point: Sixty to ninety minutes is a good length for an interview; concentration drops if it runs longer.

### E22

- Location: Getting the story
- Quote: "Promise only what you actually will do, such as returning to visit again or furnishing copies of recordings or transcripts."
- Key point: Promise only what you will actually do, such as visiting again or providing copies of recordings or transcripts.

### E23

- Location: Choosing digital recorders
- Quote: "Choose solid-state recorders that create uncompressed PCM WAV or AIFF audio files of CD quality (16-bit, 44.1kHz sampling rate) or better. Avoid digital voice recorders that create highly compressed audio files in proprietary formats"
- Key point: Choose a recorder that makes uncompressed WAV or AIFF files of CD quality (16-bit, 44.1 kHz) or better; avoid voice recorders that make highly compressed files in proprietary formats.

### E24

- Location: Choosing digital recorders
- Quote: "Compare recordings made with the internal microphone and an external microphone and choose the setup that works best for your recorder in your unique interview setting." / "Before you go, practice. Before you begin, test. When you are done, protect your recording."
- Key point: Compare recordings from the internal and an external microphone and choose what works best in your interview setting; practice before you go, test before you begin, and protect the recording when you are done.

### E25

- Location: Making contact & setting up
- Quote: "Be aware of extraneous noises that will be picked up by the microphone--chiming clocks, humming refrigerators, clattering dishes, et cetera" / "Record both your voices, play back the test recording, and adjust the microphone placement and recorder settings as needed."
- Key point: Watch for extraneous noises the microphone will pick up, such as chiming clocks, humming refrigerators, and clattering dishes; before starting, record both voices, play it back, and adjust microphone placement and settings.

### E26

- Location: Preparing legal documents
- Quote: "Through a contract or deed of gift, narrators agree to donate their interviews to the interviewer, the interviewer's sponsoring organization, or the designated depository." / "Ask your chosen oral history depository what type of donor agreement it prefers."
- Key point: Through a contract or deed of gift, narrators donate their interviews to the interviewer, the sponsoring organization, or the designated depository; ask the depository which agreement it prefers.

### E27

- Location: Preparing legal documents
- Quote: "the narrator must transfer copyright ownership to the individual or organization sponsoring the project. When the interviewer is someone other than the designated copyright holder, the interviewer must also transfer copyright to the sponsor."
- Key point: (US practice) The narrator must transfer copyright to the individual or organization sponsoring the project; if the interviewer is not the designated copyright holder, the interviewer must also transfer copyright to the sponsor.

### E28

- Location: Preparing legal documents
- Quote: "Ideally, a release form should be signed before an interview series begins."
- Key point: Ideally, the release form is signed before an interview series begins.

### E29

- Location: Preparing legal documents
- Quote: "It is very difficult to enforce restrictions that are linked to the duration of a lifetime, so ask the narrator to specify a particular future date to end the restriction."
- Key point: Restrictions tied to someone's lifetime are hard to enforce, so ask the narrator to name a specific date when the restriction ends.

### E30

- Location: Transcribing oral histories
- Quote: "The approximate time required to transcribe one recorded hour is 10-12 hours, depending upon the quality of the sound recording and complexity of the interview."
- Key point: (The handout's 2012 estimate) Transcribing one recorded hour takes about 10-12 hours, depending on recording quality and interview complexity.

### E31

- Location: Transcribing oral histories
- Quote: "a transcript cannot help but distort what is actually on the sound recording--sarcasm may go unnoticed, for example." / "Many oral historians believe that researchers should listen to the recordings themselves, making their own interpretive judgments rather than relying on the transcript."
- Key point: A transcript inevitably distorts the recording (sarcasm may go unnoticed, for example); many oral historians believe researchers should listen to the recordings and make their own judgments rather than rely on the transcript.

### E32

- Location: Transcribing oral histories
- Quote: "change as little as possible. The narrator's word choice, including grammar and speech patterns, should be accurately represented."
- Key point: When transcribing, change as little as possible, accurately keeping the narrator's word choice, grammar, and speech patterns.

### E33

- Location: Transcribing oral histories
- Quote: "Narrators may preview the draft transcript and provide spellings of proper names and clarification of misunderstood information, resulting in a more accurate account."
- Key point: Letting narrators preview the draft transcript to supply spellings of proper names and clarify misheard passages makes the record more accurate.

### E34

- Location: Indexing oral histories
- Quote: "A first step toward making an oral history useful for future editing is to create an index or log of the recording, providing the location of subjects on the tape or digital file by minute and second." / "Index soon after the interview."
- Key point: Index the recording, noting the minute and second where each subject appears; do it soon after the interview.

### E35

- Location: Protecting & preserving recordings
- Quote: "create consistent file and folder names so that they can be easily recognized and retrieved."
- Key point: Use consistent file and folder names so they are easy to recognize and retrieve.

### E36

- Location: Protecting & preserving recordings
- Quote: "A list of correctly spelled names and terms mentioned in the interview" / "An explanation of any significant background sounds, equipment problems, or other issues affecting the recording or interview content" / "Research notes, interview outlines, and correspondence with the narrator"
- Key point: When handing over recordings, include a list of correctly spelled names and terms from the interview, an explanation of significant background sounds, equipment problems, and similar issues, and your research notes, interview outlines, and correspondence with the narrator.

### E37

- Location: Critiquing & citing oral histories
- Quote: "Like any other primary document, an oral history must be explored for validity and accuracy." / "How are the narrator's stories like and different from other versions of the topic? Why?" / "Based on what this interview uncovers, what additional research do I need to do to learn more about the topic or verify these stories?"
- Key point: Like any primary document, an oral history must be checked for validity and accuracy: how do the narrator's stories compare with other versions, and why; and what further research is needed to verify them.

### E38

- Location: Critiquing & citing oral histories
- Quote: "Did I ask open-ended questions? leading questions? good follow-up questions?"
- Key point: After an interview, listen to your recording and ask yourself: did I ask open-ended questions? Leading questions? Good follow-up questions?

### E39

- Location: Critiquing & citing oral histories
- Quote: "Oral histories are usually considered unpublished primary materials." / "provide sufficient information to lead the interested reader to the physical location of the cited source"
- Key point: Oral histories are usually treated as unpublished primary sources; citations should give enough information for readers to find where the original is held.

### E40

- Location: Reaching the public
- Quote: "A signed legal-release form transferring copyright is a must for those considering the creation of public programs. Ethical practice requires you to tell the narrator that the recording might be used in a production or publication."
- Key point: A signed release form transferring copyright is a must for public programs; ethically, you must tell narrators that the recording may be used in a production or publication.

### E41

- Location: Using digital media
- Quote: "Transfer the unedited, original audio recordings to a computer hard drive as soon as possible." / "Remember LOCKSS: Lots of copies keep stuff safe."
- Key point: Transfer the unedited original recordings to a computer hard drive as soon as possible; remember LOCKSS: "Lots of copies keep stuff safe."

### E42

- Location: Establishing ethical relationships
- Quote: "You may be the only person who records your narrators' stories, so take time to include their memories on subjects beyond your own immediate interests."
- Key point: You may be the only person who records this narrator's story, so also record memories beyond your immediate research interests.
