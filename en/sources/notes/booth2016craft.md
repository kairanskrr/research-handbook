# Booth, Colomb & Williams 2016: The Craft of Research (4th edition)

- Source: `booth2016craft`; the book itself is not openly available. Read two university pages that state they are adapted from it: [Brandeis University Writing Program handout Articulating Your Research Topic](https://www.brandeis.edu/writing-program/resources/faculty/handouts/articulating-research-topic.html); [Penn State York library Spark Collection page The Research Process](https://sites.psu.edu/spark/research-process/)
- Version: the Brandeis handout states "Credit: University Writing Center, 2020. Adapted from Wayne C. Booth et al., The Craft of Research, Fourth Edition (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2016), 44-46."; the Penn State page says its template is "adapted from The Craft of Research, 3rd edition". Both are adaptations, not the book's own text. When citing, write "the Brandeis Writing Program, adapting Booth et al." rather than attributing the words to the book. Locations give page headings.

### E1

- Location: Brandeis handout, opening paragraph
- Quote: "The process below can help you become more confident articulating your project by breaking it down into three smaller steps"
- Key point: The handout breaks articulating a research topic into three small steps, so beginners can state their project even before they have fully worked it out.

### E2

- Location: Brandeis handout, Step 1: Name Your Topic
- Quote: "If you are beginning a project with only a topic and maybe the glimmerings of a good question or two, start by naming your project:" / "I am trying to learn about/working on/studying _______________."
- Key point: Step 1, name your topic: fill in the sentence "I am working on / studying ____".

### E3

- Location: Brandeis handout, Step 2: Add an Indirect Question
- Quote: "Now add an indirect question that indicates what you do not know or understand about your topic:" / "because I want to find out who/what/when/where/whether/why/how _______________."
- Key point: Step 2, add an indirect question that says what you do not yet know or understand about the topic: "because I want to find out who/what/when/where/whether/why/how ____".

### E4

- Location: Brandeis handout, Step 3: Answer So What? by Motivating Your Question
- Quote: "This step tells you whether your question might interest not just you but others. To do that, add a second indirect question that explains why you asked your first question." / "in order to help my reader better understand _______________."
- Key point: Step 3 answers "so what?": add a second indirect question explaining why you asked the first, "in order to help my reader better understand ____"; this tests whether the question interests others, not just you.

### E5

- Location: Brandeis handout, example in Step 3
- Quote: "I am working on Lincoln's beliefs about predestination and their influence on his reasoning" / "because I want to find out how his belief in destiny and God's will influenced his understanding of the causes of the Civil War," / "in order to help my reader understand how his religious beliefs may have influenced his military decisions."
- Key point: Example: I am working on Lincoln's beliefs about predestination and their influence on his reasoning, because I want to find out how his belief in destiny shaped his understanding of the causes of the Civil War, in order to help my reader understand how his religious beliefs may have influenced his military decisions.

### E6

- Location: Brandeis handout, after Step 3
- Quote: "Regularly test your progress by asking a roommate, relative or friend to force you to flesh out those three steps. Even if you can't take them all confidently, you'll know where you are and where you still have to go."
- Key point: Regularly ask a roommate, relative or friend to make you spell out all three steps; even if you cannot do them all confidently yet, you will see where you are and what is still missing.

### E7

- Location: Brandeis handout, To summarize
- Quote: "What you are writing about: "I am working on the topic of…"" / "What you don’t know about it: "because I want to find out…"" / "Why you want your reader to know and care about it: "in order to help my reader understand better…""
- Key point: Summary: the three steps state what you are writing about (topic), what you do not know about it (question), and why readers should know and care (significance).

### E8

- Location: Penn State page, 1 Name Your Topic
- Quote: "Try to be specific enough so you can find, read, and synthesize the information in the time you have."
- Key point: Make the topic specific enough that you can find, read and synthesize the material in the time you have.

### E9

- Location: Penn State page, 2 Add an Indirect Question
- Quote: "Find questions that you find interesting. You can do this by finding background information on your topic." / "What is its history? How does it fit within a larger context? How is it categorized? What are points of agreement or disagreement? Where is the research on this topic heading?"
- Key point: To find questions, first look up background on the topic and ask: What is its history? How does it fit a larger context? How is it categorized? Where do people agree or disagree? Where is research on it heading?

### E10

- Location: Penn State page, 3 Motivate Your Question
- Quote: "This part of the planning process argues why a reader should care about your research. And it’s the most challenging step to articulate. You may not have a conclusive answer until you’ve completed a first draft"
- Key point: Explaining why a reader should care is the hardest of the three steps to articulate, and you may not have a firm answer until you finish a first draft.
