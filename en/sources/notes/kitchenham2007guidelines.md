# Kitchenham & Charters 2007: Guidelines for performing Systematic Literature Reviews in Software Engineering

- Source: `kitchenham2007guidelines`, [original PDF](https://legacyfileshare.elsevier.com/promis_misc/525444systematicreviewsguide.pdf)
- Version: Technical report EBSE-2007-01, v2.3. Page numbers are the PDF's page sequence.

### E1

- Location: §1, PDF p. 11
- Quote: "However, unless a literature review is thorough and fair, it is of little scientific value."
- Key point: Unless a literature review is thorough and fair, it has little scientific value. This is why reviews need to be "systematic."

### E2

- Location: §2, PDF p. 11
- Quote: "A systematic literature review (often referred to as a systematic review) is a means of identifying, evaluating and interpreting all available research relevant to a particular research question, or topic area, or phenomenon of interest."
- Key point: A systematic review is defined as identifying, evaluating, and interpreting **all** available research relevant to a particular question.

### E3

- Location: §2.5.1, PDF p. 12
- Quote: "If, during the initial examination of a domain prior to commissioning a systematic review, it is discovered that very little evidence is likely to exist or that the topic is very broad then a systematic mapping study may be a more appropriate exercise than a systematic review. A systematic mapping study allows the evidence in a domain to be plotted at a high level of granularity. This allows for the identification of evidence clusters and evidence deserts to direct the focus of future systematic reviews and to identify areas for more primary studies to be conducted."
- Key point: When little evidence exists or the topic is very broad, a systematic mapping study is more suitable first. It shows where research clusters and where the gaps are, pointing the way for later systematic reviews and new studies.

### E4

- Location: §4, PDF p. 14
- Quote: "This document summarises the stages in a systematic review into three main phases: Planning the Review, Conducting the Review, Reporting the Review."
- Key point: A systematic review has three phases: planning, conducting, and reporting.

### E5

- Location: §5.1, PDF pp. 14–15
- Quote: "Prior to undertaking a systematic review it is necessary to confirm the need for such a review."
- Key point: Before starting, confirm the review is actually needed, e.g., check whether a similar review already exists.

### E6

- Location: §5.3.1, PDF p. 17
- Quote: "The most important activity during planning is to formulate the research question(s)."
- Key point: The most important planning activity is formulating the research question(s).

### E7

- Location: §5.4, PDF p. 20
- Quote: "A pre-defined protocol is necessary to reduce the possibility of researcher bias. For example, without a protocol, it is possible that the selection of individual studies or the analysis may be driven by researcher expectations."
- Key point: A pre-defined protocol reduces the chance of researchers selecting papers and analyses to fit their expectations. It covers background, research questions, search strategy, selection criteria and procedure, quality assessment, data extraction, and synthesis.

### E8

- Location: §5.4, PDF p. 21
- Quote: "It is usually helpful to pilot the selection criteria on a subset of primary studies."
- Key point: Once the selection criteria are set, pilot them on a small subset of papers.

### E9

- Location: §5.5, PDF p. 21
- Quote: "The protocol is a critical element of any systematic review."
- Key point: The protocol is a critical element of a systematic review and should be reviewed before the review is conducted.

### E10

- Location: §6.1.2, PDF p. 23
- Quote: "Publication bias refers to the problem that positive results are more likely to be published than negative results."
- Key point: Publication bias: positive results are more likely to be published than negative ones, so searching only the published literature makes conclusions too optimistic.

### E11

- Location: §6.2.2, PDF p. 27
- Quote: "Initially, selection criteria should be interpreted liberally, so that unless a study identified by the electronic and hand searches can be clearly excluded based on title and abstract, a full copy should be obtained."
- Key point: Selection happens in several rounds. The first round should be liberal: obtain the full text of anything that cannot be clearly excluded on title and abstract. The report also quotes the remark that software engineering abstracts are too poor to rely on alone, so conclusions should be read as well.

### E12

- Location: §6.2.3, PDF p. 28
- Quote: "When two or more researchers assess each paper, agreement between researchers can be measured using the Cohen Kappa statistic. [...] Alternatively, individual researchers can apply a test-retest approach, and re-evaluate a random sample of the primary studies found after initial screening to check the consistency of their inclusion/exclusion decisions."
- Key point: When several researchers screen, measure agreement with Cohen's Kappa; a lone researcher can use test-retest, re-evaluating a random sample of papers after some time to check consistency.

### E13

- Location: §6.3, PDF p. 27
- Quote: "In addition to general inclusion/exclusion criteria, it is considered critical to assess the 'quality' of primary studies"
- Key point: Beyond inclusion/exclusion criteria, assess the quality of each included study, to filter further or to explain why results differ across studies.

### E14

- Location: §6.3.4, PDF p. 37
- Quote: "It is tempting to assume that because something wasn't reported, it wasn't done. This assumption may be incorrect. Researchers should attempt to obtain more information from the authors of the study."
- Key point: Something not being reported does not mean it was not done. Try to get more information from the study's authors.

### E15

- Location: §6.4.3, PDF pp. 40–41
- Quote: "Whenever feasible, data extraction should be performed independently by two or more researchers. [...] For single researchers such as PhD students, other checking techniques must be used. For example supervisors could perform data extraction on a random sample of the primary studies and their results cross-checked with those of the student."
- Key point: Data extraction is best done independently by two or more researchers. A single researcher (such as a PhD student) can ask their advisor to extract data from a random sample of papers and cross-check.

### E16

- Location: §6.5, PDF p. 41
- Quote: "Data synthesis involves collating and summarising the results of the included primary studies. Synthesis can be descriptive (non-quantitative). However, it is sometimes possible to complement a descriptive synthesis with a quantitative summary. Using statistical techniques to obtain a quantitative synthesis is referred to as meta-analysis."
- Key point: Synthesis means collating and summarizing the results of the included studies. It can be descriptive, sometimes complemented by a quantitative summary; quantitative synthesis with statistical methods is called meta-analysis.
