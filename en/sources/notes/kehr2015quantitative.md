# Kehr & Kowatsch 2015: Quantitative Longitudinal Research: A Review of IS Literature, and a Set of Methodological Guidelines

- Source: `kehr2015quantitative`, [original PDF](https://www.alexandria.unisg.ch/entities/publication/ffeb99ad-44cf-4501-be14-2999c9171dae) (final conference version in the University of St. Gallen institutional repository); conference page https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2015_cr/94
- Version: Full paper at ECIS 2015 (the 23rd European Conference on Information Systems), 18 pages. Page numbers are those in the paper's footer. It discusses quantitative longitudinal research with human subjects in information systems.

### E1

- Location: §1 Introduction, p. 1
- Quote: "Also, a doubtless explanation and prediction of IS-related phenomena requires a chronological perspective, given that causes precede effects"
- Key point: Causes precede effects, so explaining and predicting phenomena requires a chronological perspective. This is why longitudinal research can offer clues for causal inference.

### E2

- Location: §1 Introduction, p. 2
- Quote: "our work is organized around a conceptualization of Singer and Willett (2003a), defining longitudinal research as repeated observations of the same units on the same outcomes over a certain amount of time."
- Key point: Longitudinal research is defined as repeated observations of the same units on the same outcomes over a period of time.

### E3

- Location: §1 Introduction, p. 2
- Quote: "authors refer to longitudinal research when retrospectively assessing outcomes through one observation only, i.e. when requesting subjects to remember and report past changes"
- Key point: Some studies observe only once and ask subjects to recall past changes, yet call themselves longitudinal; the authors do not adopt this broad definition.

### E4

- Location: §4.1 Guideline 1, p. 5
- Quote: "Thus, theoretical considerations on the expected fluctuation of the outcome variable must precede any decision on a particular study design, such as (1) the number and space of repeated measurements, or (2) the selection of an adequate time metric."
- Key point: Before deciding on the number and spacing of measurements and the time metric, think through theoretically how the outcome variable is expected to fluctuate.

### E5

- Location: §4.1 Guideline 1, p. 6
- Quote: "metric of time may include any measure that is non-reversible, e.g. age and grades, money spent, or hours of system use"
- Key point: The time metric need not be calendar time; any non-reversible measure (age, grade, money spent, hours of system use) can serve as the time scale.

### E6

- Location: §4.1 Guideline 1, p. 6
- Quote: "If such groundwork is missing, scholars could still consider qualitative prestudies to correctly anticipate oscillation (Ployhart and Ward, 2011), or align wave space and frequency to the fluctuation of the fastest changing variable"
- Key point: Without existing theory to rely on, you can run a qualitative prestudy to anticipate the rhythm of change, or set wave spacing and frequency to match the fastest-changing variable.

### E7

- Location: §4.2 Guideline 2, p. 7
- Quote: "This class of research questions indeed requires at least three measurement occasions to correctly and extensively describe linear growth, and even more waves are required to identify non-linear change over time"
- Key point: Describing the shape of change (e.g., linear growth) requires at least three measurement occasions; identifying non-linear change requires more.

### E8

- Location: §4.2 Guideline 2, p. 7
- Quote: "However, there is a feasible class of research questions aiming to detect whether or why a change exists at all." / "Typically, pretest-posttest designs with two measurement occasions are applied in such a context"
- Key point: Questions that only ask whether or why a change occurs commonly use a pretest-posttest design with two measurement occasions.

### E9

- Location: §4.2 Guideline 2, p. 7
- Quote: "However, these designs have been subject to continuous criticism alluding to reliability and validity artifacts" / "As a silver bullet to these issues, scholars typically apply randomized control group trials, assuming that measurement errors will equally affect all groups, whereas true change will reflect in differences of change between samples with identical pre-conditions"
- Key point: Two-wave designs have long been criticized for failing to separate real change from measurement error; the usual remedy is a randomized control group: measurement error affects all groups equally, so real change shows up as a difference in change between groups.

### E10

- Location: §4.3 Guideline 3, p. 8
- Quote: "However, higher effectiveness may also be confounded by a simple training effect – users accustomed to the existing usability issues and learned to bypass them, resulting in a shorter time needed to fulfill the task."
- Key point: Example: if you repeatedly measure how efficiently the same users complete a task, gains may come from a training effect (users get used to the problems and learn to work around them) rather than from a better product.

### E11

- Location: §4.3 Guideline 3, p. 8
- Quote: "If subjects remember their answers from preceding measurement occasions, they will probably aspire to repeat their responses to keep consistency, despite real changes, resulting in false-negative findings" / "Furthermore, repeated measurements increase the likelihood of subjects correctly guessing the study purpose, leading to experimenter demand effects"
- Key point: Subjects who remember earlier answers may repeat them for consistency, masking real change; repeated measurement also makes it easier for subjects to guess the study's purpose and answer as the researcher expects.

### E12

- Location: §4.3 Guideline 3, p. 9
- Quote: "When studying why a change occurs, we suggest introducing a control group of similar individuals as a silver bullet to these issues."
- Key point: When studying why a change occurs, the authors suggest adding a control group of similar individuals to address these threats to validity.

### E13

- Location: §4.3 Guideline 3, p. 9
- Quote: "In fact, attrition is an expectable and, in most cases, unavoidable issue when dealing with longitudinal data" / "First, sample sizes decrease, diminishing statistical power and the amount of available analysing techniques (ibid.). Second, systematic differences between respondents and non-respondents may threaten internal and external validity"
- Key point: Attrition (subjects dropping out) is almost unavoidable in longitudinal research. It shrinks the sample and lowers statistical power, and those who stay may differ systematically from those who leave, threatening internal and external validity.

### E14

- Location: §4.3 Guideline 3, p. 9
- Quote: "If, for any reason, all subjects with low perceived acceptance had left the study at some point of time, an artificial elevation in average acceptance would have resulted."
- Key point: Example: if everyone with low acceptance dropped out, average acceptance would be artificially inflated.

### E15

- Location: §4.3 Guideline 3, p. 9
- Quote: "Thus, one should anticipate and expect attrition by including more participants than needed (Ployhart and Vandenberg, 2010), by adopting response enhancement techniques like incentives (Anseel et al., 2010) or by reducing the amount and spaces of waves to a reasonable number."
- Key point: To deal with attrition: recruit more participants than needed, raise response rates with incentives and similar techniques, and keep the number and spacing of waves reasonable.

### E16

- Location: §4.3 Guideline 3, p. 9
- Quote: "In prior IS literature investigating change over time, however, most studies did not explicitly account for attrition (20 out of 21), but simply excluded incomplete data sets"
- Key point: Of the 21 longitudinal information systems studies the authors reviewed, 20 did not explicitly handle attrition and simply dropped incomplete data.

### E17

- Location: §4.4 Guideline 4, p. 10
- Quote: "In contrast to cross-sectional data, longitudinal data is interdependent, i.e. data gathered from a successive wave can be, to a certain extent, predicted by data gathered at a preceding wave" / "interdependent measurement values most likely violate the assumption of uncorrelated residuals"
- Key point: Longitudinal data are interdependent: data from a later wave can partly be predicted from an earlier wave, which violates the assumption of uncorrelated residuals behind many common methods (such as analysis of variance).

### E18

- Location: §4.4 Guideline 4, p. 10
- Quote: "Other scholars, in contrast, relied on descriptive statistics (Vitari and Ravarini, 2009), correlation analysis (Kim, 2009a), or structure equation modeling (e.g. Kim et al., 2009; Venkatesh and Sykes, 2013), potentially reporting inflated relationships between analysed variables due to autocorrelation."
- Key point: Analyzing longitudinal data with ordinary methods such as correlation analysis can inflate relationships between variables because of autocorrelation.

### E19

- Location: §4.4 Guideline 4, pp. 10–11
- Quote: "Repeated measures GLM (e.g. repeated measures ANOVA, paired-samples t-test, trend analysis by regression) is applicable for any research question referring to the group level" / "Hierarchical linear modeling (HLM), in contrast, enables researchers to model interunit differences in intraunit changes using a multiple level approach"
- Key point: If you care only about group-level change, use a repeated measures general linear model (e.g., repeated measures ANOVA, paired t-test); to study how individuals' trajectories of change differ, use hierarchical linear modeling (multilevel models).
