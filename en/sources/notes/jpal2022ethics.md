# J-PAL research resource: Ethical conduct of randomized evaluations

- Source: `jpal2022ethics`, [original web page](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/ethical-conduct-randomized-evaluations)
- Version: J-PAL research resource page by Laura Feeney, Sarah Kopper, and Anja Sautmann, updated October 2022; accessed October 2026. The page states that the authors are not ethicists and that it does not aim to be comprehensive. Locations give subheadings.

### E1

- Location: Introduction: Ethical framework
- Quote: "IRB review cannot substitute for the researcher’s responsibility to consider the ethical implications of their research and ask themselves as well as members of the communities they work in if they are comfortable with their research protocols."
- Key point: Institutional review board (IRB) review cannot substitute for researchers' own ethical responsibility; ask yourself, and members of the communities you work in, whether you are comfortable with the research protocol.

### E2

- Location: Introduction: Ethical framework
- Quote: "A feature that is important to the research design, such as randomly drawing payment amounts, may appear unethical or unfair to field staff."
- Key point: A feature important to the research design (such as randomly drawing payment amounts) may look unethical or unfair to field staff.

### E3

- Location: How research ethics contribute to research quality
- Quote: "Carefully consulting and including feedback and consent from local partners, community members, and government in research design and implementation is critical to conducting high-quality, ethical research"
- Key point: Consulting local partners, community members, and government, and including their feedback and consent in design and implementation, is critical to high-quality, ethical research.

### E4

- Location: Beneficence principle: “Do not harm” and “minimize risks, maximize benefits”
- Quote: "For example, a study that is so poorly designed that the results are not credible has no benefit and would thus fail a risk/benefit ratio assessment because it would expose individuals to the burdens of participation without any knowledge gains (Kukla 2007)."
- Key point: A study so poorly designed that its results are not credible has no benefit; it fails the risk/benefit assessment because it imposes the burdens of participation without any knowledge gains.

### E5

- Location: Implementing the “do not harm” principle in practice
- Quote: "Do not deny participants an intervention, treatment, or services they would otherwise be entitled to receive."
- Key point: Do not deny participants an intervention, treatment, or service they would otherwise be entitled to.

### E6

- Location: Implementing the justice principle in practice
- Quote: "Some intervention designs, such as phase-in designs, allow for greater justice in the sense that no one is permanently denied access (Glennerster & Takavarasha 2013)."
- Key point: Designs such as phase-in designs serve justice better, because no one is permanently denied access.

### E7

- Location: Implementing the “minimal risk” principle in practice
- Quote: "Exposure of sensitive or private data is the most common risk in social science studies, and research protocols should carefully guard against this risk."
- Key point: Exposure of sensitive or private data is the most common risk in social science studies.

### E8

- Location: Implementing respect for persons in practice
- Quote: "It should not be exploitatively low but should not be so high as to potentially undermine participants’ abilities to make a rational decision about the risks and benefits of the study"
- Key point: Compensation for participants should not be exploitatively low, nor so high that it undermines their ability to weigh the study's risks and benefits rationally.
