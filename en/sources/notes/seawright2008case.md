# Seawright & Gerring 2008: Case Selection Techniques in Case Study Research

- Source: `seawright2008case`, [PDF posted on co-author Gerring's Boston University homepage (Internet Archive snapshot)](https://web.archive.org/web/2016/http://blogs.bu.edu/jgerring/files/2013/06/CaseSelection.pdf)
- Version: Publisher's typeset PDF of Political Research Quarterly 61(2): 294-308, posted by the author on his homepage; locations give journal page numbers.

### E1

- Location: introduction, p. 294
- Quote: "Case selection is the primordial task of the case study researcher, for in choosing cases, one also sets out an agenda for studying those cases."
- Key point: Case selection is the case study researcher's primordial task: choosing cases also sets the agenda for studying them.

### E2

- Location: introduction, p. 294
- Quote: "In case studies of this sort, the chosen case is asked to perform a heroic role: to stand for (represent) a population of cases that is often much larger than the case itself."
- Key point: The chosen case is asked to play a "heroic" role: to represent a population of cases that is often much larger.

### E3

- Location: introduction, p. 295
- Quote: "scholars continue to lean primarily on pragmatic considerations such as time, money, expertise, and access." / "Of course, these are perfectly legitimate factors in case selection. Yet they do not provide a methodological justification for why case A might be preferred over case B."
- Key point: Scholars mostly choose cases on pragmatic grounds such as time, money, expertise, and access; these are perfectly legitimate, but they are not a methodological reason for choosing A over B.

### E4

- Location: introduction, p. 295
- Quote: "Thus, even if cases are initially chosen for pragmatic reasons, it is essential that researchers understand retroactively how the properties of the selected cases comport with the rest of the population."
- Key point: Even if cases were first chosen for pragmatic reasons, researchers must afterward understand where they stand relative to the rest of the population.

### E5

- Location: Why Not Choose Cases Randomly?, p. 295
- Quote: "This result shows that for a comparative case study composed of five cases (or less), randomized case selection procedures will often produce a sample that is substantially unrepresentative of the population."
- Key point: The authors' simulations show that for a comparative case study of five cases or fewer, random selection often yields a sample substantially unrepresentative of the population.

### E6

- Location: Techniques of Case Selection, p. 296
- Quote: "one desires (1) a representative sample and (2) useful variation on the dimensions of theoretical interest."
- Key point: Case selection has two goals: a representative sample, and useful variation on the dimensions of theoretical interest.

### E7

- Location: Techniques of Case Selection, p. 296
- Quote: "the seven case study types presented in Table 1: typical, diverse, extreme, deviant, influential, most similar, and most different."
- Key point: Seven case selection types: typical, diverse, extreme, deviant, influential, most similar, and most different.

### E8

- Location: Typical Case, p. 299
- Quote: "the researcher wants to find a typical case of some phenomenon so that he or she can better explore the causal mechanisms at work in a general, cross-case relationship."
- Key point: You choose a typical case to explore the causal mechanisms behind a general cross-case relationship in depth.

### E9

- Location: Extreme Case, p. 301
- Quote: "Selecting cases on the dependent variable is indeed problematic if the researcher treats the resulting sample—the extreme case—as if it were representative of a population."
- Key point: Selecting cases on the dependent variable (the outcome) is indeed problematic if the selected extreme cases are treated as representative of the population.

### E10

- Location: Extreme Case, p. 301
- Quote: "Note that the extreme case method refers back to a larger sample of cases lying in the background of the analysis. These cases provide a full range of variation as well as a more representative picture of the population."
- Key point: The extreme case method refers back to a larger set of cases in the background of the analysis, which provide the full range of variation and a more representative picture of the population.

### E11

- Location: Deviant Case, p. 302
- Quote: "The deviant case method selects cases that, by reference to some general cross-case relationship, demonstrate a surprising value; they are poorly explained."
- Key point: A deviant case shows a surprising value relative to some general cross-case relationship; it is poorly explained.

### E12

- Location: Deviant Case, p. 302
- Quote: "The purpose of a deviant case analysis is usually to probe for new—but as yet unspecified—explanations."
- Key point: Deviant case analysis usually aims to probe for new, as yet unspecified explanations.

### E13

- Location: Most Similar, p. 304
- Quote: "In its purest form, the chosen pair of cases is similar on all the measured independent variables, except the independent variable of interest."
- Key point: The ideal most-similar design: two cases alike on all measured independent variables except the one of interest.

### E14

- Location: Most Different, p. 306
- Quote: "the researcher tries to identify cases where just one independent variable as well as the dependent variable covary, and all other plausible independent variables show different values." / "Analysts have usually taken the position that this research design is a weaker tool for causal inference than the most similar method"
- Key point: Most-different design: only one independent variable covaries with the outcome, while all other plausible independent variables differ; analysts usually consider it weaker than the most-similar design.

### E15

- Location: Complications, p. 306
- Quote: "Often, a researcher begins in an exploratory mode and proceeds to a confirmatory mode—that is, she develops a specific X/Y hypothesis." / "Unfortunately, research strategies that are ideal for exploration are not always ideal for confirmation."
- Key point: Research often moves from exploration to confirmation, but designs ideal for exploration are not always suited to confirmation.

### E16

- Location: Complications, p. 306
- Quote: "One can explain, straightforwardly, that the initial research was undertaken in an exploratory fashion and therefore was not constructed to test the specific hypothesis that is—now—the primary argument."
- Key point: One remedy: state plainly that the initial research was exploratory and was not designed to test what is now the main argument.
