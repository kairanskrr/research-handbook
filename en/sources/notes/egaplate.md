# EGAP Methods Guide: 10 Things You Need to Know About the Local Average Treatment Effect

- Source: `egaplate`, [original web page](https://methods.egap.org/guides/research-questions/late_en.html)
- Version: Evidence in Governance and Politics (EGAP) methods guide by Peter van der Windt; accessed October 2026. Locations give the section number.

### E1

- Location: §1 What the LATE is
- Quote: "In some cases it is possible to estimate the “Local Average Treatment Effect” (LATE), also known as the “Complier Average Causal Effect” (CACE). The LATE is the average treatment effect for the Compliers."
- Key point: The local average treatment effect (LATE), also called the complier average causal effect (CACE), is the average treatment effect among compliers (people whose treatment status follows their assignment).

### E2

- Location: §2 With one-sided noncompliance you need to satisfy an exclusion restriction to estimate the LATE
- Quote: "The exclusion restriction (also called “excludability”) stipulates that outcomes respond to treatments, not treatment assignments."
- Key point: The exclusion restriction: outcomes respond to the treatment actually received, not to the treatment assignment itself.

### E3

- Location: §6 The LATE only reflects treatment effects among compliers
- Quote: "While a LATE estimate is better than nothing, it provides a consistent estimate of the average treatment effect only for a subgroup of the population: the Compliers."
- Key point: The local average treatment effect represents only the subgroup of compliers, not the whole sample.

### E4

- Location: §5 How to conduct statistical inference about the LATE
- Quote: "Because the LATE equals the ratio of the ITT and the share of Compliers, the LATE will be equal to zero whenever the ITT equals zero."
- Key point: The local average treatment effect equals the intention-to-treat (ITT) effect divided by the share of compliers; if the ITT effect is zero, so is the LATE.
