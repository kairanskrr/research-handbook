# Jacobs, Büthe et al. 2021: The Qualitative Transparency Deliberations: Insights and Implications

- Source: `jacobs2021qualitative`, [author accepted manuscript (White Rose repository)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/158221/)
- Version: Author accepted manuscript (the February 2020 SSRN version of the overview article), held in the White Rose open repository of the University of Sheffield and partner universities and marked as free for personal research and study; the published version is Perspectives on Politics 19(1): 171–208, 2021. The Qualitative Transparency Deliberations (QTD) were launched by the Qualitative and Multi-Method Research section of the American Political Science Association. Locations give the manuscript's subheadings and page numbers.

### E7

- Location: Opening, p. 7 (main findings)
- Quote: "sharing some source materials is seen as an intellectually valuable practice within many qualitative research traditions; however, uniform and maximalist data-sharing requirements would be highly problematic for ethical, practical, and epistemological reasons;"
- Key point: Many qualitative traditions see sharing some source materials as valuable, but uniform, maximalist data-sharing requirements would be highly problematic for ethical, practical, and epistemological reasons.

### E8

- Location: Opening, p. 7 (main findings)
- Quote: "researchers' ethical obligations to protect human participants and their communities ought to take priority over the sharing of information with research consumers."
- Key point: Researchers' ethical obligations to protect human participants and their communities should take priority over sharing information with readers.

### E9

- Location: Opening, p. 7 (broad consensus)
- Quote: "the process through which the evidence used in a study was generated; [...] the analytic process through which the scholar arrived at conclusions; and [...] the risks faced by human participants in a study and the steps taken to protect them and their communities."
- Key point: Qualitative research communities broadly agree that publications should explain three things in detail: how the evidence was generated; the analytic process that led to the conclusions; and the risks participants faced and the steps taken to protect them.

### E10

- Location: Costs and Risks, p. 17
- Quote: "In communities under close government surveillance, phrases used, events referenced, or even the date and time of an interview may be sufficient to reveal interlocutors' identities."
- Key point: In communities under close government surveillance, phrases used, events referenced, or even the date and time of an interview can be enough to reveal interviewees' identities.

### E11

- Location: Costs and Risks, p. 18
- Quote: "What may seem like a low-risk disclosure today might become high risk in the future, as political and social conditions change."
- Key point: A disclosure that looks low-risk today may become high-risk as political and social conditions change.

### E12

- Location: Costs and Risks, p. 19
- Quote: "those who do take part are less likely to provide candid responses if they know that full transcripts will be made publicly available."
- Key point: Interviewees are less likely to answer candidly if they know full transcripts will be made public.

### E13

- Location: Costs and Risks, p. 19
- Quote: "data-sharing is not a binary, "all or nothing" choice. Scholars might be able to mitigate many of the associated costs, for instance, by providing access to a select set of documents or transcript passages that are especially informative about an empirical claim."
- Key point: Data sharing is not an "all or nothing" choice; you can share just the few documents or transcript passages most informative about a given empirical claim.

### E14

- Location: Implications for Research Practice, p. 24
- Quote: "there appears to be a fundamental divide between qualitative researchers who see value in at least some logics and practices of transparency, on the one hand, and qualitative researchers who reject the very concept of transparency as incompatible with their understanding of knowledge production, on the other hand."
- Key point: There is a fundamental divide among qualitative researchers: some see value in at least some transparency practices, while others (mainly interpretivists) reject the very concept of transparency as incompatible with their understanding of knowledge production.

### E15

- Location: Implications for Research Practice, p. 24
- Quote: "there was universal agreement that the sharing of qualitative data should not be uniformly required, given the considerable costs and risks of data-sharing for some forms of research."
- Key point: All working groups agreed that sharing qualitative data should not be uniformly required.

### E16

- Location: Implications for Research Practice, p. 25
- Quote: "scholars should routinely provide sufficient information about the location of publicly available sources to ensure that others can find them, and should specify the particular parts of any source that are being drawn upon (e.g., by including page numbers)."
- Key point: When citing publicly available sources, give enough location information for others to find them, and specify the parts you used (e.g., page numbers).

### E17

- Location: Implications for Research Practice, p. 25
- Quote: "when quoting from a response to an interview question, sharing the complete response in order to provide wider context" / "where transcripts cannot be shared, reporting the number of interviews consistent and inconsistent with a proposed hypothesis;"
- Key point: When quoting an interview answer, share the complete response for context; where transcripts cannot be shared, report how many interviews are consistent and how many inconsistent with a proposed hypothesis.

### E18

- Location: Implications for Research Practice, p. 25
- Quote: "the use of Annotation for Transparent Inquiry, a technology developed by the Qualitative Data Repository at Syracuse University, that allows researchers to layer a citation, analytical note, source excerpt, and possible link to a source over the relevant passage in the article text."
- Key point: You can use Annotation for Transparent Inquiry, a technology developed by the Qualitative Data Repository at Syracuse University, to layer a citation, analytical note, source excerpt, and link to the source over the relevant passage of the article.

### E19

- Location: Implications for Research Practice, p. 25
- Quote: "there is a clear consensus on the vital importance of providing readers with detailed accounts of how the evidence used in a study was generated."
- Key point: Qualitative traditions clearly agree that readers need detailed accounts of how a study's evidence was generated.

### E20

- Location: Implications for Research Practice, p. 26
- Quote: "providing information not just about the production of evidence that they themselves generated but also about the origins of sources that pre-date the study, such as textual materials, making explicit the scholar's critical use of his/her sources;"
- Key point: Explain not only the evidence you generated yourself but also the origins of sources that pre-date the study (such as textual materials), making explicit how you used those sources critically.

### E21

- Location: Implications for Research Practice, p. 26
- Quote: "providing an interview table containing key metadata for all interviews conducted;"
- Key point: Provide an interview table with key metadata for all interviews conducted.

### E22

- Location: Implications for Research Practice, p. 26
- Quote: "specifying, for small-n analysis, what was known about the cases at the time of their selection and/or identify those cases that were almost chosen for analysis but ultimately not included;"
- Key point: For small-n analysis, state what was known about the cases when they were selected, and/or identify cases that were almost chosen but ultimately left out.

### E23

- Location: Implications for Research Practice, p. 27
- Quote: "noting the steps taken to challenge one's own premises or early hunches in the course of a project;" / "reporting when initial hypotheses were dropped or modified — or when new hypotheses were developed — in light of the evidence;" / "explicitness about whether an analysis aims for generalization beyond the cases being examined;"
- Key point: Analytic transparency includes noting the steps you took to challenge your own premises and early hunches, reporting which hypotheses were dropped, modified, or newly developed in light of the evidence, and being explicit about whether the analysis is meant to generalize beyond the cases studied.

### E24

- Location: Potential Benefits of Transparency, p. 15
- Quote: "formalizing certain aspects of qualitative analysis — such as background beliefs and the probative value of evidence — can make it even clearer how conclusions have been derived from an array of observations and make it easier for readers to evaluate empirical findings."
- Key point: The process tracing working group notes that formalizing parts of the analysis, such as background beliefs and the probative value of evidence, can make it clearer how conclusions were derived and easier for readers to evaluate them.

### E25

- Location: Costs and Risks, p. 20
- Quote: "Excessively long and complex transparency appendices, for instance, may obscure the most important features for readers to attend to. Similarly, methods featuring extremely high levels of analytic explicitness — such as formal, Bayesian process tracing — may generate less readable and comprehensible text than do more informal, narrative approaches."
- Key point: Overly long and complex transparency appendices can obscure what matters most; and highly explicit methods such as formal Bayesian process tracing may be harder to read than a narrative account.
