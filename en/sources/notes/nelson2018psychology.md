# Nelson, Simmons & Simonsohn 2018: Psychology's Renaissance

- Source: `nelson2018psychology`, [original PDF (author's site)](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Nelson-Simmons-Simonsohn-2018.pdf)
- Version: Annual Review of Psychology 69:511–534. Page numbers are journal page numbers.

### E1

- Location: Abstract, p. 511
- Quote: "In 2010–2012, a few largely coincidental events led experimental psychologists to realize that their approach to collecting, analyzing, and reporting data made it too easy to publish false-positive findings."
- Key point: Events in 2010–2012 made experimental psychologists realize that their way of collecting, analyzing, and reporting data made it too easy to publish false-positive findings.

### E2

- Location: Introduction, p. 512
- Quote: "Authors voluntarily posting their data. Top journals routinely publishing replication attempts, both failures and successes. Hundreds of researchers preregistering their studies. [...] Enormous increases in sample sizes. Some top journals requiring the full disclosure of measures, conditions, exclusions, and the rules for determining sample sizes."
- Key point: The authors list the changes since then: authors voluntarily posting their data; top journals routinely publishing replication attempts, both failures and successes; hundreds of researchers preregistering; much larger sample sizes; and some top journals requiring full disclosure of measures, conditions, data exclusions, and how sample size was determined.

### E3

- Location: The Renaissance Begins: 2010–2012, pp. 512–513
- Quote: "Social psychology's most prestigious journal, the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, published an article by Daryl Bem (2011) in which he presented nine experiments supporting a transparently outlandish claim—that people can be influenced by an unforeseeable future event."
- Key point: One triggering event: social psychology's most prestigious journal published Bem's nine experiments supporting the evidently absurd conclusion that people are influenced by future events they cannot foresee.

### E4

- Location: The Renaissance Begins, p. 513
- Quote: "an article reporting the surprisingly severe consequences of selectively reporting data and analyses, a practice that we later called p-hacking. In that article, we showed that conducting multiple analyses on the same data set and then reporting only the one(s) that obtained statistical significance (e.g., analyzing multiple measures but reporting only one) can dramatically increase the likelihood of publishing a false-positive finding."
- Key point: Definition of p-hacking: running many analyses on the same data and reporting only the significant one (e.g., measuring several outcomes and reporting one). This greatly raises the chance of publishing false-positive results.

### E5

- Location: The Renaissance Begins, p. 513
- Quote: "about 65% of respondents indicated that they had dropped a dependent variable when reporting a study."
- Key point: In the 2012 survey by John et al., about 65% of responding psychologists admitted dropping a dependent variable (outcome measure) when reporting a study.

### E6

- Location: P-Hacking Explains an Old Paradox, p. 514
- Quote: "They would instead explore the data further, examining, for example, whether outliers were interfering with the effect, whether the effect was significant within a subset of participants or trials, or whether it emerged when the dependent variable was coded differently."
- Key point: When the first analysis is not significant, researchers usually do not give up but keep exploring: removing outliers, looking at a subset of participants, computing the dependent variable another way. These are common forms of p-hacking.

### E7

- Location: P-Hacking Explains an Old Paradox, p. 516
- Quote: "we showed how acceptable levels of p-hacking could raise false-positive rates to unacceptable levels (e.g., from 5% to 61%; see Figure 1)."
- Key point: In simulations, combining several kinds of flexibility then considered "acceptable" raised the false-positive rate from 5% to 61%.

### E8

- Location: False Positives Are Bad, p. 518
- Quote: "P-hacking is a pervasive problem precisely because researchers usually do not realize that they are doing it or appreciate that what they are doing is consequential"
- Key point: P-hacking is pervasive precisely because researchers usually do not realize they are doing it or do not see that it matters. It requires no ill intent.

### E9

- Location: P-Hacking Explains an Old Paradox, p. 514
- Quote: "On the other hand, the overwhelming majority of published studies are underpowered and, thus, theoretically unlikely to obtain results that are statistically significant"
- Key point: Published results are almost all significant, yet most studies are underpowered and theoretically should not be significant so often. The authors argue that p-hacking explains this contradiction.

### E10

- Location: Preventing P-Hacking: Disclosure, p. 518
- Quote: "At the bare minimum, this means requiring authors to disclose all of their measures, manipulations, and exclusions, as well as how they determined their sample sizes"
- Key point: The minimum safeguard is disclosure: report all measures, all manipulations, all data exclusions, and how sample size was determined.

### E11

- Location: p. 519, footnote 11
- Quote: "We report how we determined our sample size, all data exclusions (if any), all manipulations, and all measures in the study"
- Key point: The standard disclosure statement the authors suggest putting in papers (often called the "21-word statement").

### E12

- Location: Preventing P-Hacking: Preregistration, p. 519
- Quote: "Preregistrations are time-stamped plans for data analysis written before any data are analyzed."
- Key point: A preregistration is a time-stamped plan for data analysis written before any data are analyzed.

### E13

- Location: Replications, p. 523
- Quote: "Again, 36% were conclusive successes; however, among the remaining 64%, only 25% were conclusive failures, and 49% were inconclusive, i.e., neither failures nor successes" / "This high share of inconclusive results reflects the fact that many of the 100 replications were underpowered"
- Key point: Reassessing the Open Science Collaboration's 100 replications with a method that can classify results as inconclusive: 36% were conclusive successes; of the remaining 64%, only 25% were conclusive failures and 49% were inconclusive. Many were inconclusive because the replications themselves had samples that were too small.

### E14

- Location: P-Hacking Explains an Old Paradox, p. 516
- Quote: "It is tempting to take comfort in the fact that psychology publications usually contain more than one experiment" / "If a researcher is willing to file-drawer even a small number of studies, a false-positive four-study article becomes easy enough to produce"
- Key point: Psychology papers usually contain more than one experiment, but multiple experiments do not automatically make a conclusion reliable: if a researcher is willing to file-drawer a few unsuccessful studies, producing a false-positive paper with four "successful" experiments is not hard.

### E15

- Location: The Renaissance Begins, p. 514
- Quote: "In 2011, psychologist Brian Nosek began organizing several collaborative replication efforts, in which multiple independent labs attempted to replicate previously published research" / "He and Jeffrey Spies also worked to develop an online platform, the Open Science Framework (OSF), which was originally released in 2012; it allowed researchers to more transparently record, share, and report their work."
- Key point: From 2011, Nosek organized several collaborative replication projects in which multiple labs tried to replicate published studies; with Spies he also developed the Open Science Framework (OSF), an online platform launched in 2012 that lets researchers document, share, and report their research more transparently.

### E16

- Location: Innocent Errors: Unintentional Errors, p. 525
- Quote: "They found that 54% of articles contained at least one error, and 12% had at least one error that altered the statistical significance of a reported result."
- Key point: Bakker & Wicherts checked the statistical results reported in psychology journals: 54% of articles contained at least one error, and 12% had at least one error that changed whether a result was statistically significant.
