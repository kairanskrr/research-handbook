# Kitzinger 1995: Qualitative research: Introducing focus groups

- Source: `kitzinger1995qualitative`, [original (PubMed Central, scanned PDF)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2550365/)
- Version: BMJ 1995;311(7000):299–302, the fifth of a seven-part series on qualitative research; medicine and sociology. PubMed Central holds scanned pages, so the quotes below come from the scanned PDF's text layer; text recognition ran some words together (such as "ofthe"), and the spaces were restored when quoting; everything else is verbatim. Locations give journal page numbers.

### E1

- Location: Rationale and uses of focus groups, p. 299
- Quote: "Focus groups are a form of group interview that capitalises on communication between research participants in order to generate data. Although group interviews are often used simply as a quick and convenient way to collect data from several people simultaneously, focus groups explicitly use group interaction as part of the method."
- Key point: A focus group is a group interview that uses communication between participants to generate data; unlike a group interview that simply questions several people at once, it explicitly makes group interaction part of the method.

### E2

- Location: Rationale and uses of focus groups, p. 299
- Quote: "The method is particularly useful for exploring people's knowledge and experiences and can be used to examine not only what people think but how they think and why they think that way."
- Key point: Focus groups are especially good for exploring people's knowledge and experiences, showing not only what people think but how and why they think that way.

### E3

- Location: box Some potential sampling advantages with focus groups, p. 299
- Quote: "Do not discriminate against people who cannot read or write" / "Can encourage participation from those who are reluctant to be interviewed on their own"
- Key point: Focus groups do not exclude people who cannot read or write, and can draw in people reluctant to be interviewed alone.

### E4

- Location: Rationale and uses of focus groups, p. 300
- Quote: "The downside of such group dynamics is that the articulation of group norms may silence individual voices of dissent. The presence of other research participants also compromises the confidentiality of the research session."
- Key point: The downside of group dynamics is that group norms can silence individual dissent, and the presence of other participants weakens confidentiality.

### E5

- Location: Rationale and uses of focus groups, p. 300
- Quote: "Group work can actively facilitate the discussion of taboo topics because the less inhibited members of the group break the ice for shyer participants."
- Key point: Groups can also help with taboo topics: less inhibited members speak first and break the ice for shyer ones.

### E6

- Location: Sampling and group composition, p. 300
- Quote: "Focus group studies can consist of anything between half a dozen to over fifty groups, depending on the aims of the project and the resources available. Most studies involve just a few groups, and some combine this method with other data collection techniques."
- Key point: A study may run anywhere from about six to over fifty groups, depending on its aims and resources; most run just a few, and some combine focus groups with other data collection methods.

### E7

- Location: Sampling and group composition, p. 300
- Quote: "Focus group discussion of a questionnaire is ideal for testing the phrasing of questions and is also useful in explaining or exploring survey results."
- Key point: Having a focus group discuss a questionnaire is ideal for testing question wording, and also helps explain or explore survey results.

### E8

- Location: Sampling and group composition, p. 300
- Quote: "Most researchers recommend aiming for homogeneity within each group in order to capitalise on people's shared experiences. However, it can also be advantageous to bring together a diverse group (for example, from a range of professions) to maximise exploration of different perspectives within a group setting. However, it is important to be aware of how hierarchy within the group may affect the data"
- Key point: Most researchers recommend homogeneous groups to draw on shared experience, but mixing backgrounds on purpose can widen the range of perspectives; watch how hierarchy within the group affects the data.

### E9

- Location: Sampling and group composition, p. 300
- Quote: "The groups can be "naturally occurring" (for example, people who work together) or may be drawn together specifically for the research. Using preexisting groups allows observation of fragments of interactions that approximate to naturally occurring data"
- Key point: Groups can be naturally occurring (such as colleagues) or convened for the research; preexisting groups let you observe fragments of interaction close to natural data.

### E10

- Location: Running the groups, p. 301
- Quote: "The ideal group size is between four and eight people. Sessions may last one to two hours (or extend into a whole afternoon or a series of meetings)."
- Key point: Kitzinger puts the ideal group size at four to eight people, with sessions of one to two hours.

### E11

- Location: Running the groups, p. 301
- Quote: "The facilitator should explain that the aim of focus groups is to encourage people to talk to each other rather than to address themselves to the researcher." / "Later on in the session, however, the researcher can adopt a more interventionist style: urging debate to continue beyond the stage it might otherwise have ended and encouraging the group to discuss the inconsistencies both between participants and within their own thinking."
- Key point: The facilitator should explain that the aim is for people to talk to each other rather than to the researcher; later in the session the researcher can intervene more, pushing debate further and asking the group to discuss inconsistencies between participants and within their own thinking.

### E12

- Location: Running the groups, p. 301
- Quote: "Differences between individual one off interviews have to be analysed by the researchers through armchair theorising; differences between members of focus groups should be explored in situ with the help of the research participants."
- Key point: Differences between one-off individual interviews can only be theorized by the researcher afterward; differences between focus group members can be explored on the spot with the participants' help.

### E13

- Location: Running the groups, p. 301
- Quote: "A common exercise consists of presenting the group with a series of statements on large cards." / "The final layout of the cards is less important than the discussion that it generates."
- Key point: A common exercise has the group sort and rank statements written on cards; what matters is the discussion the sorting generates, not the final arrangement.

### E14

- Location: Running the groups, p. 301
- Quote: "Finally, it may be beneficial to present research participants with a brief questionnaire, or the opportunity to speak to the researcher privately, giving each one the opportunity to record private comments after the group session has been completed."
- Key point: After the session, a short questionnaire or a chance to talk privately lets each person record comments they would not make in the group.

### E15

- Location: Running the groups, p. 301
- Quote: "Ideally the group discussions should be tape recorded and transcribed. If this is not possible then it is vital to take careful notes"
- Key point: Group discussions should ideally be recorded and transcribed; if that is not possible, careful notes are essential.

### E16

- Location: Analysis and writing up, p. 301
- Quote: "In general, it is not appropriate to give percentages in reports of focus group data, and it is important to try to distinguish between individual opinions expressed in spite of the group from the actual group consensus. As in all qualitative analysis, deviant case analysis is important"
- Key point: Focus group reports should generally not give percentages; distinguish individual opinions voiced against the group from real group consensus, and, as in all qualitative analysis, analyze deviant cases.

### E17

- Location: Analysis and writing up, pp. 301–302
- Quote: "The only distinct feature of working with focus group data is the need to indicate the impact of the group dynamic and analyse the sessions in ways that take full advantage of the interaction between research participants. In coding the script of a group discussion, it is worth using special categories for certain types of narrative, such as jokes and anecdotes, and types of interaction, such as "questions," "deferring to the opinion of others," "censorship," or "changes of mind.""
- Key point: The one distinctive feature of analyzing focus group data is showing the impact of group dynamics and making full use of interaction between participants; when coding, it helps to have special codes for kinds of narrative such as jokes and anecdotes, and kinds of interaction such as questions, deferring to others, censorship and changes of mind.

### E18

- Location: Analysis and writing up, p. 302
- Quote: "A focus group research report that is true to its data should also usually include at least some illustrations of the talk between participants, rather than simply presenting isolated quotations taken out of context."
- Key point: A focus group report true to its data should show at least some of the talk between participants, not just isolated quotations taken out of context.

### E19

- Location: Conclusion, p. 302
- Quote: "Interviews may be more appropriate for tapping into individual biographies, but focus groups are more suitable for examining how knowledge, and more importantly, ideas, develop and operate within a given cultural context. Questionnaires are more appropriate for obtaining quantitative information and explaining how many people hold a certain (pre-defined) opinion; focus groups are better for exploring exactly how those opinions are constructed."
- Key point: Interviews suit individual life histories; focus groups suit examining how knowledge and ideas develop and work in a cultural context; questionnaires answer how many people hold an opinion, while focus groups explore how those opinions are formed.
