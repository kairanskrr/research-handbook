# Klein and Roodman 2005: Blind Analysis in Nuclear and Particle Physics

- Source: `klein2005blind`, [original](https://inspirehep.net/files/123bf8d8ec8f80e60be89076c65dbdbd)
- Version: Preprint submitted to the Annual Review of Nuclear and Particle Science, SLAC-PUB-12051 (a Stanford Linear Accelerator Center report funded by the US Department of Energy), freely available on INSPIRE-HEP. Published version: Annu. Rev. Nucl. Part. Sci. 55 (2005) 141–163. Locations give preprint section numbers.

### E1

- Location: §2 Experimenter's Bias and the Motivations for Blind Analyses
- Quote: "The crucial difference between experimenter's bias and any other bias in a measurement is that the size of an experimenter's bias cannot be estimated. Thus the only available approach is to use a methodology that prevents or suppresses it."
- Key point: The crucial difference between experimenter's bias and other biases is that its size cannot be estimated, so the only option is a methodology that prevents or suppresses it.

### E2

- Location: §2
- Quote: "If the decision to stop analyzing and publish relies on the value of the result--in particular how close it adheres to the experimentalist's preconceptions-- the result may be biased toward the preconceived value." / "It is also probably the most common kind of bias found in nuclear or particle physics."
- Key point: If the decision to stop analyzing and publish depends on how close the result is to the expected value, the result may be biased toward that value ("stopping bias"). The authors consider this probably the most common kind of bias in nuclear and particle physics.

### E3

- Location: §1 Introduction (speed of light example)
- Quote: "One striking feature is the 17 km/sec shift between the series of experiments from 1930-1940 and later determinations." / "the investigator searches for the source or sources of such errors, and continues to search until he gets a result close to the accepted value. Then he stops!"
- Key point: A series of speed-of-light measurements from 1930 to 1940 differed by 17 km/s from later determinations. One retrospective analysis suggested that investigators kept searching for sources of error until they got a result close to the accepted value.

### E4

- Location: §1
- Quote: "Although the effect is not striking, the measurements do tend to cluster nearer the prior published averages than the `final' value." / "that would not necessarily mean that the experimental results were biased"
- Key point: The authors examined historical measurements of four quantities and found that values tend to cluster near the previously published average rather than the later "final" value; they note this does not necessarily mean experimenter's bias and could have other causes.

### E5

- Location: §2
- Quote: "While blind analysis techniques may not be feasible or necessary in all measurements, as a general rule the possibility of experimenter's bias should be considered in all experiments."
- Key point: Blind analysis may not be feasible or necessary in every measurement, but every experiment should consider the possibility of experimenter's bias.

### E6

- Location: §2
- Quote: "In general, it is crucial that the blind analysis technique be designed as simply and narrowly as possible."
- Key point: A blind analysis scheme should be designed as simply and narrowly as possible.

### E7

- Location: §2
- Quote: "It is also important to realize that blind analyses solve one and only one problem, the influence of experimenter's bias on the measurement." / "either a correction based on a measurement or the inclusion of the bias as a systematic uncertainty still needs to be made, whether the analysis has been done blindly or not."
- Key point: Blind analysis solves one problem only, experimenter's bias; other biases from methods and instruments must still be corrected or included as systematic uncertainties, blind or not.

### E8

- Location: §2
- Quote: "One useful principle that may be adopted is that the publication simply describe the full analysis procedure, in this case to be explicit about which selections were applied after the unblinding."
- Key point: If selections are added after unblinding, one useful principle is to describe the full analysis procedure in the paper and state explicitly which selections were applied after unblinding.

### E9

- Location: §2
- Quote: "There is no reason to publish a result known to be wrong, just because the analysis was done blindly."
- Key point: There is no reason to publish a result known to be wrong just because the analysis was blind.

### E10

- Location: §2
- Quote: "Ideally, an experiment should consider such situations in advance, to prepare for such cases."
- Key point: Ideally, decide in advance how to handle surprises that may come up after unblinding.

### E11

- Location: §2
- Quote: "Collaborations can require that the decision to unblind a result be made as a part of the internal review process, with the consultation or approval of a wider sub-group, and not by the data analysts alone."
- Key point: Collaborations can require that the decision to unblind be made as part of internal review, with the consultation or approval of a wider group, not by the analysts alone.

### E12

- Location: §2
- Quote: "Typical objections to the use of blind analysis techniques are that it slows the pace of data analysis, that certain aspects of the analysis become difficult, and that unexpected phenomena can only be found by full exploration of the data. The latter issue is a serious one."
- Key point: Common objections to blind analysis: it slows the analysis, makes some steps harder, and unexpected phenomena can only be found by fully exploring the data. The authors acknowledge the last is a serious issue.

### E13

- Location: §2
- Quote: "certain cross-checks may only be possible after the blind procedure has been removed."
- Key point: Some cross-checks are only possible after unblinding.

### E14

- Location: §3.2 Hidden Answer Methods
- Quote: "In general the hidden answer method works best for experiments measuring a single precise parameter, when that parameter does not depend directly on the number of observed events."
- Key point: The hidden answer method works best for experiments measuring a single precise parameter that does not depend directly on the number of observed events.

### E15

- Location: §3.2.1 Hidden Detector Parameters (Irvine gravity experiment)
- Quote: "they kept the value of their near mass known only to 1%--the exact mass known only to someone outside their collaboration." / "Subsequent improvements to the analysis were made and later published, but they nevertheless published the measurement made before these improvements were made."
- Key point: In a tabletop gravity experiment, the exact value of the near mass was known only to someone outside the collaboration, while the group knew it only to 1%; later improvements to the analysis were made, but they still published the measurement made before them.

### E16

- Location: §3.2.2 Hidden Offset
- Quote: "the competing groups should begin with different seeds for the pseudo-random generation of the hidden offset C and the hidden sign."
- Key point: When an experiment has several independent analysis groups, each uses a different random seed to generate its hidden offset and sign, and they cannot see each other's results.

### E17

- Location: §3.4
- Quote: "a blindness scheme is not intended to keep experimenters from looking at the data, but to keep the results of the analysis from influencing the analysis itself."
- Key point: Blind analysis does not keep experimenters from looking at the data; it keeps the results of the analysis from influencing the analysis itself.

### E18

- Location: §1
- Quote: "Other examples often cited as possible evidence of bias in measurements are the unusually low χ2 values found in global fits to data sets"
- Key point: Unusually low chi-square values in global fits (measurements agreeing "too well") are often cited as possible evidence of bias.

### E19

- Location: §4 Conclusion (quoting Feynman)
- Quote: "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself - and you are the easiest person to fool."
- Key point: The authors quote Feynman: the first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool.
