# Smith, Cullis, Thompson 2005: The analysis of crop cultivar breeding and evaluation trials: an overview of current mixed model approaches

- Source: `smith2005analysis`, [original](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0021859605005587)
- Version: Journal of Agricultural Science 143: 449-462, 2005 (part of the journal's centenary review series). The Cambridge University Press website offers the PDF free (Unpaywall marks it as open access). The authors are from the New South Wales Department of Primary Industries in Australia and Rothamsted Research in the UK. Locations give sections.

### E1

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "Typically, the yield data generated from crop breeding and evaluation programmes arises from series of field trials known as multi-environment trials (MET). These trials allow the investigation of varietal yield performance across a range of geographic locations and, possibly, years (seasons)."
- Key point: Yield data for breeding and variety evaluation usually come from a series of field trials called multi-environment trials, which are used to examine how varieties perform across locations and possibly years (seasons).

### E2

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "These advantages include the ease with which incomplete data (not all varieties in all environments) can be handled, the ability to use more realistic within-trial models for error variation (e.g. incomplete blocks, spatial correlation models) and the ability to assume some sets of effects (e.g. variety and/or environment effects) to be random rather than fixed."
- Key point: Advantages of analyzing multi-environment trials with linear mixed models: they easily handle incomplete data (not every variety is grown in every environment), allow more realistic models for within-trial error (incomplete blocks, spatial correlation models), and let variety and/or environment effects be treated as random.

### E3

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "is that it provides no insight into the nature of VrE interaction. This may hinder varietal selection and recommendation decisions."
- Key point: A major shortcoming of traditional analysis of variance is that it does not reveal the nature of the variety-by-environment interaction, which can hamper variety selection and recommendation.

### E4

- Location: Two-stage analyses
- Quote: "Many authors (Patterson & Silvey 1980; Talbot 1984; Patterson & Nabugoomu 1992) use a two-stage approach in which variety means are first obtained from the analyses of individual trials and are then combined to form the data for an overall mixed model analysis. The two-stage approach is an approximation to the more efficient one-stage approach."
- Key point: Two-stage analysis: first analyze each trial separately to get variety means, then combine them in an overall mixed model analysis. It approximates the more efficient one-stage analysis (which uses all plot data directly).

### E5

- Location: Two-stage analyses
- Quote: "Historically the two-stage approach was required due to the difficulty in obtaining individual plot data. Often this was not stored electronically but this should no longer be an issue."
- Key point: Two-stage analysis was used in the past because individual plot data were hard to obtain and often not stored electronically; this should no longer be a problem.

### E6

- Location: Conclusions
- Quote: "A further challenge is to encourage the use of random rather than fixed variety effects. This is not an easy task, particularly as this is still a controversial topic among statisticians."
- Key point: Encouraging people to treat variety effects as random rather than fixed is not easy, and it remains contentious among statisticians.

### E7

- Location: Conclusions
- Quote: "the present authors believe that variety effects should be assumed to be random since this minimizes selection errors when identifying the best varieties"
- Key point: The authors argue that variety effects should be treated as random, because this minimizes the chance of error when selecting the best varieties.
