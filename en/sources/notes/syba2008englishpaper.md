# Harvard College Writing Center: A Brief Guide to Writing the English Paper

- Source: `syba2008englishpaper`, [original](https://writingproject.fas.harvard.edu/files/hwp/files/bg_writing_english.pdf)
- Version: Part of the Harvard College Writing Center's "Brief Guides" series, written by Michelle Syba, copyright 2008; the original URL returns 403 to command-line access, so the PDF was read via an Internet Archive snapshot from November 2024. The original is in a two-column layout, and the text was extracted column by column

### E1

- Location: The Challenges of Writing About English Literature
- Quote: "When you perform a close reading, then, you observe how form and content interact."
- Key point: Close reading means observing how form and content interact.

### E2

- Location: The Challenges of Writing About English Literature
- Quote: "When you close read, take an inductive approach. Start by observing particular details in the text, such as a repeated image or word, an unexpected development, or even a contradiction."
- Key point: Close reading works inductively: it starts from specific details, such as repeated images or words, unexpected turns, or even contradictions.

### E3

- Location: The Challenges of Writing About English Literature
- Quote: "If you can make a case about how an apparently trivial detail reveals something significant about the text, then your paper will have a thought-provoking thesis to argue."
- Key point: If you can argue that a seemingly trivial detail reveals something important about the text, your paper has a thought-provoking thesis.

### E4

- Location: Common Types of English Papers
- Quote: "Others, however, will ask you to read two or more texts in relation to each other, or to consider a text in light of claims made by other scholars and critics."
- Key point: Some papers read several texts together, or read one text in light of the views of other scholars and critics.

### E5

- Location: Common Types of English Papers, A theoretically-informed close reading
- Quote: "Critical theories provide focus for your analysis; if “abjection” is the guiding concept for your paper, you should focus on the scenes in the novel that are most relevant to the concept."
- Key point: Theory gives the analysis a focus; guided by a particular concept, you concentrate on the scenes most relevant to that concept.

### E6

- Location: Common Types of English Papers, A historically-informed close reading
- Quote: "The tracts could help you to show not only how Robinson Crusoe draws on Puritan narrative conventions, but also—more significantly—how the novel revises those conventions."
- Key point: Using historical documents (such as Puritan pamphlets) is not just about showing which conventions a novel borrows; more importantly, it shows how the novel reworks those conventions.

### E7

- Location: Example #1: Close Reading Prose, step 2
- Quote: "This should be a list not just of page numbers but of quotations."
- Key point: When gathering evidence, record not just page numbers in your list but also copy out the quotations.

### E8

- Location: Example #1: Close Reading Prose
- Quote: "If a scene or an object is depicted repeatedly, it is probably important for the book as a whole. In most cases, it will be depicted in different ways that complicate or conflict with each other."
- Key point: Recurring scenes or objects are probably important, and the way they are written each time often complicates or even conflicts with the others.

### E9

- Location: Example #2: Close Reading Poetry, step 3
- Quote: "Your thesis won’t simply point out the development, however; it will make an argument about the development’s significance."
- Key point: A thesis does not just point out a change; it argues for what the change means.

### E10

- Location: Example #2, explanation of the thesis
- Quote: "The thesis identifies a tension between two moments in the poem and doesn’t try to tidy up or argue away that tension." / "Weird complications tend to offer richer material than complications easily overcome and tensions tidily reconciled."
- Key point: A good thesis identifies a tension rather than trying to smooth it over; strange complexities offer richer material than contradictions that are easily overcome.

### E11

- Location: Example #2, explanation of the thesis
- Quote: "The thesis isn’t particularly combative or controversial. Instead, it offers a way of understanding the poem that wouldn’t be apparent to a first-time reader"
- Key point: A thesis need not be combative or manufacture controversy; it offers a way of understanding the text that is not apparent on a first reading.

### E12

- Location: After Questions to Ask as You Read a Text
- Quote: "In some single-author courses you might work with manuscript drafts and biography, and thereby have sources with which to speculate about an author’s intentions. More often, however, you will not have enough evidence to speculate intelligently about the author’s intentions. In the absence of such evidence, orient your claims towards the text."
- Key point: When manuscripts and biographical material are available, you can speculate about authorial intent; more often there is not enough evidence, so direct your claims at the text.

### E13

- Location: Tips and Conventions, Avoid plot summary
- Quote: "A paper that recounts what happened in a novel (or a play)—or that analyzes selected scenes in the same order they occur in the novel—is letting the novel’s author rather than the paper’s author structure the paper’s argument."
- Key point: Retelling the plot, or analyzing scenes one by one in the novel's order, lets the novelist rather than the paper's author structure the argument.

### E14

- Location: Tips and Conventions, Avoid plot summary
- Quote: "One rule of thumb is to begin with the most obvious piece of evidence and move progressively to the least obvious piece of evidence."
- Key point: A rule of thumb: start with the most obvious evidence and work toward the least obvious.

### E15

- Location: Tips and Conventions, Avoid basing your argument on opinion
- Quote: "Textual evidence and not personal conviction should be the basis of your thesis and argument."
- Key point: The thesis and argument rest on textual evidence, not personal beliefs.

### E16

- Location: Tips and Conventions, Focus on speakers, not authors
- Quote: "first-person poems and narratives have a “speaker” or “narrator” who should not be confused with the author."
- Key point: Do not confuse the "speaker" or "narrator" of first-person poems and narratives with the author.

### E17

- Location: Tips and Conventions, Use block quotations appropriately
- Quote: "When you use a block quotation, make sure that it is rich enough to reward extended analysis (which should be at least as long as the quotation itself)."
- Key point: When you use a long quotation, make sure it is worth analyzing at length, and that your analysis is at least as long as the quotation.

### E18

- Location: Tips and Conventions, Use block quotations appropriately
- Quote: "averting the stagnant paper structure in which a thesis is followed by a list of illustrative examples."
- Key point: Avoid the static structure of "thesis plus a list of examples."

### E19

- Location: Common Types of English Papers, A research paper
- Quote: "When reading articles, make sure that they have been peer reviewed"
- Key point: When reading research articles, make sure they are peer-reviewed.

### E20

- Location: Example #1, step 4
- Quote: "In your paper, you need not account for every single usage of the word “heart”; select the examples that are most in tension with the primary meaning"
- Key point: You do not need to account for every usage; pick the examples in greatest tension with the main meaning.
