# Georgetown Law Writing Center 2025: Using Cases in Legal Analysis: Methods and Examples

- Source: `georgetown2025cases`, [Original PDF (Writing Center, Georgetown University Law Center, US; free)](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/academics/wp-content/uploads/sites/58/2025/08/Using_Cases_in_Legal_Analysis_Methods_and_ExamplesFCD.pdf)
- Version: Handout from the Writing Center at Georgetown University Law Center, first drafted by Alice Hsieh in 2012 and revised by Justin New in 2025. Written for **US law students**, in the context of a common law system like that of the US; most examples come from legal memos. Locations are given as handout subheadings.

### E1

- Location: Opening
- Quote: "All of these uses of cases are relevant in a common law system where judges create binding law through cases"
- Key point: The handout notes that these ways of using cases apply in a common law system, where judges create binding law through cases.

### E2

- Location: Identifying and Briefing Cases
- Quote: "The briefing method is useful when selecting and preparing cases for analysis, as it allows you to dissect a case for later reassembly."
- Key point: Case briefing helps you select and prepare cases for analysis: you dissect a case first and reassemble it later.

### E3

- Location: Identifying and Briefing Cases
- Quote: "Issue presented to the court." / "Facts of the case." / "Procedural posture of the case." / "Holding, including the answer to the issue and the court's reasoning." / "Commentary, or dicta, provided by the court." / "Analytical approach or pattern used by the court."
- Key point: Possible headings for a brief: the issue presented to the court; the facts; the procedural posture; the holding (the answer to the issue and the court's reasoning); the court's commentary (dicta); and the court's analytical approach or pattern. The handout also lists the standard of review and the prevailing rule in the area.

### E4

- Location: Identifying and Briefing Cases
- Quote: "Be mindful of the particular issue you are addressing and do not be afraid to engage with your argument's weaknesses."
- Key point: Choose cases with the specific issue you are addressing in mind, and do not avoid the weaknesses in your argument.

### E5

- Location: Cases Interpreting Statutes
- Quote: "If the statute has codified common law, then cases existing before the codification are good law and useful in interpreting the statute. If the statute has partially or wholly rejected common law, then previous cases are likely not good law, but may at least be useful in determining why the statute reads as it does."
- Key point: If a statute codified common law, cases from before the codification are still good law and help interpret it; if the statute partially or wholly rejected common law, earlier cases are likely no longer good law but can help explain why the statute reads as it does.

### E6

- Location: Cases Interpreting Statutes
- Quote: "If you are working with a statute, it is not enough to quote the statute and apply your case. Rather, your analysis should include the key language from case law that interprets and updates the statute."
- Key point: When analyzing a statute, quoting the text is not enough; include the key language from the case law that interprets and updates it.

### E7

- Location: Cases Used to Synthesize a Rule
- Quote: "Use this approach when you need to weave together case law to articulate a rule." / "Based on these three cases, a synthesized rule might state"
- Key point: When you need to weave several cases together to state a rule, use the synthesized-rule approach; the handout's example derives a rule from three cases (two granting the claim, one denying it).

### E8

- Location: Cases Demonstrating Interpretive Patterns Or Legal Trends
- Quote: "Because the courts may be split in how they interpret a certain area of law--with some using sociological methods, others recognizing the role of medicine in the law, others factoring in history, and so on--identifying trends can be a useful mode of argumentation."
- Key point: Courts may be split in how they interpret an area of law (some using sociological methods, some weighing medicine, some history), and identifying these trends can itself be a mode of argument.
