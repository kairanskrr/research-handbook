# Eisner 2009: How to Read a Technical Paper

- Source: `eisnerread`, [original](https://www.cs.jhu.edu/~jason/advice/how-to-read-a-paper.html)
- Version: Web page, dated 2009, last modified 2018-08-05

### E1

- Location: Multi-pass reading
- Quote: "Skim the paper first, skipping over anything that would take much mental effort. Just get an idea of where the paper is going, why it was written, what's old hat and what's new to you."
- Key point: Skim first, skipping anything that takes much mental effort; just work out where the paper is going, why it was written, what is old and what is new to you.

### E2

- Location: Write as you read
- Quote: "Write as you read. This keeps your attention focused and makes you engage with the paper."
- Key point: Writing as you read keeps your attention focused and makes you engage with the paper.

### E3

- Location: Low-level notes
- Quote: "restate unclear points in your own words [...] come up with examples that illustrate the author's ideas, and examples that would be problematic for the author [...] challenge the paper's claims or methods"
- Key point: Annotate the paper: restate unclear points in your own words, come up with examples that illustrate the author's ideas and examples that would be problematic for them, and question the paper's claims and methods.

### E4

- Location: High-level notes
- Quote: "Low-level notes aren't enough. Also keep high-level notes about papers. You should try to distill the paper down: summarize the things that interested you, contrast with other papers, and record your own questions and ideas for future work."
- Key point: Annotations are not enough; also keep high-level notes: distill the paper, compare it with other papers, and record your own questions and follow-up ideas.

### E5

- Location: High-level notes
- Quote: "At a minimum, you should re-explain the ideas in your own words: produce some text that is aimed at your future self. You should be able to reread this later and quickly reconstruct your understanding of the paper."
- Key point: At a minimum, re-explain the ideas in your own words for your future self, so that you can reread the notes later and quickly recover your understanding.

### E6

- Location: High-level notes (quoting Michael Mitzenmacher)
- Quote: "Read creatively. Reading a paper critically is easy, in that it is always easier to tear something down than to build it up. Reading creatively involves harder, more positive thinking. What are the good ideas in this paper?"
- Key point: Reading critically is easy; reading creatively is harder: find the good ideas in the paper and think about how they could be generalized and improved.

### E7

- Location: Which parts to focus on
- Quote: "I do think that when you are learning a new area, you should read at least some papers extremely thoroughly. That means knowing what every sentence and every subscript is doing, so that you really learn all of the techniques used in the paper."
- Key point: When learning a new area, read at least some papers extremely thoroughly, knowing what every sentence and every subscript is doing.

### E8

- Location: Which parts to focus on — Experiments
- Quote: "then you should go back and carefully examine the experimental design, including the choice of data. Were the experiments fair? Do they support the claims? What's really going on? Are the conclusions likely to generalize beyond this experimental scenario?"
- Key point: If you care about a paper's conclusions (whether the method works, whether to use it), examine the experimental design and choice of data carefully: were the experiments fair, do they support the claims, and do the conclusions generalize beyond this setting?

### E9

- Location: Set aside time
- Quote: "There is no shame in reading slowly. It still takes me several hours to absorb a paper on something that I genuinely don't know well."
- Key point: There is no shame in reading slowly; even an experienced researcher takes several hours to absorb a paper in an unfamiliar area.

### E10

- Location: What to read
- Quote: "put yourself in an author's shoes; what phrases might they have used?"
- Key point: When searching, put yourself in the authors' shoes: what phrases might they have used for the problem?

### E11

- Location: What to read
- Quote: "backward references: follow the bibliography to earlier papers [...] forward references: see who else has cited the work"
- Key point: Once you find a relevant paper, follow its bibliography backward to earlier papers and look forward to see who has cited it.

### E12

- Location: What to read
- Quote: "has someone else already listed the right papers for you? [...] course syllabi [...] literature review chapters from dissertations"
- Key point: First check whether someone has already listed the right papers for you: survey articles, course syllabi, the literature review chapters of dissertations, and so on.

### E13

- Location: What to read
- Quote: "read a lot of abstracts (and skim the papers as needed) before deciding which papers are best to read"
- Key point: Read a lot of abstracts first, then decide which papers are most worth reading closely.

### E14

- Location: Which parts to focus on
- Quote: "If you don't know who to ask, find other papers that cite this one (via Google Scholar) and see what they say about this paper."
- Key point: If you cannot tell what matters in a paper, find papers that cite it and see what they say about it.
