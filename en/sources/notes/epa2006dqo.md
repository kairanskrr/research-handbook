# US Environmental Protection Agency 2006: Guidance on Systematic Planning Using the Data Quality Objectives Process (EPA QA/G-4)

- Source: `epa2006dqo`, [original PDF](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-06/documents/g4-final.pdf)
- Version: EPA/240/B-06/001, February 2006. One of the quality system guidance documents of the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). Locations give the PDF page (counting from the cover).

### E1

- Location: Introduction, PDF p. 11
- Quote: "Unless some form of planning is conducted prior to investing the necessary time and resources to collect data; the chances can be unacceptably high that these data will not meet specific project needs."
- Key point: Without planning before sampling, the chances that the data collected will not meet the project's needs can be unacceptably high.

### E2

- Location: Introduction, PDF p. 16
- Quote: "The DQO Process is the Agency's recommendation when data are to be used to make some type of decision (e.g., compliance or non-compliance with a standard) or estimation (e.g., ascertain the mean concentration level of a contaminant)."
- Key point: When data will be used to make a decision (e.g. compliance or non-compliance with a standard) or an estimate (e.g. the mean concentration of a contaminant), EPA recommends planning with the data quality objectives (DQO) process.

### E3

- Location: Introduction, PDF p. 16
- Quote: "The Triad Approach, developed by EPA's Technology Innovation Program, combines systematic planning with more recent technology advancements, such as techniques that allow for results of early sampling to inform the direction of future sampling."
- Key point: EPA's Triad Approach combines systematic planning with newer technologies so that early sampling results guide later sampling.

### E4

- Location: Introduction, PDF pp. 17–18, Figure 2
- Quote: "The DQO Process consists of seven iterative steps that are documented in Figure 2." / "Step 1. State the Problem. [...] Step 2. Identify the Goal of the Study. [...] Step 3. Identify Information Inputs. [...] Step 4. Define the Boundaries of the Study [...] Step 5. Develop the Analytic Approach. [...] Step 6. Specify Performance or Acceptance Criteria [...] Step 7. Develop the Plan for Obtaining Data"
- Key point: The DQO process has seven iterative steps: state the problem, identify the goal of the study, identify information inputs, define the boundaries of the study, develop the analytic approach, specify performance or acceptance criteria, and develop the plan for obtaining data.

### E5

- Location: Chapter 1, PDF p. 26
- Quote: "The DQO planning team is typically composed of the project manager, technical staff, data users, and stakeholders."
- Key point: The planning team typically includes the project manager, technical staff, data users, and stakeholders.

### E6

- Location: Chapter 4, PDF p. 41
- Quote: "When the target population consists of continuous media, such as air, water, or soil, the sampling unit must be defined as some area, volume, or mass that may be selected from the target population."
- Key point: When the target population is a continuous medium such as air, water, or soil, the sampling unit must be defined as some area, volume, or mass.

### E7

- Location: Chapter 6, PDF p. 57
- Quote: "In general, sampling error is much larger than measurement error and consequently needs a larger proportion of resources to control."
- Key point: In general, sampling error is much larger than measurement error and so needs a larger share of resources to control.

### E8

- Location: Chapter 6, PDF p. 60
- Quote: "You can never totally eliminate the possibility that you will make a decision error from your data when performing a statistical hypothesis test."
- Key point: When you run a statistical test on your data, you can never totally eliminate the possibility of a decision error.

### E9

- Location: Chapter 7, PDF p. 83
- Quote: "if you expect spatial or temporal variability in the data to be very high, you may wish to consider designs that use less expensive and less precise analytical methods, so that you can focus your resources on collecting a larger number of samples over space and time"
- Key point: If you expect very high spatial or temporal variability, consider cheaper, less precise analytical methods and put the resources into taking more samples at more locations and times.

### E10

- Location: Chapter 3, PDF p. 38
- Quote: "If you can address your problem in part through the use of an existing data set, then you should inquire about its quality assurance and control information to assess whether the data will satisfy your needs."
- Key point: Before using an existing data set, ask about its quality assurance and quality control information to judge whether it meets your needs.

### E11

- Location: Introduction, PDF p. 12
- Quote: "The process uses a common-sense approach to ensure that the level of documentation and rigor of effort in planning is commensurate with the intended use of the information and the available resources."
- Key point: The level of detail and rigor in planning should be commensurate with the intended use of the data and the resources available.

### E12

- Location: Chapter 7, PDF p. 83
- Quote: "In a probability-based sampling design, each possible sampling unit has a known probability of being selected, and only those sampling units selected will provide data for the study. In a judgmental sampling design, the sampling units are not assigned a known probability of being selected, but rather, are selected at the discretion of the person in charge of the sampling effort."
- Key point: In a probability-based sampling design, each possible sampling unit has a known probability of selection; in a judgmental design, the units are chosen by the judgment of the person sampling, and the probability of selection is unknown.

### E13

- Location: Chapter 7, PDF p. 83
- Quote: "Examples of common probability-based sampling approaches include simple random sampling, stratified sampling, and systematic and grid sampling. Probability-based sampling allows you to draw quantitative conclusions about the target population, while also properly expressing uncertainty in these conclusions"
- Key point: Common probability-based designs include simple random, stratified, systematic, and grid sampling; they let you draw quantitative conclusions about the target population and properly express the uncertainty of those conclusions.

### E14

- Location: Chapter 7, PDF p. 83
- Quote: "However, judgmental sampling designs will not allow you to characterize uncertainty properly. As a result, the outcome of statistical analysis on data collected through judgmental sampling cannot be used to make any type of scientifically-defensible probabilistic statements about the target population."
- Key point: Judgmental sampling does not let you characterize uncertainty properly; statistical analysis of judgmentally sampled data cannot support defensible probabilistic statements about the target population.

### E15

- Location: Chapter 7, PDF p. 84
- Quote: "if the spatial properties of a target population (e.g., an area of soil to be characterized) indicate that different subareas of the population have different underlying characteristics (e.g., different portions of the area of interest had different prior uses), then utilizing a stratified sampling design with strata that correspond to these distinct subareas will help ensure proper representation of all characteristics associated with the given population."
- Key point: If different parts of the study area have different properties (e.g. different past land use), stratifying the sample by these parts ensures each one is represented.
