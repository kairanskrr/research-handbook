Locations give "page name / subheading." This file is a local excerpt made for task no. 106.

### E1

- Location: Philosophical Arguments / What Is an Argument?
- Quote: "An important thing to remember is that even if a given argument is bad, its conclusion might still be true"
- Key point: A bad argument does not mean its conclusion is false.

### E2

- Location: Philosophical Arguments / An Argument's Structure
- Quote: "When you're arguing, you'll usually take certain theses for granted (these are the premises of your argument) and attempt to show that if one accepts those premises, then one ought also to accept the argument's conclusion."
- Key point: To argue is to take certain theses as premises and show that anyone who accepts them should also accept the conclusion.

### E3

- Location: Philosophical Arguments / Different Flavors of Argument
- Quote: "Mathematical proofs are good examples of this. Most of the arguments philosophers concern themselves with are also — or purport to be — arguments of this flavor."
- Key point: The typical deductive argument (if the premises are true, the conclusion must be) is the mathematical proof; most arguments philosophers deal with are, or purport to be, of this kind.

### E4

- Location: Philosophical Arguments / What Makes a Deductive Argument Good?
- Quote: "Are its premises true and worth accepting?" / "Does its conclusion really follow from the premises?" / "These are completely independent issues."
- Key point: Evaluating a deductive argument means asking two independent questions: are the premises true and worth accepting, and does the conclusion really follow from them?

### E5

- Location: Philosophical Arguments / Valid Arguments
- Quote: "To tell whether an argument is valid, figure out what the form of the argument is, and then try to think of some other argument of that same form and having true premises but a false conclusion. If you succeed, then every argument of that form must be invalid."
- Key point: To test validity, identify the argument's form, then look for another argument of the same form with true premises and a false conclusion; if you find one, the form is invalid.

### E6

- Location: Philosophical Arguments / Implicit or Hidden Premises
- Quote: "Sometimes a premise is left out because the author takes it to be obvious, as in the engineer argument and the exploding car argument. But sometimes the missing premise is very contentious, as in the abortion argument."
- Key point: An unstated premise is sometimes just something the author finds obvious, and sometimes highly contentious.

### E7

- Location: Philosophical Arguments / Sound Arguments
- Quote: "An argument is sound just in case it's valid and all its premises are in fact true."
- Key point: Sound = valid + all premises actually true.

### E8

- Location: Philosophical Arguments / Sound Arguments
- Quote: "When you're doing philosophy, it is never enough simply to say that you disagree with someone's conclusion, or that their conclusion is wrong. If your opponent's conclusion is wrong, then there must be something wrong with their argument, and you need to say what you think it is."
- Key point: Simply saying you disagree with someone's conclusion is never enough; if the conclusion is wrong, something in the argument must be wrong, and you must say what.

### E9

- Location: Philosophical Arguments / Persuasive Arguments
- Quote: "Their premises should be uncontentious — or at least, less contentious than the argument's conclusion."
- Key point: A persuasive argument has premises that are uncontentious, or at least less contentious than its conclusion.

### E10

- Location: Philosophical Arguments / Persuasive Arguments
- Quote: "In any event, an argument's premises should be credible independently of its conclusion — in the sense that it'd be possible for someone to reasonably accept the premises even if they had not already accepted the conclusion."
- Key point: Premises should be credible independently of the conclusion: someone who does not already accept the conclusion could reasonably accept them.

### E11

- Location: Philosophical Arguments / Begging the Question
- Quote: "A good rule of thumb is the following: if an argument contains a premise or step that would not be accepted by a reasonable person who is initially prone to doubt the argument's conclusion, then the argument begs the question."
- Key point: A rule of thumb for begging the question (circular reasoning): if a premise or step would not be accepted by a reasonable person initially inclined to doubt the conclusion, the argument begs the question.

### E12

- Location: Philosophical Arguments / Conclusive or Not?
- Quote: "often (perhaps always) we won't be in positions where the premises of an argument are absolutely indubitable and immune to philosophical challenge. So although we're working with deductive arguments, these arguments usually won't decisively settle the debates we're considering."
- Key point: The premises of philosophical arguments are rarely beyond all doubt, so even deductive arguments usually don't settle the matter once and for all.

### E13

- Location: What is the Philosophical Method? / What do philosophers do?
- Quote: "They try to unearth hidden assumptions in their opponents' arguments. They try to show that their opponents' views are inconsistent or lead to absurd results"
- Key point: Philosophers unearth hidden assumptions in their opponents' arguments, or show that their views are inconsistent or lead to absurd results.

### E14

- Location: What is the Philosophical Method? / Appeals to authority/popularity and the burden of proof
- Quote: "Where the burden of proof lies will sometimes depend on the "dialectical situation," that is, on the kind of debate the parties are having."
- Key point: Where the burden of proof lies sometimes depends on the "dialectical situation," i.e., what kind of debate the parties are having.

### E15

- Location: What is the Philosophical Method? / Appeals to authority/popularity and the burden of proof
- Quote: "a mere statement that the renowned Professor X holds a certain position carries no argumentative weight of its own."
- Key point: The mere fact that a renowned professor holds a position carries no argumentative weight of its own.

### E16

- Location: What is the Philosophical Method? / Shifting the burden of proof
- Quote: "Arguments by analogy often exemplify such shifts in the burden of proof, because they are hostage to unnoticed or unacknowledged disanalogies."
- Key point: Arguments by analogy are hostage to unnoticed or unacknowledged disanalogies.

### E17

- Location: What is the Philosophical Method? / Charity
- Quote: "We should exercise "charity" when interpreting our opponent's arguments. That is, we should try to understand them in the strongest versions we can — even if that requires helping the original authors out in some ways."
- Key point: The principle of charity: interpret your opponent's argument in its strongest version, even helping the original author strengthen it if necessary.

### E18

- Location: What is the Philosophical Method? / Charity
- Quote: "We can help our own view the most by engaging with and responding to the strongest arguments we know the other side can give."
- Key point: Responding to the strongest arguments the other side can give does the most for your own view.

### E19

- Location: Distinctions and Definitions / Equivocating
- Quote: "Equivocation is a bad form of argument where some of the key terms can be understood in multiple ways, and the plausibility of the argument depends on reading the term differently in different places."
- Key point: Equivocation: a key term can be understood in several ways, and the argument only looks good because the term is read differently in different places.

### E20

- Location: Distinctions and Definitions / (Different Kinds of) Definitions
- Quote: "Another kind of activity philosophers can go in for is stipulating that they're going to use some expression in a specific way, which might not be the way other people use the word, or understand it in other contexts." / "It's important not to confuse these."
- Key point: Besides analyzing our existing concepts, philosophers can stipulate how they will use a word in their own work; don't confuse the two activities.

### E21

- Location: Distinctions and Definitions / (Different Kinds of) Definitions
- Quote: "She wants to know what we're thinking and talking about when we think and talk about belief and death. She's seeking an analysis of our pre-existing concepts."
- Key point: Asking "what is belief?" or "what is death?" seeks not a stipulation but an analysis of our existing concepts.

### E22

- Location: Distinctions and Definitions / Thought-Experiments and Counter-Examples
- Quote: "One way we test analyses is by trying to come up with counter-examples"
- Key point: One way to test a conceptual analysis is to look for counterexamples.

### E23

- Location: Distinctions and Definitions / Thought-Experiments and Counter-Examples
- Quote: "Perhaps it's not in fact technologically possible to freeze a person and revive him again. This is not important." / "So if it's possible even in principle for someone to be frozen, and for his biological processes to stop, without his thereby dying, then Professor Smith's proposed analysis is wrong."
- Key point: An analysis claims to hold in every possible case, so a counterexample that is possible even in principle refutes it; whether it is technologically feasible does not matter.

### E24

- Location: Distinctions and Definitions / Thought-Experiments and Counter-Examples
- Quote: "We should recognize and acknowledge that such intuitions aren't sacrosanct. Sometimes they're wrong." / "But in general, there is a presumption that our pre-theoretical intuitions are true, and we should throw them out only if we have very strong reasons for doing so."
- Key point: Intuitions are not sacrosanct and are sometimes wrong, but in general pre-theoretical intuitions are presumed true and should be abandoned only for strong reasons.

### E25

- Location: Distinctions and Definitions / Thought-Experiments and Counter-Examples
- Quote: "you haven't in fact described a coherent possibility, and so you haven't succeeded in offering a genuine counter-example to his claim." / "Sometimes it can be hard to tell whether you've described a coherent possibility or not."
- Key point: If the situation you describe is incoherent (like a "round square"), it is not a genuine counterexample; sometimes it is hard to tell whether it is coherent.

### E26

- Location: Distinctions and Definitions / Thought-Experiments and Counter-Examples
- Quote: "In general, we want to distinguish between questions about what it is to be X and questions about how we find out that something is X."
- Key point: Distinguish questions about what it is to be X from questions about how we find out that something is X.

### E27

- Location: What is the Philosophical Method? / Working from the middle
- Quote: "In philosophy, there's hardly anything that's really immune to questioning. And the more supporting weight you want some assumption to bear, the more ways philosophers will find to challenge it."
- Key point: In philosophy hardly anything is immune to questioning; the more weight you put on an assumption, the more ways others will find to challenge it.

### E28

- Location: What is the Philosophical Method? / Working from the middle
- Quote: "I usually won't mean your objection must be fatal to the argument. Maybe the argument can be fixed up in some way to work around it."
- Key point: A "good objection" need not be fatal; the argument may be fixable to work around it.

### E29

- Location: Philosophical Arguments / Sound Arguments (quoted from his Philosophical Glossary)
- Quote: "You should not say that Locke has proven some claim, or shown or demonstrated that something is the case, unless you think that Locke's arguments for his claim are successful."
- Key point: Don't say someone "proved" something unless you think the argument succeeds; write "argued" or "tried to show."
