# Schmidt 2016: Using Archives: A Guide to Effective Research

- Source: `schmidt2016using`, [original](https://www2.archivists.org/usingarchives)
- Version: A free guide on the website of the Society of American Archivists (SAA, the professional association of US archivists), by Laura Schmidt; the page says it was updated 2016-03-26; read 2026-10-06. The guide has ten web pages (what are archives, types of archives, finding and evaluating archives, requesting materials remotely, planning a visit, common rules of use, copyright, restrictions, and unprocessed collections, visiting the archives, conclusion, and appendix: sample annotated finding aid). Locations give page titles. The guide is written for US readers, and the search tools and laws it mentions are American.

### E1

- Location: What Are Archives and How Do They Differ from Libraries?
- Quote: "Materials in an archives are often unique, specialized, or rare objects, meaning very few of them exist in the world, or they are the only ones of their kind."
- Key point: Materials in archives are often unique or very rare.

### E2

- Location: Types of Archives
- Quote: "Defining your research topic and knowing what sorts of materials you are looking for will help you determine the appropriate institutions to contact."
- Key point: Define your research topic and know what kinds of materials you are looking for first; that tells you which institutions to contact.

### E3

- Location: Finding and Evaluating Archives
- Quote: "Consult bibliographies and works cited sections in books on your topic. Where did the information come from?"
- Key point: Check the bibliographies and works cited in books on your topic: where did their information come from?

### E4

- Location: Finding and Evaluating Archives
- Quote: "Contact other experts in the field. Which repositories did they visit for their research?"
- Key point: Ask other experts in the field which repositories they visited.

### E5

- Location: Finding and Evaluating Archives, Finding Aids
- Quote: "A finding aid (sometimes called inventory, collection listing, register, or calendar) is a text document providing a description of the contents of a collection, just like a table of contents outlines the contents of a book."
- Key point: A finding aid is a document describing the contents of a collection, like a book's table of contents.

### E6

- Location: Finding and Evaluating Archives, Finding Aids
- Quote: "Finding aids sometimes provide narrative portions describing the background of a collection (how and when it was formed, how the archives acquired it, etc.), and how the archival staff has arranged or ordered the materials in the collection."
- Key point: Finding aids sometimes describe a collection's background (how and when it was formed, how the archives acquired it) and how the materials are arranged.

### E7

- Location: Finding and Evaluating Archives, Digital Collections
- Quote: "However, be aware that digital collections often reflect just a fraction of the total holdings of a repository. There may be nondigitized materials at the same institution that are also pertinent to your research."
- Key point: Digital collections are often only a fraction of a repository's holdings; the same institution may hold relevant materials that are not digitized.

### E8

- Location: Finding and Evaluating Archives, Digital Collections
- Quote: "Note that when searching digital collections online it can sometimes be unclear whether the items you are viewing represent a complete collection or are part of a larger collection. Try to determine the highest collection level for the most complete overview of related items."
- Key point: Online, it may be unclear whether you are seeing a complete collection or part of a larger one; find the highest collection level to see the whole picture.

### E9

- Location: Finding and Evaluating Archives, Archival Staff
- Quote: "One of the most important ways to evaluate the holdings of an archives is contacting an archival professional who oversees the collections." / "I have already viewed the following [finding aids, catalogs, etc.] on your website, and thought that these specific resources would be useful for my research" / "Do you have any recommendations of other relevant materials in your collections?"
- Key point: One of the most important ways to evaluate an archive's holdings is to contact the archivist responsible for the collections. The guide's sample email describes the research project, lists the finding aids already viewed and the materials wanted, and asks whether other relevant materials could be recommended.

### E10

- Location: Finding and Evaluating Archives, Archival Staff
- Quote: "After you have examined the catalogs, finding aids, and website of an archives, call or email the repository to confirm your findings and conclusions."
- Key point: Go through the catalogs, finding aids, and website first, then call or email the archive to confirm.

### E11

- Location: Finding and Evaluating Archives, Archival Staff
- Quote: "In the case of an archives that does not list collections on its website (or does not have a website), contacting the staff is the only way to ascertain its holdings."
- Key point: For archives that do not list collections on their website (or have no website), contacting the staff is the only way to learn what they hold.

### E12

- Location: Requesting Materials Remotely
- Quote: "Will the archives provide scans or photocopies of the materials you wish to consult? What are the fees for those services?"
- Key point: Ask whether the archives provide scans or photocopies, and what they charge.

### E13

- Location: Requesting Materials Remotely
- Quote: "Will the archives allow a research assistant to access materials on your behalf?"
- Key point: Ask whether a research assistant may consult materials on your behalf.

### E14

- Location: Requesting Materials Remotely
- Quote: "Archivists routinely answer reference questions for researchers, so if the information you need can be retrieved in a short amount of time, there is a good chance they can relay it to you without having you come in person."
- Key point: Archivists routinely answer reference questions; if the information can be found quickly, they will likely just tell you, with no need to visit.

### E15

- Location: Planning to Visit an Archives
- Quote: "If the materials you are requesting are stored off-site, they may take several hours or days to retrieve."
- Key point: Materials stored off-site may take hours or even days to retrieve.

### E16

- Location: Planning to Visit an Archives
- Quote: "Check to see whether there are any limits on the amount of materials you may request or specific request times."
- Key point: Check in advance how much you may request at once and when requests can be submitted.

### E17

- Location: Planning to Visit an Archives
- Quote: "Examine the reproduction policies of the archives. Regulations and fees for requesting photocopies, scans, digital photography, microfilming, and reproductions of photos and audio-visual materials vary among archives."
- Key point: Check the reproduction policies in advance; rules and fees for photocopies, scans, photography, and so on vary between archives.

### E18

- Location: Planning to Visit an Archives
- Quote: "Inquire whether any opportunities for research grants or funding are offered by the archives."
- Key point: Ask whether the archives offer research grants.

### E19

- Location: Planning to Visit an Archives
- Quote: "Schedule some additional time for the unexpected." / "Also, consider the option of a return visit to the archives in case you need to verify information, check additional materials, or pursue something you had not thought of earlier."
- Key point: Leave time for the unexpected, and consider a return visit to verify information, check more materials, or follow new leads.

### E20

- Location: Typical Usage Guidelines in Archival Repositories, Registry and personal identification
- Quote: "Some archives also require a note of recommendation or special permissions before admitting researchers."
- Key point: Some archives require a letter of recommendation or special permission before admitting researchers.

### E21

- Location: Typical Usage Guidelines in Archival Repositories, Use of pencil only
- Quote: "This is a preservation practice in case accidental marks are made on archival materials; pencil can be erased while pen marks cannot."
- Key point: Pencils only, so that any accidental marks on archival materials can be erased.

### E22

- Location: Typical Usage Guidelines in Archival Repositories, Careful handling and maintaining order
- Quote: "It is also important that materials remain in the order in which the researcher received them so they can be located later and observed in their proper contexts."
- Key point: Keep materials in the order you received them, so they can be found later and seen in their proper context.

### E23

- Location: Notes on Copyright, Restrictions, and Unprocessed Collections, Copyright
- Quote: "In other words even if the archives physically owns a particular document, the copyright of the document and stipulations on how it can be used may be managed by another individual or institution."
- Key point: An archive owning a document physically does not mean it owns the copyright; copyright and conditions of use may belong to someone else.

### E24

- Location: Notes on Copyright, Restrictions, and Unprocessed Collections, Copyright
- Quote: "It is the responsibility of researchers to find the copyright holder in order to publish or cite from the materials."
- Key point: To publish or quote from the materials, researchers are responsible for finding the copyright holder (this describes the US situation).

### E25

- Location: Notes on Copyright, Restrictions, and Unprocessed Collections, Restrictions
- Quote: "The donor who originally gave materials to the archives set a time limit or certain stipulations on how those materials could be used, generally due to privacy concerns or sensitive materials."
- Key point: One reason for restricted access is that the donor set a time limit or conditions of use, usually because of privacy or sensitive content.

### E26

- Location: Notes on Copyright, Restrictions, and Unprocessed Collections, Restrictions
- Quote: "U.S. Census records are restricted for seventy-two years before they become available to researchers to protect personal information."
- Key point: For example, US Census records are closed for 72 years before researchers can see them, to protect personal information.

### E27

- Location: Notes on Copyright, Restrictions, and Unprocessed Collections, Restrictions
- Quote: "Researchers may, in some cases, gain access to restricted materials if they file a petition or request permission from the appropriate entity."
- Key point: Sometimes you can petition the appropriate party for permission to see restricted materials.

### E28

- Location: Notes on Copyright, Restrictions, and Unprocessed Collections, Unprocessed collections
- Quote: "If you know of an unprocessed collection you want to use, talk to the archival staff as far ahead of your visit as possible and inquire about use policies."
- Key point: If you want to use an unprocessed collection, contact the staff as early as possible and ask about use policies.

### E29

- Location: Visiting an Archives, Prioritize your requests
- Quote: "What are the materials that would be most helpful for you to view? Make plans to see those first and ask the archival staff for them promptly to ensure you have time to see them."
- Key point: Look at the most useful materials first, and request them early so you have time to see them.

### E30

- Location: Visiting an Archives, Take thorough citations
- Quote: "While you are working, make sure to take full citations for the materials you are viewing, including any unique identification assigned to the materials by the archives such as the call number, collection title, etc."
- Key point: As you work, record full citations, including the archive's unique identifiers such as call number and collection title.

### E31

- Location: Visiting an Archives, Take thorough citations
- Quote: "If you need to go back and reference something in those materials again, or if another researcher is later trying to track your sources from a published work, this will help the archival staff locate the materials."
- Key point: Full citations let you find things again, and let researchers who read your work trace the originals.

### E32

- Location: Visiting an Archives, Point out corrections
- Quote: "Mistakes or omissions sometimes occur in finding aids, websites, and descriptions of materials."
- Key point: Finding aids, websites, and descriptions of materials sometimes contain errors or omissions.

### E33

- Location: Appendix: Sample Annotated Finding Aid, Title Page annotation
- Quote: "“Bulk dates” refers to the date range where most of the materials in the collection originate."
- Key point: "Bulk dates" are the date range in which most of the collection's materials originate.

### E34

- Location: Appendix: Sample Annotated Finding Aid, Access and Use annotation and sample
- Quote: "If there are any restrictions placed on an archival collection, those should be noted here. Other information in this section includes how the archives received the collection, and copyright and citation notes." / "Cite as: [item], folder, box, Corinth Chamber of Commerce records, Corinth Historical Society."
- Key point: The "access and use" section of a finding aid gives restrictions, provenance, copyright, and the preferred citation; the sample citation is "item, folder, box, collection name, repository."

### E35

- Location: Typical Usage Guidelines in Archival Repositories, Request forms
- Quote: "Photocopies of unpublished materials provided for a researcher may require additional permissions before they are published."
- Key point: Photocopies of unpublished materials provided to a researcher may need additional permission before publication.
