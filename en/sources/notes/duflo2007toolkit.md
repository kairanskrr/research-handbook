# Duflo, Glennerster & Kremer 2007: Using Randomization in Development Economics Research: A Toolkit

- Source: `duflo2007toolkit`, [Original PDF (NBER)](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/t0333/t0333.pdf)
- Version: NBER Technical Working Paper 333 (December 2006), later published in the Handbook of Development Economics, vol. 4 (2007). The working paper PDF is free on the NBER website. Locations give section numbers.

### E1

- Location: §3 introduction
- Quote: "most of the projects involving randomization differ from this model in several important ways. They are in general conducted with implementation partners (governments, NGOs, or private companies) who are implementing real-world programs and are interested in finding out whether they work or how to improve them."
- Key point: Unlike clinical trials under lab conditions, most randomized studies in development economics are done with implementation partners (governments, NGOs, firms) and evaluate real programs.

### E2

- Location: §3.1 Partners
- Quote: "introducing randomization in real-world programs almost always requires working with a partner who is in charge of actually implementing the program."
- Key point: Introducing randomization into real-world programs almost always requires working with the partner who actually implements the program.

### E3

- Location: §3.1 Partners
- Quote: "When possible, working with governments offers several advantages. First, it can allow for much wider geographic scope. Second, the results may be more likely to feed into the policy process." / "However, NGOs and firms offer a much more flexible environment, where it can be easier for the researchers to monitor the implementation of the research design."
- Key point: Working with governments allows wider geographic scope and makes it more likely that results feed into policy; NGOs and firms are more flexible, and researchers can more easily monitor whether the design is carried out.

### E4

- Location: §3.3.1 Oversubscription Method
- Quote: "A natural opportunity for introducing randomization occurs when there are limited resources or implementation capacity and demand for a program or service exceeds supply. In this case, a natural and fair way to ration resources is to select those who will receive the program by lottery among eligible candidates."
- Key point: When resources are limited and demand exceeds supply, a lottery among eligible applicants is a natural and fair way to ration, and it introduces randomization.

### E5

- Location: §3.3.2 Randomized Order of Phase-In
- Quote: "Randomizing the order of phase-in can allow evaluation of program effects in contexts where it is not acceptable for some groups or individuals to receive no support."
- Key point: Randomizing the order of phase-in allows evaluation where it is not acceptable for anyone to go without support.

### E6

- Location: §3.3.2 Randomized Order of Phase-In
- Quote: "When planning a phase-in design, the time between phases should be sufficient to encompass any treatment lag." / "Randomized phase-in becomes problematic when the comparison group is affected by the expectation of future treatment."
- Key point: In a phase-in design, the time between phases must be longer than the treatment lag; if the comparison group changes its behavior because it expects to be treated later, the design has a problem.

### E7

- Location: §3.3.3 Within-Group Randomization
- Quote: "The drawback of such designs is that they increase the likelihood that the comparison group is contaminated."
- Key point: Randomizing within a unit (e.g., different grades in the same school) makes contamination of the comparison group more likely.

### E8

- Location: §3.3.4 Encouragement Designs
- Quote: "Rather than randomize over the treatment itself, researchers randomly assign subjects an encouragement to receive the treatment."
- Key point: An encouragement design randomizes not the treatment itself but an encouragement to take it up.

### E9

- Location: §4.2 Grouped Errors
- Quote: "for a given sample size, an increase in the number of individuals sampled per cluster increases the precision much less than increasing the number of clusters being randomized."
- Key point: For a given total sample size, adding more individuals per cluster increases precision much less than adding more randomized clusters.

### E10

- Location: §4.6 Power calculations in practice
- Quote: "despite all the precision of these formulas, power calculations involve substantial guess work in practice." / "The best way to obtain guesses about these parameters is in general to use previously collected data, ideally from the same country or region."
- Key point: However precise the formulas, power calculations involve a lot of guesswork in practice; the parameters are best estimated from existing data, ideally from the same country or region.

### E11

- Location: §4.6 Power calculations in practice
- Quote: "As a rule of thumb for a policy intervention evaluation, this should be the smallest effect size that is large enough such that the intervention would be cost effective if it were to be scaled up."
- Key point: When evaluating a policy intervention, the effect size to detect can be set to the smallest effect that would still make the intervention cost-effective if scaled up.

### E12

- Location: §4.6 Power calculations in practice
- Quote: "Some argue that since the study has little chance to deliver conclusive results, it is not worthwhile to conduct it." / "The greatest incremental precision on a given question comes from the first few observations. Moreover, results from several low power studies can be combined in a meta-analysis which will have more power."
- Key point: Whether low-powered studies are worth doing is debated: some say a study unlikely to give conclusive results is not worthwhile; the authors note that the first few studies on a question add the most precision, and several low-powered studies can be combined in a meta-analysis.

### E13

- Location: §4.4 Control Variables
- Quote: "Note that controlling for covariates affected by the treatment would bias the estimate of the treatment effect by capturing part of its impact." / "Note that this choice must in principle be specified in advance to avoid the risk of specification searching."
- Key point: Controlling for covariates affected by the treatment biases the effect estimate; which covariates to control for should in principle be specified in advance to avoid specification searching.

### E14

- Location: §4.5 Stratification
- Quote: "The randomization ensures that treatment and control groups will be similar in expectation. But stratification is used to ensure that along important observable dimensions this is also true in practice in the sample."
- Key point: Randomization makes the groups similar in expectation; stratification ensures they are also similar in the actual sample on important observable characteristics.

### E15

- Location: §5.1 Level of Randomization
- Quote: "First, as discussed in section 4.2, the larger the groups that are randomized, the larger the total sample size needed to achieve a given power." / "Second, however, spillovers from treatment to comparison groups can bias the estimation of treatment effects. In such case, the randomization should occur at a level that captures these effects."
- Key point: The larger the randomized units, the larger the total sample needed; but if the treatment group can affect the comparison group, randomize at a level that contains these spillovers.

### E16

- Location: §5.1 Level of Randomization
- Quote: "It is much easier for a research team to ensure that villages are being treated according to an initial random assignment than to monitor individuals."
- Key point: Randomizing by village makes compliance easier to ensure than randomizing individuals: the research team can monitor whether villages are treated as assigned far more easily than it can monitor individuals.

### E17

- Location: §5.3.1 Conducting Baseline Surveys
- Quote: "In principle, randomization renders baseline surveys unnecessary, since it ensures the treatment and comparison groups are similar in expectation." / "When the intervention is expensive and data collection is relatively cheap, conducting a baseline will save money. When the intervention is cheap but data collection is expensive, it may be more cost effective to run a larger experiment without conducting a baseline."
- Key point: In principle, randomization makes a baseline survey unnecessary; when the intervention is expensive and surveys are cheap, a baseline saves money, but when the intervention is cheap and surveys are expensive, skipping the baseline and enlarging the sample may be better.

### E18

- Location: §5.3.1 Conducting Baseline Surveys
- Quote: "Second, a baseline survey provides an opportunity to check that the randomization was conducted appropriately. Third, collecting baseline data offers an opportunity to test and refine data collection procedures."
- Key point: A baseline survey also lets you check that randomization was done correctly and test and refine data collection procedures.

### E19

- Location: §5.3.1 Conducting Baseline Surveys
- Quote: "The alternative strategy of collecting "pre-intervention data" retrospectively in the postsurvey will usually be unacceptable, because even if the program does not affect those variables it may well affect recall of those variables."
- Key point: Collecting "pre-intervention data" retrospectively in the follow-up survey is usually unacceptable, because the program may affect how people recall those variables.

### E20

- Location: §5.3.2 Using Administrative Data
- Quote: "However, this could introduce biases as a difference in measured outcomes between treatment and comparison areas could reflect different data collection methodologies."
- Key point: If the treatment group is measured with program data and the comparison group with a separate survey, differences in outcomes may just reflect different data collection methods.

### E21

- Location: §6.2 Partial Compliance
- Quote: "One must compare all those initially allocated to the treatment group to all those initially randomized to the comparison group, whatever their actual behavior and their actual treatment status. The analysis cannot exclude subjects or cut the sample according to behavior that may have been affected by the random assignment."
- Key point: You must compare everyone originally assigned to treatment with everyone originally assigned to comparison, whatever treatment they actually received; you cannot drop or split the sample by behavior that the assignment may have affected.

### E22

- Location: §6.2.1 From Intention To Treat to Average Treatment Effects
- Quote: "In many contexts, the intention-to-treat estimate is actually a parameter of interest."
- Key point: In many contexts, the intention-to-treat effect is itself the quantity of interest (e.g., because some people will never take up a policy once it is rolled out).

### E23

- Location: §6.2.1 From Intention To Treat to Average Treatment Effects
- Quote: "Under the monotonicity and the independence assumptions, the Wald estimator gives us the effect of the treatment on those whose treatment status was affected by the instrument, which is known as the local average treatment effect (LATE)"
- Key point: Under the monotonicity and independence assumptions, using random assignment as an instrument gives the effect on those whose take-up was changed by the assignment: the local average treatment effect.

### E24

- Location: §6.2.1 From Intention To Treat to Average Treatment Effects
- Quote: "In this case, the Wald estimate is the effect of the treatment on the treated."
- Key point: When no one in the comparison group receives the treatment, this estimate is the effect of the treatment on the treated.

### E25

- Location: §6.2.2 When is IV Not Appropriate
- Quote: "This, however, is inappropriate, because children in treatment schools who were not sent to the balsakhi may have benefited from the fact that their class is now smaller for most of the day, and their weakest peers have left the classrooms."
- Key point: Children in treatment schools who were not tutored directly may also benefit (smaller classes, weakest peers taken out), so scaling up the intention-to-treat effect by the take-up rate is inappropriate here.

### E26

- Location: §6.3 Externalities
- Quote: "If they are local, randomization at the group level can allow for estimation of the total program effect on the group. If externalities do not operate across groups, group-level randomization is sufficient to identify overall treatment effects. It cannot, however, decompose the direct and spillover effects."
- Key point: If spillovers are local and do not cross groups, randomizing by group can estimate the total program effect, but it cannot separate direct effects from spillovers.

### E27

- Location: §6.3 Externalities
- Quote: "A first technique is to purposefully vary the level of exposure to a treatment within a group."
- Key point: One way to measure spillovers is to deliberately vary the share of a group that receives the treatment.

### E28

- Location: §6.4 Attrition
- Quote: "Random attrition will only reduce a study's statistical power; however, attrition that is correlated with the treatment being evaluated may bias estimates."
- Key point: Random attrition only reduces statistical power; attrition correlated with the treatment can bias estimates.

### E29

- Location: §6.4 Attrition
- Quote: "Even if attrition rates are similar in treatment and comparison groups, it remains possible that the attritors were selected differently in the treatment and comparison groups."
- Key point: Even when attrition rates are similar in both groups, the people who drop out may differ between the groups.

### E30

- Location: §6.4 Attrition
- Quote: "This makes attrition a very difficult problem to solve ex post and that implies managing attrition during the data collection process is essential." / "it is important to collect good information in the baseline on how to find them later on (for example the names of neighbors and relatives that can be interviewed if the respondent cannot be found)."
- Key point: Attrition is very hard to fix after the fact, so it must be managed during data collection; collect information at baseline for finding people later (such as names of neighbors and relatives).

### E31

- Location: §6.4 Attrition
- Quote: "A first step in the analysis of an evaluation must always be to report attrition levels in the treatment and comparison groups and to compare attritors with non-attritors using baseline data"
- Key point: The first step of the analysis must be to report attrition rates in each group and compare those who left with those who stayed, using baseline data.

### E32

- Location: §7.1 Grouped Data
- Quote: "show the cluster-correlated Huber-White estimator performs poorly when the number of clusters is small (less than 50), leading to over-rejection of the null hypothesis of no effect." / "When the number of clusters is small, hypothesis tests can also be generated using randomization inference"
- Key point: Simulation studies cited by the authors show that with fewer than 50 clusters, the usual cluster-robust standard errors perform poorly and over-reject the null hypothesis of no effect; with few clusters, randomization inference can be used for testing.

### E33

- Location: §7.2 Multiple Outcomes
- Quote: "all the variables collected in a prospective evaluation were, presumably, collected because they were considered potential outcome variables. All should therefore be reported whether they are significant or not."
- Key point: Every variable collected in a prospective evaluation was collected as a potential outcome, so all should be reported, significant or not.

### E34

- Location: §7.3 Subgroups
- Quote: "Ideally, researchers should know when designing the evaluation protocol, either through a priori reasoning or through knowledge gained from other studies, which of the possible subgroups should be investigated separately and make this decision clear ex ante."
- Key point: Ideally, decide when designing the protocol, based on theory or earlier studies, which subgroups to analyze separately, and specify them in advance.
