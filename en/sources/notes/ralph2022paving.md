# Ralph & Baltes 2022: Paving the Way for Mature Secondary Research: The Seven Types of Literature Review

- Source: `ralph2022paving`, [arXiv:2205.01163](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.01163)
- Version: arXiv v2 (ESEC/FSE 2022).

### E1

- Location: §2.1 Ad Hoc Reviews
- Quote: "An ad hoc literature review is simply a discussion of some literature, as contained in most research papers as part of a background or related work section. [...] Ad hoc reviews use purposive sampling; that is, researchers purposefully select papers or studies that are useful, relevant, or support their arguments."
- Key point: An ad hoc literature review is simply a discussion of some literature, as in the background or related work section of most papers. The papers are chosen purposively: useful, relevant, or supporting the authors' views.

### E2

- Location: §2.1 Ad Hoc Reviews
- Quote: "Ad hoc reviews are often appropriate, for example, to support theory development, identify promising research topics, or prepare for a comprehensive exam. However, they suffer from important limitations: their unsystematic nature introduces sampling bias and defies replication. This may lead to cherry-picking of evidence supporting authors' arguments. Therefore, ad hoc reviews are inappropriate for supporting empirical statements such as 'x causes y' [...]"
- Key point: Ad hoc reviews suit theory development, finding promising topics, preparing for a comprehensive exam, and so on; but they suffer from sampling bias, cannot be replicated, and invite cherry-picking, so they are unsuitable for supporting empirical conclusions such as "X causes Y."

### E3

- Location: §2.2.5 Scoping Reviews
- Quote: "What is often called a 'systematic mapping study' in SE is usually called a scoping review elsewhere [...]. Scoping reviews are often primarily descriptive. They count the number of studies on a topic."
- Key point: What software engineering calls a "systematic mapping study" is usually called a scoping review elsewhere (e.g. in medicine); scoping reviews are mainly descriptive and count studies.

### E4

- Location: §2.2.5 Scoping Reviews
- Quote: "Scoping reviews include a similar search but typically do not provide sufficient quantitative synthesis to answer important empirical questions. Therefore, scoping reviews do not inform evidence-based practice like meta-analyses and case surveys do."
- Key point: The authors argue that scoping reviews (mapping studies) usually lack enough synthesis to answer important empirical questions.

### E5

- Location: §3.1 Recommendations
- Quote: "Software engineering needs more meta-analysis, case surveys, and meta-synthesis, but fewer scoping and rapid reviews. [...] predominately descriptive scoping reviews are essentially works-in-progress. They belong in workshops, poster sessions and short paper tracks. [...] If the literature does support some kind of synthesis, the scoping review is like a pilot study for a more ambitious systematic review."
- Key point: The authors call for more meta-analysis, case surveys, and meta-synthesis, and fewer scoping and rapid reviews. Mainly descriptive scoping reviews are "essentially works-in-progress" that belong in workshops, posters, or short papers; if the literature supports synthesis, a scoping review is like a pilot for a more complete systematic review.

### E6

- Location: §2.2.3 Case Survey (aka Case Meta-analysis)
- Quote: "A case survey’s primary studies are (typically qualitative) case studies in the broadest sense (i.e., a scholarly account of some events). Experience reports and grey literature may or may not be included, depending on the study’s purposes."
- Key point: A case survey's primary studies are case studies in the broadest sense, i.e. scholarly accounts of some events; whether experience reports and grey literature (material not published through formal channels) are included depends on the study's purpose.

### E7

- Location: §2.2.3
- Quote: "Unlike meta-synthesis, however, a case survey transforms qualitative accounts into a quantitative dataset that supports null-hypothesis testing. Case surveys share the philosophy of meta-analysis (positivism), not meta-synthesis (constructivism). They typically begin with a priori hypotheses and an a priori coding scheme."
- Key point: Unlike meta-synthesis, a case survey transforms qualitative accounts into a quantitative dataset that supports null-hypothesis testing; it shares the philosophy of meta-analysis (positivism) and usually starts from a priori hypotheses and a coding scheme.

### E8

- Location: §2.2.3
- Quote: "The researcher reads each case and extracts data into the coding scheme, often using simple dichotomous variables like ‘did the team have retrospective meetings? [yes/no]’" / "The resulting dataset is often too sparse for regression modeling, so researchers use simple bivariate correlations to test hypotheses"
- Key point: The researcher reads each case and extracts data into the coding scheme, often as simple yes/no variables (e.g. "did the team have retrospective meetings?"); the resulting dataset is often too sparse for regression modeling, so hypotheses are usually tested with simple bivariate correlations.

### E9

- Location: §2.2.3
- Quote: "Now case meta-analysis is widely used in management and information systems" / "SE case surveys remain rare." / "Case surveys have great potential in SE because case studies are so common."
- Key point: Case surveys are widely used in management and information systems but remain rare in software engineering; because case studies are so common there, the authors see great potential.

### E10

- Location: §3 Discussion
- Quote: "With their sparse data sets, case surveys need many case studies."
- Key point: Because their data are sparse, case surveys need many case studies.

### E11

- Location: §3 Discussion and §3.1 Recommendations
- Quote: "Meta-analysis and case surveys synthesize the results of many studies to answer specific empirical (often causal) questions about the world." / "Software engineering needs more meta-analysis, case surveys, and meta-synthesis, but fewer scoping and rapid reviews."
- Key point: Meta-analysis and case surveys synthesize the results of many studies to answer specific empirical (often causal) questions about the world; the authors call for more meta-analysis, case surveys, and meta-synthesis in software engineering.

### E12

- Location: §2.2.4 Critical Reviews
- Quote: "A critical review analyzes a sample of primary studies to support an argument or critique." / "critical reviews in software engineering often investigate methodological topics"
- Key point: A critical review analyzes a sample of primary studies to support an argument or critique; in software engineering, critical reviews often address methodological topics.

### E13

- Location: §2.2.4
- Quote: "Stol et al. [31]’s critical review of the use of grounded theory in SE criticizes method slurring; that is, claiming to have used a research methodology that was not actually used to create illusory legitimacy."
- Key point: Example: Stol et al. criticize "method slurring" in software engineering, i.e. claiming to have used a research method that was not actually used, to create illusory legitimacy.

### E14

- Location: §2.2.4
- Quote: "Critical reviews are not for supporting evidence-based practice, or summarizing the evidence for a theory. Critical reviews are part of the meta-scientific discourse; i.e., the conversations a scientific community has internally about how it conducts research."
- Key point: Critical reviews are not for supporting evidence-based practice or summarizing the evidence for a theory; they are part of meta-scientific discourse, the conversation a scientific community has about how it does research.

### E15

- Location: §2.2.4
- Quote: "Second, for many critical reviews, including all relevant primary studies is impossible and unnecessary." / "a random sample of papers from a selection of top journals and conferences is sufficient because critical reviews do not assess causal claims"
- Key point: For many critical reviews, including every relevant study is impossible and unnecessary; a random sample from selected top journals and conferences is enough, because they do not assess causal claims and publication bias is irrelevant.

### E16

- Location: §2.2.4
- Quote: "Analysis performed within a critical review can be quantitative, qualitative or both. However, critical reviews typically adopt a critical stance; that is, they go beyond mere description and offer specific critiques of the work being reviewed."
- Key point: Analysis in a critical review can be quantitative, qualitative, or both, but it usually takes a critical stance: going beyond description to offer specific critiques of the work examined.

### E17

- Location: §3 Discussion
- Quote: "they focus on an empirical topic (e.g. cognitive biases in SE); not a meta-scientific topic (e.g. construct validity); therefore, they are not configured, from the outset, to deliver useful metascientific critique."
- Key point: Scoping reviews (mapping studies) focus on an empirical topic (e.g. cognitive biases in software engineering) rather than a meta-scientific one (e.g. construct validity), so from the outset they are not set up to deliver meta-scientific critique.

### E18

- Location: §3.1 Recommendations
- Quote: "Meta-science in SE has been hamstrung by reviewers and venues insisting that every paper reports an empirical study, regardless of whether its research question is empirical."
- Key point: The authors argue that meta-science in software engineering has been hamstrung by reviewers and venues insisting that every paper report an empirical study, whether or not its research question is empirical.

### E19

- Location: §3.1 Recommendations
- Quote: "First, when elucidating a ubiquitous problem (e.g. poor construct validity) citing specific examples of papers making that mistake is unnecessary. The credibility gained is not worth the costs to community cohesion, interpersonal relationships, and future collaborations. Authors should explain this reasoning and reviewers should accept this compromise."
- Key point: When elucidating a ubiquitous problem, citing specific papers that make the mistake is unnecessary: the credibility gained is not worth the cost to community cohesion, relationships, and future collaboration; authors should explain this choice and reviewers should accept it.

### E20

- Location: §3.1 Recommendations
- Quote: "Second, reviewers should not misapply the “find ALL relevant studies” or “exclude low quality studies” criteria to critical reviews."
- Key point: Reviewers should not misapply criteria such as "find all relevant studies" or "exclude low-quality studies" to critical reviews.

### E21

- Location: §3.2 Limitations
- Quote: "This is not an empirical paper. It is meta-science: part of our community’s internal discourse about what we are doing, why, and how to do it."
- Key point: The authors state that their paper is not empirical but meta-science: part of the community's internal discourse about what we are doing, why, and how to do it.
