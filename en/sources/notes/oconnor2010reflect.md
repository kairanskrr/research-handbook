# O'Connor et al. 2010: The REFLECT statement: Methods and processes of creating reporting guidelines for randomized controlled trials for livestock and food safety

- Source: `oconnor2010reflect`, [original PDF](https://www.aasv.org/shap/issues/v18n1/v18n1p18.pdf)
- Version: Journal of Swine Health and Production 18(1):18–26, 2010. Full text freely available on the website of the American Association of Swine Veterinarians (AASV). The same statement was published simultaneously in four other journals. Locations give section headings.

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "The conduct of randomized controlled trials in livestock with production, health, and food-safety outcomes presents unique challenges that may not be adequately reported in trial reports."
- Key point: Randomized controlled trials in livestock with production, health, and food-safety outcomes present unique challenges that may be inadequately reported.

### E2

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "The consensus meeting resulted in the production of the REFLECT (Reporting Guidelines for Randomized Controlled Trials) statement for livestock and food safety (LFS) and 22-item checklist. Fourteen items were modified from the CONSORT checklist, and an additional sub-item was proposed to address challenge trials."
- Key point: The consensus meeting produced the REFLECT statement for livestock and food safety and a 22-item checklist; 14 items were adapted from the CONSORT checklist for human trials, and one sub-item was added specifically for challenge trials.

### E3

- Location: Introduction (first part of the main text)
- Quote: "The differences include two types of "participants" (the animals' owners-managers who consent to participation in a trial, and the animals who are the actual study subjects), the common use of clustered study designs, the use of a deliberate challenge to animals with infectious agents in some trials (aka challenge trials), and non-clinical outcomes (eg, production indices). These differences make the direct use of the CONSORT statement challenging."
- Key point: How livestock trials differ from human trials: there are two types of "participants" (the animals' owners or managers, who consent to the trial, and the animals actually studied); cluster designs are common; some trials deliberately infect animals with pathogens (challenge trials); and outcomes are often non-clinical (e.g., production measures). These differences make it hard to apply CONSORT directly.

### E4

- Location: Results
- Quote: "There is a need for clear identification of the unit of allocation of the intervention and the unit of assessment and inference. Interventions can be allocated at any level of the organizational structure and the outcome assessed at the same or lower level."
- Key point: Clearly identify the unit to which the intervention is allocated and the unit of assessment and inference. Interventions can be allocated at any level of the organizational structure, with outcomes measured at the same or a lower level.

### E5

- Location: Results
- Quote: "In livestock trials, non-independence of observations can arise because animals are often housed and managed in groups. Animals housed together have something more in common than animals housed separately, as they share the same microclimate, ration, health-management procedures, etc."
- Key point: Livestock are often housed and managed in groups; animals in the same pen share microclimate, diet, health management, and so on, making them more alike, so observations are not independent.

### E6

- Location: Results
- Quote: "Failure to properly account for non-independence of the data in the statistical analysis results in a violation of the association of independence that underlies many statistical procedures."
- Key point: A statistical analysis that does not account for non-independence of the data violates the assumption of independence that many statistical methods rely on.

### E7

- Location: Results
- Quote: "For example, swine may be studied within pens, within barns, within sites, and within production companies."
- Key point: Example: swine can be organized in nested levels: pens, barns, sites, and production companies.

### E8

- Location: Results
- Quote: "Livestock trials with production, health, and food-safety outcomes are frequently conducted in research settings in which experimental challenge of trial animals (often with pathogenic organisms) is under the control of the researcher."
- Key point: These livestock trials are often run in research facilities where the investigators control experimental challenge of the study animals (usually with pathogens).

### E9

- Location: Results
- Quote: "For example, a study may allocate udder halves to receive the treatment, therefore the allocation unit is the udder half; however, the outcome may be measured on the individual teat, ie, the outcome unit."
- Key point: Example: the treatment is allocated to udder halves (the allocation unit), but the outcome is measured on individual teats (the outcome unit).

### E10

- Location: Discussion
- Quote: "As an example of the impact of animal housing, a feedlot veterinarian may expect a different outcome from a vaccine allocated to individual animals, compared to group-level application. Similarly, a challenge study that used 100 times the normal dose of Salmonella to induce Salmonella shedding may have questionable external validity."
- Key point: A vaccine allocated to individual animals may give a different outcome from one given to whole groups; and a Salmonella challenge at 100 times the normal dose makes it doubtful whether conclusions generalize to real conditions.

### E11

- Location: Discussion
- Quote: "Although the REFLECT statement directly applies to reporting of studies, it may also be consulted and useful in the design and conduct stages of a trial."
- Key point: REFLECT applies directly to reporting, but it can also be consulted in the design and conduct stages.

### E12

- Location: Introduction (first part of the main text)
- Quote: "The issue of inferior quality of veterinary RCT reports was first raised in editorials and commentaries in veterinary journals in the early to mid-1990's." / "Recently, several systematic reviews of therapeutic, preventive, and food-safety trials in livestock species have highlighted the need for better reporting."
- Key point: The poor quality of veterinary RCT reports was first raised in editorials in veterinary journals in the early to mid-1990s; later, several systematic reviews of livestock treatment, prevention, and food-safety trials also found that reporting needed to improve.

### E13

- Location: Table 3 (definitions)
- Quote: "The owner-manager of the study facility who consented to participate in the trial."
- Key point: In REFLECT, "participant" means specifically the owner or manager of the study facility who consented to participate in the trial.

### E14

- Location: footnote on the first page (Note)
- Quote: "The REFLECT statement is also published in Journal of Food Protection, Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine, Zoonoses and Public Health, Preventive Veterinary Medicine, and Journal of Swine Health and Production."
- Key point: The REFLECT statement was published simultaneously in five journals.

### E15

- Location: Results
- Quote: "In addition, the use of "participant" in the original CONSORT statement was limited to refer only to animals' owners-managers, who consent to participation in the trial. The term "study unit" was preferred for the units within the study."
- Key point: REFLECT restricts CONSORT's "participant" to the animals' owners or managers who consent to participate; the animals and other units studied are called "study units."

### E16

- Location: Table 2, Item 3 (Participants)
- Quote: "Eligibility criteria for owner/managers and study units at each level of the organizational structure, and the settings and locations where the data were collected."
- Key point: Report the eligibility criteria for owners or managers and for study units at each level, and the settings and locations where data were collected.
