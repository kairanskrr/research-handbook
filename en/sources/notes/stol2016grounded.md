# Stol, Ralph & Fitzgerald 2016: Grounded Theory in Software Engineering Research: A Critical Review and Guidelines

- Source: `stol2016grounded`, [original (author version in the University of Limerick institutional repository)](https://hdl.handle.net/10344/5396)
- Version: ICSE 2016 paper, pp. 120-131, DOI 10.1145/2884781.2884833. Institutional repository version, licensed CC BY-NC-SA 1.0.

### E1

- Location: §1 Introduction
- Quote: "The goal of GT is to generate theory rather than test or validate existing theory. GT is suitable for investigating questions such as what's going on here?"
- Key point: The goal of grounded theory is to generate theory, not to test existing theory; it suits questions like "what is actually going on here."

### E2

- Location: §2.1 Key Components of Grounded Theory
- Quote: "Grounded Theory refers to a method of inductively generating theory from data" / "GT studies often focus on unstructured text (e.g. interview transcripts, documents, field notes); however, they may also include structured text, diagrams and images, and even quantitative data"
- Key point: Grounded theory is a method for inductively generating theory from data; the data are mostly unstructured text such as interview transcripts, documents, and field notes, but can also include structured text, diagrams, and even quantitative data.

### E3

- Location: §2.3 Versions of Grounded Theory
- Quote: "It is now widely acknowledged that there are at least three main streams of GT" / "Glaser's GT (classic or Glaserian GT); Strauss and Corbin's GT (Straussian GT); and Charmaz's constructivist GT."
- Key point: There are at least three main schools of grounded theory: Glaser's classic grounded theory, Strauss and Corbin's grounded theory, and Charmaz's constructivist grounded theory.

### E4

- Location: §2.3 Versions of Grounded Theory
- Quote: "Classic GT can be characterized as having a strong focus on emergence (of research questions, of codes, of theory), whereas Straussian GT meticulously suggests a set of 'mini-steps.'" / "Constructivist GT emphasizes understanding and acknowledges that data, interpretations, and resulting theory depend on the researcher's view."
- Key point: Classic grounded theory stresses "emergence" (the research question, codes, and theory all emerge from the data); the Straussian version prescribes a series of fine-grained steps; the constructivist version stresses understanding and acknowledges that both the interpretation of data and the theory depend on the researcher's perspective.

### E5

- Location: Table 1, Research question row
- Quote: "The researcher starts with an 'area of interest.'" / "Defining a RQ a priori is considered 'forcing'" / "Research question may be defined upfront, derived from the literature or suggested by a colleague; RQ is often broad and open-ended." / "Research begins with "initial research questions," which evolve throughout the study"
- Key point: The classic version starts from an "area of interest," and fixing a research question in advance is seen as "forcing"; the Straussian version allows a broad, open question to be set in advance; the constructivist version starts from an "initial research question" that evolves during the study.

### E6

- Location: Table 1, Role of the literature row
- Quote: "An extensive literature review should be delayed until after the theory is emerging to prevent the influence of existing concepts on the emerging theory." / "The literature may be consulted throughout the process, as concepts from the literature may be used if applicable" / "Acknowledges not only Glaser's reasons for delaying the literature review but also the impracticality of this strategy. Charmaz highlights the need to tailor a literature review to fit the purpose of the GT study"
- Key point: The classic version holds that a comprehensive literature review should be done only after the theory has emerged; the Straussian version allows the literature to be consulted throughout; the constructivist version acknowledges the reasons for delaying the literature review but also that this is unrealistic, and advocates tailoring the literature review to the study's purpose.

### E7

- Location: §2.1, Limit exposure to literature
- Quote: "A major reason to limit study of the literature is to prevent the researcher from testing existing theories, or thinking in terms of established concepts."
- Key point: The main reason for limiting reading of the literature is to keep researchers from ending up testing existing theory or thinking about the problem in terms of ready-made concepts.

### E8

- Location: Table 1, Coding procedures row (constructivist grounded theory)
- Quote: "Initial coding: examining data word-by-word, line-by-line or incident-by-incident to make sense of the text without injecting the researcher's assumptions, biases, motivations." / "Focused coding: selecting categories from the most frequent or important codes, and using them to categorize the data; does not require a single core category or variable." / "Theoretical coding: specifying the relationship between categories to integrate them into a cohesive theory."
- Key point: Coding in constructivist grounded theory has three steps: initial coding (making sense of the data word by word, line by line, or incident by incident, without importing the researcher's assumptions); focused coding (selecting categories from the most frequent or most significant codes and using them to sort the data, without requiring a single core category); and theoretical coding (specifying how categories relate to one another and integrating them into a coherent theory).

### E9

- Location: Table 1, Coding procedures row (classic and Straussian versions)
- Quote: "Open coding: 'fracturing' of the data" / "Open coding: generation of 'categories' and how they vary dimensionally." / "Selective coding: delimiting coding to only those variables that relate to one (or in some cases, several) core variables to establish a parsimonious theory." / "Axial coding: putting back data in new ways after open coding by identifying relationships between categories; this is effectively Glaser's theoretical coding." / "Selective coding: deciding on the central category that all major categories can link to"
- Key point: Both schools start with open coding. In the classic version, selective coding keeps only codes related to the core variable; the Straussian version has "axial coding" (identifying relationships among categories, roughly equivalent to Glaser's theoretical coding), and its selective coding means choosing a central category to which all major categories can be connected. The two schools use the same terms differently.

### E10

- Location: §2.1, Immediate and continuous data analysis / Theoretical sampling
- Quote: "The researcher begins analyzing data immediately and does not finish collecting data before beginning analysis—data collection and analysis are simultaneous" / "The researcher identifies further data sources based on gaps in the emerging theory or to further explore unsaturated concepts."
- Key point: Researchers start analyzing as soon as they have data, without waiting for data collection to finish; collection and analysis proceed together. Theoretical sampling: deciding what data to seek next based on gaps in the emerging theory, or to further explore concepts that are not yet saturated.

### E11

- Location: §2.1, Coding
- Quote: "The researcher does not classify data into a preconceived coding scheme, or infer categories from logically deduced hypotheses"
- Key point: Coding does not sort data into a predefined coding scheme, nor derive categories from deductively derived hypotheses.

### E12

- Location: §2.1, Memoing
- Quote: "The researcher writes memos (e.g. notes, diagrams, sketches) to elaborate categories as they emerge, describe preliminary properties and relationships between categories, and identify gaps" / "if the researcher skips this stage, he is not doing grounded theory"
- Key point: Researchers write memos (notes, diagrams, sketches) to flesh out emerging categories, describe their tentative properties and relationships, and identify gaps. Glaser holds that skipping this step means it is not grounded theory.

### E13

- Location: §2.1, Constant comparison
- Quote: "From the start of the study, the researcher constantly compares data, memos, codes and categories"
- Key point: From the start of the study, the researcher constantly compares data, memos, codes, and categories.

### E14

- Location: §2.1, Theoretical saturation
- Quote: "Theoretical saturation refers to the point at which a theory's components are well supported and new data is no longer triggering revisions or reinterpretations of the theory"
- Key point: Theoretical saturation means every part of the theory is well supported, and new data no longer lead to changes or reinterpretation of the theory.

### E15

- Location: §2.1, Cohesive theory
- Quote: "The researcher attempts to move beyond superficial categories and develop a cohesive theory of the studied phenomenon."
- Key point: Researchers must go beyond surface-level categories and develop a coherent theory of the phenomenon under study.

### E16

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "We carefully and systematically selected 98 articles that mention GT, of which 52 explicitly claim to use GT, with the other 46 using GT techniques only. Only 16 articles provide detailed accounts of their research procedures."
- Key point: Of 98 software engineering journal papers that mention grounded theory, 52 explicitly claim to use grounded theory and 46 only use some of its techniques; only 16 describe their research process in detail.

### E17

- Location: §4.4 Few "GT" Studies Generate Theory
- Quote: "Eight articles presented contributions that were clearly cohesive theories consisting of constructs and relationships" / "Together these roles provide an explanation for the "social" process of selforganization in agile teams, and as such they go beyond a mere taxonomy of roles." / "We observe that studies that produce a 'set of themes' rather than a theory tend only to borrow discrete practices from GT—what we call grounded theory à la carte."
- Key point: Of the 52 papers, only 8 explicitly present a coherent theory made of concepts and relationships. The six roles proposed by Hoda et al. explain how agile teams self-organize, and so go beyond a mere taxonomy of roles. Studies that produce only "a set of themes" often borrow grounded theory practices piecemeal, which the authors call "à la carte grounded theory."

### E18

- Location: §4.2 Many Studies Present Little to No Detail
- Quote: "One article claimed theoretical sampling, but instead of collecting additional data to further investigate as of yet unsaturated concepts in the emerging theory, a number of case companies were selected seemingly a priori"
- Key point: Some papers claim to have done theoretical sampling when in fact they selected a few companies in advance, rather than gathering more data to fill out concepts that were not yet saturated.

### E19

- Location: §5.1 Method Slurring
- Quote: "If a study does not involve simultaneous data collection and analysis, constant comparison, coding, memoing and theory development, it is not a grounded theory study." / "base analysis on seed categories or preconceived analytical frameworks, they are not using grounded theory."
- Key point: A study without concurrent data collection and analysis, constant comparison, coding, memoing, and theory development is not a grounded theory study; nor is one that first sorts data according to existing theory or starts analysis from "seed categories" or a predefined framework.

### E20

- Location: §5.2 Considerations for Conducting and Reporting Grounded Theory
- Quote: "Research cannot be reconstructed as GT at write-up." / "researchers should describe their implementation of GT, not GT in principle." / "If techniques have been borrowed from the grounded theory literature, researchers should simply state that those techniques have been used without discussing GT."
- Key point: A study cannot be "repackaged" as grounded theory only when the paper is being written; describe what you specifically did rather than explaining the principles of grounded theory; if you only borrowed techniques, say which techniques you used and do not invoke grounded theory.

### E21

- Location: §5.2
- Quote: "Anyone considering a GT study should read several books before even deciding whether GT is the right method, let alone beginning data collection." / "Good introductions are available for classic GT, Straussian GT, and constructivist GT."
- Key point: People considering grounded theory should read several of the monographs before deciding whether to adopt it, let alone before starting data collection. Each of the three schools has its own introductory works (the paper cites Glaser 1978 and Glaser & Strauss 1967, Corbin & Strauss 2015, and Charmaz 2014 respectively).

### E22

- Location: §5.1 Method Slurring
- Quote: "Grounded Theory is not the only valid method of either analyzing predominately qualitative data or generating theories."
- Key point: Grounded theory is not the only legitimate method for analyzing qualitative data or generating theory.

### E23

- Location: §5.3 Challenges in Doing Grounded Theory Research in Software Engineering
- Quote: "one strategy is to choose an explicit primary data source (e.g. interviews) and theoretically sample from the remaining data based on leads arising from the primary data source." / "Participants' post-hoc reconstructions of how and why they performed certain actions are less reliable than, for example, their accounts of their current frustrations or enduring values." / "Source code, commit logs, project management data and direct observation allow the researcher to triangulate many interviewee claims."
- Key point: Interviewees' after-the-fact reconstructions of why and how they did something are less reliable than their statements about current concerns or long-held values. Software settings produce a lot of varied data; one approach is to pick a primary data source (such as interviews) and then theoretically sample from the remaining data following the leads it provides. Source code, commit records, project management data, and direct observation can be used to check what interviewees say.

### E24

- Location: Figure 5, Grounded Theory Data Collection and Analysis
- Quote: "Describe your theoretical sampling with examples." / "When did you stop collecting data? Describe how theoretical saturation became apparent."
- Key point: When reporting, give examples of how theoretical sampling was done, and explain when data collection stopped and how you judged that theoretical saturation had been reached.

### E25

- Location: §1 Introduction; §6 Conclusion
- Quote: "There is growing awareness that Software Engineering (SE) research must consider social, cultural and human aspects of software construction" / "We believe that grounded theory offers a highly suitable methodology to address social, cultural and human aspects in software engineering"
- Key point: Software engineering research increasingly attends to the social, cultural, and human aspects of software development; the authors consider grounded theory well suited to studying these aspects.

### E26

- Location: §4.3 Many Studies Ignore GT Variants
- Quote: "Of the 52 articles that claim to use grounded theory, 39 did not acknowledge the existence of different variants." / "Two articles claim or cite classic GT but use axial coding, a Straussian practice"
- Key point: Of the 52 papers claiming to use grounded theory, 39 do not mention that different schools exist; two claim to use the classic version yet use Straussian axial coding.

### E27

- Location: §4.1 Grounded Theory "Use" is Ambiguous
- Quote: "In three cases, the authors developed a set of preliminary categories, which were then combined with a "grounded theory approach"—starting with a classification from the literature is highly suspect"
- Key point: Three papers first set a list of preliminary categories and then "combined them with grounded theory methods"; starting from a taxonomy in the literature is a highly questionable practice.

### E28

- Location: §4.2 Many Studies Present Little to No Detail
- Quote: "Besides being incomplete, it also suggests coding happens in three distinct phases, which is not what Glaser or Strauss had in mind."
- Key point: Summarizing grounded theory as "three coding phases" is not only incomplete but also implies that coding falls into three sharply separate phases, which is not what Glaser or Strauss intended.
