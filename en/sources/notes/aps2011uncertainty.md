# Physical Review A editors 2011: Editorial: Uncertainty Estimates

- Source: `aps2011uncertainty`, [original](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.83.040001)
- Version: Physical Review A 83, 040001, April 29, 2011, signed The Editors (the editors of the American Physical Society's Physical Review A). A one-page editorial, free to read on the publisher's website. It represents only this journal's editors' requirements at the time. Locations give paragraphs.

### E1

- Location: paragraphs 1–2
- Quote: "The purpose of this Editorial is to discuss the importance of including uncertainty estimates in papers involving theoretical calculations of physical quantities." / "It is not unusual for manuscripts on theoretical work to be submitted without uncertainty estimates for numerical results."
- Key point: The editorial discusses why papers with theoretical calculations should include uncertainty estimates; the editors say it is not unusual for theoretical papers to be submitted with numerical results lacking uncertainty estimates.

### E2

- Location: paragraph 2
- Quote: "In contrast, papers presenting the results of laboratory measurements would usually not be considered acceptable for publication in Physical Review A without a detailed discussion of the uncertainties involved in the measurements."
- Key point: By contrast, papers reporting laboratory measurements would usually not be acceptable in Physical Review A without a detailed discussion of measurement uncertainty.

### E3

- Location: paragraph 3, the six classes of theoretical papers
- Quote: "Theoretical papers can be broadly classified as follows:" / "1. Development of new theoretical techniques or formalisms. 2. Development of approximation methods, where the comparison with experiment, or other theory, itself provides an" / "assessment of the error in the method of calculation. 3. Explanation of previously unexplained phenomena, where a semiquantitative agreement with experiment is already" / "significant. 4. Proposals for new experimental arrangements or configurations, such as optical lattices. 5. Quantitative comparisons with experiment for the purpose of (a) verifying that all significant physical effects have been" / "taken into account, and/or (b) interpolating or extrapolating known experimental data. 6. Provision of benchmark results intended as reference data or standards of comparison with other less accurate methods."
- Key point: The editorial broadly divides theoretical papers into six classes: developing new theoretical techniques or formalisms; developing approximation methods (where comparison with experiment or other theory is itself an error assessment); explaining previously unexplained phenomena (where semi-quantitative agreement with experiment is already meaningful); proposing new experimental schemes; quantitative comparison with experiment (testing whether all important physical effects are included, or interpolating and extrapolating known experimental data); and providing benchmark results as reference data.

### E4

- Location: para. 4
- Quote: "It is primarily papers in the last two categories that require a careful assessment of the theoretical uncertainties."
- Key point: It is mainly the last two categories (quantitative comparison with experiment and providing benchmark results) that require a careful assessment of the theoretical uncertainties.

### E5

- Location: para. 4
- Quote: "The uncertainties can arise from two sources: (a) the degree to which the numerical results accurately represent the predictions of an underlying theoretical formalism, for example, convergence with the size of a basis set, or the step size in a numerical integration, and (b) physical effects not included in the calculation from the beginning, such as electron correlation and relativistic corrections."
- Key point: Theoretical uncertainties have two sources: how accurately the numerical results represent the predictions of the underlying theory (e.g., convergence with basis-set size or integration step size), and physical effects left out of the calculation from the start (e.g., electron correlation, relativistic corrections).

### E6

- Location: para. 4
- Quote: "The aim is to estimate the uncertainty, not to state the exact amount of the error or provide a rigorous bound."
- Key point: The aim is to estimate the uncertainty, not to state the exact size of the error or give a rigorous bound.

### E7

- Location: para. 5
- Quote: "Papers presenting the results of theoretical calculations are expected to include uncertainty estimates for the calculations whenever practicable, and especially under the following circumstances:" / "1. If the authors claim high accuracy, or improvements on the accuracy of previous work. 2. If the primary motivation for the paper is to make comparisons with present or future high precision experimental" / "measurements. 3. If the primary motivation is to provide interpolations or extrapolations of known experimental measurements."
- Key point: Papers reporting theoretical calculations should include uncertainty estimates whenever practicable, especially when they claim high accuracy or improvement over previous work, when their main purpose is comparison with current or future high-precision experiments, or when their main purpose is to interpolate or extrapolate known experimental data.

### E8

- Location: para. 5
- Quote: "There are many cases where it is indeed not practical to give a meaningful error estimate for a theoretical calculation; for example, in scattering processes involving complex systems."
- Key point: In many cases it is genuinely impractical to give a meaningful error estimate for a theoretical calculation, for example scattering processes involving complex systems.
