# Georgetown Law Writing Center 2018: Developing a Thesis Statement

- Source: `georgetown2018thesis`, [Original PDF (Writing Center, Georgetown University Law Center, US; free)](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Updated-Developing-a-Thesis-Statement-Handout.pdf)
- Version: Handout from the Writing Center at Georgetown University Law Center, by Shelley Lambert, Marguerite McLamb, and Greg Malloy, 2018. Written for **US law students**. Its approach draws partly on Fajans & Falk's Scholarly Writing for Law Students. Locations are given as handout subheadings.

### E1

- Location: Basics About Thesis Statements
- Quote: "A thesis statement is an original, supportable hypothesis or assertion about a topic. The thesis targets a specific point or aspect of the law, articulates a problem, and ideally attempts to resolve it."
- Key point: A thesis statement is an original, supportable hypothesis or assertion about a topic; it targets a specific aspect of the law, articulates a problem, and ideally attempts to resolve it.

### E2

- Location: Basics About Thesis Statements
- Quote: "try a device such as the "should ... because ..." thesis formulation; argue that a certain result should happen because of particular reasons."
- Key point: You can draft a thesis with a "should ... because ..." formulation: argue that a certain result should happen, for particular reasons.

### E3

- Location: Basics About Thesis Statements
- Quote: "As you develop your argument and construct your paper, test your thesis against known and hypothetical situations, and modify the thesis to further refine or strengthen your proposition."
- Key point: As you write, test your thesis against known and hypothetical situations and revise it accordingly.

### E4

- Location: Possible Approaches for Thesis Statements
- Quote: "Arguments from precedent assert that precedent is binding or should be extended, adopted, or overruled." / "Interpretive arguments examine the language of constitutions, statutes, and regulations." / "Normative arguments assert that a certain rule or result is good, justified by morality, social policy, economics, or justice between the parties." / "Institutional arguments examine the appropriateness of the roles of the judiciary, legislature, or executive or address the administrative impact of a certain result."
- Key point: Four kinds of argument: from precedent (precedent is binding, or should be extended, adopted, or overruled); interpretive (examining the language of constitutions, statutes, and regulations); normative (a rule or result is good, justified by morality, social policy, economics, or fairness between the parties); and institutional (whether the judicial, legislative, and executive roles are appropriate, or the administrative effects of a result).

### E5

- Location: Possible Approaches for Thesis Statements
- Quote: "Identify and resolve inconsistencies, logical errors, and omissions."
- Key point: Identifying and resolving inconsistencies, logical errors, and omissions is one way to arrive at a thesis.

### E6

- Location: Sample Thesis Statements
- Quote: "This Note argues that, based solely on prior case law, particularly Circuit City, the Pyett court should have held that an arbitration clause is enforceable against an employee regardless of the employee's union status."
- Key point: An example thesis: based solely on prior case law, particularly Circuit City, the Pyett court should have held that an arbitration clause is enforceable against an employee regardless of union status.
