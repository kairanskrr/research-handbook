# Higgins et al. (eds.) 2024: Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions

- Source: `higgins2024cochrane`, [Chapter 1](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-01), [Chapter 24](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-24)

### E1

- Location: Chapter 1, Key Points
- Quote: "Systematic reviews aim to minimize bias through the use of pre-specified research questions and methods that are documented in protocols, and by basing their findings on reliable research."
- Key point: Systematic reviews reduce bias by using research questions and methods pre-specified in a protocol, and by basing their findings only on reliable research.

### E2

- Quote: "People who might make – or be affected by – decisions around the use of interventions should be involved in important decisions about the review."
- Key point: People who make, or are affected by, decisions should be involved in important decisions about the review.

### E3

- Location: Chapter 1, §1.1
- Quote: "a systematic review should typically be undertaken before embarking on new primary research."
- Key point: A systematic review should typically be done before starting new primary research.

### E4

- Location: Chapter 1, §1.1
- Quote: "completing a high-quality systematic review is often demanding and time-consuming."
- Key point: Completing a high-quality systematic review is often demanding and time-consuming.

### E5

- Location: Chapter 1, §1.2
- Quote: "For a question about the effects of an intervention, the PICO approach is usually used, which is an acronym for Population, Intervention, Comparison(s) and Outcome."
- Key point: Questions about the effects of an intervention usually use the PICO framework: Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome.

### E6

- Location: Chapter 1, Key Points
- Quote: "Systematic reviews should be conducted by a team that includes domain expertise and methodological expertise, who are free of potential conflicts of interest."
- Key point: Systematic reviews should be done by a team with both domain and methodological expertise and free of potential conflicts of interest.

### E7

- Location: Chapter 24, Key Points
- Quote: "Potential biases are likely to be greater for non-randomized studies compared with randomized trials when evaluating the effects of interventions, so results should always be interpreted with caution"
- Key point: When evaluating the effects of interventions, potential biases are likely to be greater in non-randomized studies than in randomized trials, so results should always be interpreted with caution.

### E8

- Location: Chapter 24, §24.1
- Quote: "Some of this evidence, especially about harms of interventions, will often need to come from NRSI."
- Key point: Some evidence, especially about the harms of interventions, will often need to come from non-randomized studies.

### E9

- Location: Chapter 24, §24.1
- Quote: "nutritionists may be interested in the health-related effects of a diet that includes a minimum of five portions of fruit or vegetables per day (‘five-a-day’), an aetiological question. On the other hand, public health professionals may be interested in the health-related effects of interventions to promote a change in diet to include ‘five-a-day’, an effectiveness question."
- Key point: Nutritionists care about the health effects of a "five-a-day" diet of fruit and vegetables itself, an etiological question; public health workers care about the effects of an intervention that pushes people to eat that way, an effectiveness question. These are different questions.

### E10

- Location: Chapter 24, Key Points
- Quote: "We recommend that eligibility criteria, data collection and assessment of included studies place an emphasis on specific features of study design (e.g. which parts of the study were prospectively designed) rather than ‘labels’ for study designs (such as case-control versus cohort)."
- Key point: Judge a non-randomized study by its specific design features (e.g., which parts were prospectively designed) rather than by labels such as "case-control" or "cohort" alone.

### E11

- Location: Chapter 1, §1.1
- Quote: "Cochrane reviews use primary research to generate new knowledge about the effects of an intervention (or interventions) used in clinical, public health or policy settings."
- Key point: The interventions studied in Cochrane reviews include clinical, public health, and policy interventions.

### E12

- Location: Chapter 1, §1.1
- Quote: "This Handbook is about systematic reviews on the effects of interventions, and specifically about methods used by Cochrane to undertake them."
- Key point: The Cochrane Handbook covers systematic reviews of the effects of interventions, specifically the methods Cochrane uses.

### E13

- Location: Chapter 10, Key Points
- Quote: "Potential advantages of meta-analyses include an improvement in precision, the ability to answer questions not posed by individual studies, and the opportunity to settle controversies arising from conflicting claims. However, they also have the potential to mislead seriously, particularly if specific study designs, within-study biases, variation across studies, and reporting biases are not carefully considered."
- Key point: Meta-analyses can improve precision, answer questions individual studies did not ask, and settle conflicting claims; but without careful attention to study design, within-study bias, between-study variation, and reporting bias, they can be seriously misleading.

### E14

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.1 "Do not start here!"
- Quote: "results of meta-analyses can be very misleading if suitable attention has not been given to formulating the review question; specifying eligibility criteria; identifying and selecting studies; collecting appropriate data; considering risk of bias; planning intervention comparisons; and deciding what data would be meaningful to analyse."
- Key point: If the earlier steps (framing the question, eligibility criteria, searching and selecting studies, data collection, risk of bias, planning the comparisons) were not done well, meta-analysis results can be very misleading. Do not jump straight to the statistics.

### E15

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.1, MECIR C62 (mandatory)
- Quote: "Undertake (or display) a meta-analysis only if participants, interventions, comparisons and outcomes are judged to be sufficiently similar to ensure an answer that is clinically meaningful. Meta-analyses of very diverse studies can be misleading, for example where studies use different forms of control."
- Key point: Do a meta-analysis only if participants, interventions, comparisons, and outcomes are similar enough for the pooled answer to be clinically meaningful; pooling very diverse studies (e.g., with different kinds of control groups) can mislead.

### E16

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.3
- Quote: "Do not do a meta-analysis. A systematic review need not contain any meta-analyses. If there is considerable variation in results, and particularly if there is inconsistency in the direction of effect, it may be misleading to quote an average value for the intervention effect."
- Key point: A systematic review need not contain a meta-analysis. When results vary considerably, especially in the direction of effect, reporting an average effect may mislead.

### E17

- Location: Chapter 12, §12.1
- Quote: "Legitimate reasons include limited evidence; incompletely reported outcome/effect estimates, or different effect measures used across studies; and bias in the evidence. Other commonly cited reasons for not using meta-analysis are because of too much clinical or methodological diversity, or statistical heterogeneity [...] However, meta-analysis methods should be considered in these circumstances, as they may provide important insights if undertaken and interpreted appropriately."
- Key point: Legitimate reasons not to do a meta-analysis: limited evidence; incompletely reported outcomes or effect estimates, or different effect measures across studies; and bias in the evidence. The commonly cited "too much variation / heterogeneity" is not necessarily a reason not to; done and interpreted appropriately, a meta-analysis can still be informative.

### E18

- Location: Chapter 12, Key Points
- Quote: "Synthesis methods that involve vote counting based on statistical significance have serious limitations and are unacceptable."
- Key point: Synthesis by vote counting ("how many studies were significant, how many were not") has serious limitations and is unacceptable.

### E19

- Location: Chapter 12, Table 12.1.a
- Quote: "Meta-analysis is not possible with no studies, or only one study."
- Key point: Meta-analysis is not possible with no studies or only one study.

### E20

- Location: Chapter 7, §7.1.2
- Quote: "Early appraisal tools were structured as quality 'scales', which combined information on several features into a single score. However, this approach was questioned after it was revealed that the type of quality scale used could significantly influence the interpretation of the meta-analysis results"
- Key point: Early appraisal tools were quality "scales" that summed features into a single score; this approach was questioned after it emerged that the choice of scale noticeably changed the interpretation of meta-analysis results.

### E21

- Location: Chapter 7, Key Points
- Quote: "An assessment of the internal validity of studies included in a Cochrane review should emphasize the risk of bias in their results, that is, the risk that they will over-estimate or under-estimate the true intervention effect."
- Key point: Appraising included studies should focus on the risk of bias in their results, that is, the risk of over- or under-estimating the true intervention effect.

### E22

- Location: Chapter 8, Key Points
- Quote: "Each assessment using the RoB 2 tool focuses on a specific result from a randomized trial."
- Key point: Each RoB 2 assessment covers one specific result from a randomized trial, not the whole trial.

### E23

- Location: Chapter 8, §8.2.3
- Quote: "bias arising from the randomization process; bias due to deviations from intended interventions; bias due to missing outcome data; bias in measurement of the outcome; and bias in selection of the reported result."
- Key point: The five bias domains of RoB 2: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result.

### E24

- Location: Chapter 8, Key Points
- Quote: "Judgements can be 'Low', or 'High' risk of bias, or can express 'Some concerns'." / "The overall risk of bias for the result is the least favourable assessment across the domains of bias."
- Key point: Judgments are "Low risk," "Some concerns," or "High risk"; the overall judgment is the least favorable across the domains.

### E25

- Location: Chapter 8, Key Points
- Quote: "Answers to signalling questions and judgements about risk of bias should be supported by written justifications."
- Key point: Every answer to a signaling question and every risk-of-bias judgment needs a written justification.

### E26

- Location: Chapter 8, §8.2.1
- Quote: "It is important not to select results to assess based on the likely judgements arising from the assessment."
- Key point: Do not choose which results to assess based on the judgments they are likely to receive.

### E27

- Location: Chapter 8, §8.2.2
- Quote: "the estimated effect of assignment to intervention would be the most appropriate to inform a health policy question about whether to recommend an intervention in a particular health system [...] whereas the estimated effect of adhering to the intervention as specified in the trial protocol would be the most appropriate to inform a care decision by an individual patient"
- Key point: The effect of assignment to intervention (intention-to-treat effect) suits health policy questions; the effect of adhering to the intervention suits decisions for individual patients. State which one you care about before assessing.

### E28

- Location: Chapter 25, Key Points
- Quote: "Review authors should specify important confounding domains and co-interventions of concern in their protocol."
- Key point: When including non-randomized studies, specify the important confounding domains and co-interventions of concern in the protocol in advance.

### E29

- Location: Chapter 25, Key Points
- Quote: "At the start of a ROBINS-I assessment of a study, review authors should describe a 'target trial', which is a hypothetical pragmatic randomized trial of the interventions compared in the study, conducted on the same participant group and without features putting it at risk of bias."
- Key point: A ROBINS-I assessment starts by describing a "target trial": a hypothetical randomized trial of the same interventions in the same population, free of sources of bias, against which the actual study is compared.

### E30

- Location: Chapter 25, Key Points
- Quote: "judgements for each bias domain, and for overall risk of bias, can be 'Low', 'Moderate', 'Serious' or 'Critical' risk of bias."
- Key point: ROBINS-I judgments have four levels: Low, Moderate, Serious, and Critical.

### E31

- Location: Chapter 25, §25.1
- Quote: "Evaluating risk of bias in results of NRSI requires both methodological and content expertise. The process is more involved than for randomized trials"
- Key point: Assessing risk of bias in non-randomized studies requires both methodological and content expertise and is more involved than for randomized trials.

### E32

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.4.3
- Quote: "It is generally recommended that meta-analyses are undertaken using risk ratios (taking care to make a sensible choice over which category of outcome is classified as the event) or odds ratios."
- Key point: For dichotomous outcomes, meta-analysis is generally recommended using risk ratios (choosing sensibly which category counts as the "event") or odds ratios.

### E33

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.4.3
- Quote: "The odds ratio is the hardest summary statistic to understand and to apply in practice, and many practising clinicians report difficulties in using them. There are many published examples where authors have misinterpreted odds ratios from meta-analyses as risk ratios."
- Key point: The odds ratio is the hardest summary statistic to understand, and many clinicians struggle with it; published papers often misinterpret meta-analytic odds ratios as risk ratios.

### E34

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.4.3
- Quote: "It is often sensible to use one statistic for meta-analysis and to re-express the results using a second, more easily interpretable statistic."
- Key point: A common approach is to pool with one statistic and re-express the result as a second, easier-to-interpret one (such as an absolute risk difference).

### E35

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.4.3
- Quote: "Selecting an effect measure based on what is the most consistent in a particular situation is not a generally recommended strategy, since it may lead to a selection that spuriously maximizes the precision of a meta-analysis estimate."
- Key point: Do not pick the "most consistent" effect measure after seeing the data; this can spuriously inflate the precision of the pooled result.

### E36

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.5.1
- Quote: "Selection of summary statistics for continuous data is principally determined by whether studies all report the outcome using the same scale (when the mean difference can be used) or using different scales (when the standardized mean difference is usually used)."
- Key point: Continuous outcomes: use the mean difference when all studies use the same scale, and generally the standardized mean difference when they use different scales.

### E37

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.2.1 and §10.3
- Quote: "In the second stage, a summary (combined) intervention effect estimate is calculated as a weighted average of the intervention effects estimated in the individual studies." / "larger studies, which have smaller standard errors, are given more weight than smaller studies"
- Key point: The pooled result of a meta-analysis is a weighted average of the study effects; in common methods, larger studies with smaller standard errors get more weight.

### E38

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.4.1
- Quote: "the assumption is usually made that the true effect of intervention (in both magnitude and direction) is the same value in every study (i.e. fixed across studies)." / "Instead of assuming that the intervention effects are the same, we assume that they follow (usually) a normal distribution."
- Key point: A fixed-effect model assumes the true effect is the same in every study; a random-effects model assumes the true effects differ across studies and follow a (usually normal) distribution.

### E39

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.4.1
- Quote: "The decision between fixed- and random-effects meta-analyses has been the subject of much debate, and we do not provide a universal recommendation."
- Key point: The choice between fixed and random effects has long been debated, and the Handbook gives no universal recommendation.

### E40

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.4.1
- Quote: "Since it is generally considered to be implausible that intervention effects across studies are identical (unless the intervention has no effect at all), this leads many to advocate use of the random-effects model."
- Key point: It is generally considered implausible that intervention effects are identical across studies, which leads many to advocate the random-effects model.

### E41

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.4.1
- Quote: "Others have argued that a fixed-effect analysis can be interpreted in the presence of heterogeneity, and that it makes fewer assumptions than a random-effects meta-analysis."
- Key point: Others argue that a fixed-effect result can still be interpreted when there is heterogeneity, and that it makes fewer assumptions than a random-effects model.

### E42

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.4.1
- Quote: "The choice between a fixed-effect and a random-effects meta-analysis should never be made on the basis of a statistical test for heterogeneity."
- Key point: Never choose between fixed and random effects based on a statistical test for heterogeneity.

### E43

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.4.1
- Quote: "If there is additionally some funnel plot asymmetry (i.e. a relationship between intervention effect magnitude and study size), then this will push the results of the random-effects analysis towards the findings in the smaller studies."
- Key point: The random-effects model gives small studies more weight; if there is funnel plot asymmetry (effect size related to study size), the random-effects result is pushed toward the findings of the small studies.

### E44

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.4.1
- Quote: "A pragmatic approach is to plan to undertake both a fixed-effect and a random-effects meta-analysis, with an intention to present the random-effects result if there is no indication of funnel plot asymmetry."
- Key point: A pragmatic approach: plan both models in advance, and present the random-effects result if there is no sign of funnel plot asymmetry.

### E45

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.1
- Quote: "Statistical heterogeneity manifests itself in the observed intervention effects being more different from each other than one would expect due to random error (chance) alone."
- Key point: Statistical heterogeneity means the observed effects differ across studies more than random error (chance) alone would explain.

### E46

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.2
- Quote: "while a statistically significant result may indicate a problem with heterogeneity, a non-significant result must not be taken as evidence of no heterogeneity."
- Key point: A significant heterogeneity test (chi-squared test) may indicate a problem, but a non-significant result must never be taken as evidence of no heterogeneity.

### E47

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.2
- Quote: "This describes the percentage of the variability in effect estimates that is due to heterogeneity rather than sampling error (chance)."
- Key point: I² describes the percentage of variability in effect estimates that is due to heterogeneity rather than sampling error.

### E48

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.2
- Quote: "Thresholds for the interpretation of the I 2 statistic can be misleading, since the importance of inconsistency depends on several factors." / "0% to 40%: might not be important; 30% to 60%: may represent moderate heterogeneity*; 50% to 90%: may represent substantial heterogeneity*; 75% to 100%: considerable heterogeneity*."
- Key point: Interpreting I² with fixed thresholds can mislead. The Handbook's rough guide (for meta-analyses of randomized trials): 0-40% might not be important, 30-60% may be moderate, 50-90% may be substantial, 75-100% considerable. The ranges overlap on purpose; also consider the size and direction of effects and the strength of the evidence for heterogeneity.

### E49

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.2, MECIR C63 (mandatory)
- Quote: "Authors should recognize that there is much uncertainty in measures such as I 2 and Tau 2 when there are few studies. Thus, use of simple thresholds to diagnose heterogeneity should be avoided."
- Key point: With few studies, I² and Tau² are very uncertain, so avoid diagnosing heterogeneity with simple thresholds.

### E50

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.4.2
- Quote: "The confidence interval from a random-effects meta-analysis describes uncertainty in the location of the mean of systematically different effects in the different studies. It does not describe the degree of heterogeneity among studies, as may be commonly believed."
- Key point: The confidence interval of a random-effects result describes uncertainty about where the mean effect lies, not how much studies differ from one another.

### E51

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.4.3
- Quote: "we encourage their use when the number of studies is reasonable (e.g. five or more [...] and there is no clear funnel plot asymmetry."
- Key point: When the number of studies is reasonable (e.g., five or more) and there is no clear funnel plot asymmetry, reporting a prediction interval is encouraged to show the spread of true effects across studies.

### E52

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.3
- Quote: "Severe apparent heterogeneity can indicate that data have been incorrectly extracted or entered into meta-analysis software. For example, if standard errors have mistakenly been entered as SDs for continuous outcomes"
- Key point: When heterogeneity is unusually large, first check for data extraction or entry errors, such as standard errors entered as standard deviations.

### E53

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.3
- Quote: "Reliable conclusions can only be drawn from analyses that are truly pre-specified before inspecting the studies' results, and even these conclusions should be interpreted with caution. Explorations of heterogeneity that are devised after heterogeneity is identified can at best lead to the generation of hypotheses."
- Key point: Only analyses (such as subgroup analyses) truly pre-specified before looking at the results can support reliable conclusions, and even these need caution; analyses thought up after heterogeneity is found can at most generate hypotheses.

### E54

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.3
- Quote: "In general it is unwise to exclude studies from a meta-analysis on the basis of their results as this may introduce bias." / "It is advisable to perform analyses both with and without outlying studies as part of a sensitivity analysis"
- Key point: Do not exclude a study just because its result is an outlier; instead, run analyses with and without it as part of a sensitivity analysis.

### E55

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.3
- Quote: "This is not a substitute for a thorough investigation of heterogeneity."
- Key point: Using a random-effects model is no substitute for a thorough investigation of the sources of heterogeneity.

### E56

- Location: Chapter 10, Key Points
- Quote: "Sensitivity analyses should be used to examine whether overall findings are robust to potentially influential decisions."
- Key point: Use sensitivity analyses to check whether the overall findings hold up when a potentially influential decision is made differently.

### E57

- Location: Chapter 13, §13.1
- Quote: "'statistically significant' results that suggest an intervention works are more likely than 'statistically non-significant' results to be available, available rapidly, available in high impact journals and cited by others, and hence more easily identifiable for systematic reviews."
- Key point: Statistically significant results suggesting an intervention works are more likely than non-significant ones to be published, published quickly, published in high-impact journals, and cited, so reviews find them more easily.

### E58

- Location: Chapter 13, Key Points
- Quote: "It may be necessary to consult multiple bibliographic databases, trials registers, manufacturers, regulators and study authors or sponsors."
- Key point: To reduce missed studies, it may be necessary to search multiple bibliographic databases and trials registers and to contact manufacturers, regulators, and study authors or sponsors.

### E59

- Location: Chapter 13, §13.3.4.3
- Quote: "the funnel plot should be seen as a generic means of displaying small-study effects: a tendency for the intervention effects estimated in smaller studies to differ from those estimated in larger studies"
- Key point: A funnel plot displays "small-study effects": a tendency for smaller studies to estimate different effects from larger ones. Publication bias is only one possible cause.

### E60

- Location: Chapter 13, §13.3.4.4
- Quote: "As a rule of thumb, tests for funnel plot asymmetry should be used only when there are at least 10 studies included in the meta-analysis, because when there are fewer studies the power of the tests is low. Only 28% of a random sample of 300 systematic reviews indexed in 2020 included a meta-analysis with at least 10 studies"
- Key point: Rule of thumb: test for funnel plot asymmetry only when the meta-analysis has at least 10 studies; with fewer, the tests have low power. In a random sample of 300 systematic reviews from 2020, only 28% contained a meta-analysis with at least 10 studies.

### E61

- Location: Chapter 13, §13.3.4.4
- Quote: "funnel plot asymmetry should not be considered to be diagnostic for the presence of non-reporting bias ."
- Key point: Funnel plot asymmetry should not be treated as diagnostic of publication bias (non-reporting bias).

### E62

- Location: Chapter 13, §13.3.4.6
- Quote: "they compare the fixed-effect and random-effects estimates of the intervention effect. If the estimates are similar, then any small-study effects have little effect on the intervention effect estimate."
- Key point: If you are worried about small-study effects, compare the fixed-effect and random-effects estimates; if they are similar, small-study effects have little influence on the result.

### E63

- Location: Chapter 13, Key Points
- Quote: "A thorough assessment of selective non-reporting or under-reporting of results in the studies identified is likely to be the most valuable."
- Key point: When assessing bias from missing evidence, the most valuable step is a thorough check of whether the identified studies selectively did not report, or under-reported, results.

### E64

- Location: Chapter 14, Key Points
- Quote: "The GRADE approach specifies four levels of the certainty for a body of evidence for a given outcome: high, moderate, low and very low."
- Key point: GRADE rates the certainty of the body of evidence for an outcome at four levels: high, moderate, low, and very low.

### E65

- Location: Chapter 14, Key Points
- Quote: "GRADE assessments of certainty are determined through consideration of five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. For evidence from non-randomized studies and rarely randomized studies, assessments can then be upgraded through consideration of three further domains."
- Key point: GRADE considers five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias; evidence from non-randomized studies can also be rated up on three further domains.

### E66

- Location: Chapter 14, §14.2
- Quote: "In GRADE, a body of evidence from randomized trials begins with a high-certainty rating while a body of evidence from NRSI begins with a low-certainty rating."
- Key point: In GRADE, evidence from randomized trials starts at "high" certainty and evidence from non-randomized studies starts at "low."

### E67

- Location: Chapter 14, §14.2, MECIR C74 (mandatory)
- Quote: "Ideally, two people working independently should assess the certainty of the body of evidence and reach a consensus view on any downgrading decisions."
- Key point: Ideally, two people assess the certainty of the evidence independently and reach consensus on any downgrading decisions.

### E68

- Location: Chapter 14, §14.2, MECIR C75 (mandatory)
- Quote: "Justify and document all assessments of the certainty of the body of evidence (e.g. downgrading or upgrading using GRADE)."
- Key point: Justify and document every upgrading and downgrading decision.

### E69

- Location: Chapter 14, Key Points
- Quote: "'Summary of findings' tables include a row for each important outcome (up to a maximum of seven)."
- Key point: A "Summary of findings" table has one row per important outcome, up to seven outcomes.

### E70

- Location: Chapter III, Key Points
- Quote: "Cochrane review protocols and reviews should comply with the PRISMA 2020 and PRISMA for Protocols reporting guidelines."
- Key point: Cochrane review protocols and reviews should comply with PRISMA 2020 and PRISMA for Protocols.

### E71

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.4.4
- Quote: "many methods of meta-analysis are based on large sample approximations, and are unsuitable when events are rare."
- Key point: Many meta-analysis methods rely on large-sample approximations and are unsuitable when events are rare, so choose methods carefully.

### E72

- Location: Chapter 10, Key Points
- Quote: "Variation across studies (heterogeneity) must be considered, although most Cochrane reviews do not have enough studies to allow for the reliable investigation of its causes."
- Key point: Variation across studies must be considered, but most Cochrane reviews have too few studies to investigate its causes reliably.

### E73

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.3, MECIR C69 (mandatory)
- Quote: "If a fixed-effect analysis is used, the confidence intervals ignore the extent of heterogeneity. If a random-effects analysis is used, the result pertains to the mean effect across studies."
- Key point: With a fixed-effect analysis, the confidence interval ignores heterogeneity; with a random-effects analysis, the result refers to the mean effect across studies. Either way, discuss what substantial heterogeneity implies.

### E74

- Location: Chapter 14, §14.2
- Quote: "Review authors will generally grade evidence from sound non-randomized studies as low certainty, even if ROBINS-I is used."
- Key point: Even when ROBINS-I is used, evidence from sound non-randomized studies is generally rated as low certainty.

### E75

- Location: Chapter 10, §10.10.3
- Quote: "Also, investigations of heterogeneity when there are very few studies are of questionable value."
- Key point: With very few studies, investigating the sources of heterogeneity is of questionable value.
