# Neuenschwander 2014: Major Legal Challenges Facing Oral History in the Digital Age

- Source: `neuenschwander2014legal`, [original](https://ohda.matrix.msu.edu/2012/06/major-legal-challenges/)
- Version: article by John A. Neuenschwander (author of *A Guide to Oral History and the Law*, Oxford University Press 2009) for the Oral History in the Digital Age project (funded by the US Institute of Museum and Library Services); the project gives 2014 as the citation year. It covers US law. Accessed 2026-10-06. Locations give the topic of the paragraph in the article.

### E1

- Location: opening
- Quote: "The four major legal areas of concern prior to the digital age: release agreements, protecting restricted interviews, the privacy torts, and copyright have not changed"
- Key point: The four major legal concerns for oral history: release agreements, protecting restricted interviews, privacy torts, and copyright.

### E2

- Location: informed consent and release agreements
- Quote: "Overly broad clauses like “for such scholarly and educational purposes as the oral history program shall deem appropriate,” are simply too generic in the digital age." / "Potential uses of the interviews (in whole or in part)include but are not limited to research, Internet display, media productions, publications, educational curriculum, and museum exhibits."
- Key point: Broad clauses such as "for such scholarly and educational purposes as the oral history program shall deem appropriate" are too vague in the digital age; be specific, e.g., list research, online display, media productions, publication, teaching materials, and museum exhibits.

### E3

- Location: informed consent and release agreements
- Quote: "the growing use of video to record interviews together with increasing online displays of interviews and media productions all underscore the need to secure permission from interviewees and interviewers to display their names and images."
- Key point: With more interviews recorded on video and displayed online, you need permission from both interviewees and interviewers to use their names and likenesses.

### E4

- Location: restricted interviews and subpoenas, the Boston College case
- Quote: "The promise that had been made to all interviewees was that their interviews would remain confidential until their death."
- Key point: Boston College's "Belfast Project" promised all interviewees that their interviews would stay confidential until their death.

### E5

- Location: restricted interviews and subpoenas, the Boston College case
- Quote: "The choice to investigate criminal activity belongs to the government and is not subject to veto by academic researchers."
- Key point: The US Court of Appeals for the First Circuit ruled that the choice to investigate criminal activity belongs to the government and cannot be vetoed by academic researchers.

### E6

- Location: restricted interviews and subpoenas, the Boston College case
- Quote: "Ed Moloney had been directed to place a clause in each release agreement that the pledge of confidentiality would be protected “to the extent American law allows.”" / "This was not done"
- Key point: The project director was supposed to put a clause in each release agreement limiting confidentiality "to the extent American law allows," but did not.

### E7

- Location: restricted interviews and subpoenas, lessons
- Quote: "institutions do need to develop procedures to more closely scrutinize pledges of confidentiality. Such procedures should include legal risk assessments, utilizing IRB expertise where appropriate, possibly applying for a certificate of confidentiality, insuring that all parties understand the limits of the confidentiality promised, and making a commitment to mount a strong legal defense."
- Key point: Institutions need to scrutinize pledges of confidentiality more closely: do a legal risk assessment, involve the institutional review board (IRB) as appropriate, consider applying for a certificate of confidentiality, make sure everyone understands the limits of confidentiality, and be ready to fight subpoenas vigorously.

### E8

- Location: restricted interviews and subpoenas, lessons
- Quote: "researchers and scholars have had some success in civil cases utilizing a standard motion to quash to obtain either a withdrawal of a subpoena or the imposition of some reasonable limits on its scope." / "If the subpoena was issued as part of a criminal proceeding, however, state and federal courts have taken a position similar to that of the First Circuit in the Boston College case."
- Key point: In civil cases, researchers have had some success with a motion to quash; for subpoenas in criminal proceedings, US state and federal courts have taken positions similar to the Boston College case.

### E9

- Location: privacy torts
- Quote: "The major privacy torts: defamation (slander and libel), false light, public disclosure of embarrassing private facts, and misappropriation of likeness (right of publicity) have always been potential liability issues for oral historians."
- Key point: The major privacy torts: defamation (slander and libel), false light, public disclosure of embarrassing private facts, and misappropriation of name or likeness (right of publicity).

### E10

- Location: defamation, self-check list
- Quote: "Is the individual alive and still active in his/her work or the community?" / "Would a reader’s opinion or estimation of the individual be negatively impacted by the statements or characterizations?" / "Is there supporting factual information or just an unsupported negative opinion?" / "Is there other evidence that supports or corroborates the interviewee’s statements?"
- Key point: Four questions for judging defamation risk: Is the person alive and still active? Would readers think less of them? Is the statement backed by facts, or just a negative opinion? Is there other corroborating evidence?

### E11

- Location: defamation
- Quote: "Most negative statements or characterizations are not defamatory. The historical record is not and should not be something that has been sanitized by its creators and keepers." / "Only after this has been done should a program consider such steps as editing, sealing or even deleting."
- Key point: Most negative statements are not defamatory, and the historical record should not be sanitized; try to verify first, and only then consider editing, sealing, or deleting.

### E12

- Location: informed consent and release agreements
- Quote: "Researchers in the social sciences and humanities who incur IRB review are as a matter of course required to show that they too will engage in a rigorous consenting process."
- Key point: (US) Social science and humanities researchers subject to Institutional Review Board (IRB) review are routinely required to show a rigorous informed consent process.
