# Makel & Plucker 2014: Facts Are More Important Than Novelty: Replication in the Education Sciences

- Source: `makel2014facts`, [AERA article page](https://www.aera.net/Newsroom/Recent-AERA-Research/Facts-Are-More-Important-Than-Novelty-Replication-in-the-Education-Sciences), [AERA press release](https://www.aera.net/tabid/15612/mid/31214/default.aspx)

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "The present study analyzed the complete publication history of the current top 100 education journals ranked by 5-year impact factor and found that only 0.13% of education articles were replications."
- Key point: The authors analyzed the complete publication history of the current top 100 education journals by 5-year impact factor and found that only 0.13% of articles were replications.

### E2

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "Despite increased attention to methodological rigor in education research, the field has focused heavily on experimental design and not on the merit of replicating important results."
- Key point: Education research pays growing attention to methodological rigor, but it has focused on experimental design rather than on replicating important findings.

### E3

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "However, replications were significantly less likely to be successful when there was no overlap in authorship between the original and replicating articles."
- Key point: Replications were significantly less likely to succeed when there was no overlap in authorship between the original and the replicating study.

### E4

- Location: Press release
- Quote: "Nearly 68 percent (67.4 percent) of the replications successfully replicated the findings of the original study, 19.5 percent had mixed results (supporting some, but not all, findings), and 13.1 percent failed to replicate any of the original findings."
- Key point: Of these replications, 67.4% successfully replicated the original findings, 19.5% had mixed results, and 13.1% failed to replicate.

### E5

- Location: Press release
- Quote: "Replication studies that were conducted by completely new research teams were found to be successful 54 percent of the time. When replications were conducted by the original authors in the same publication as the original findings, 88.7 percent were successful."
- Key point: Replications by completely new research teams succeeded 54% of the time; replications by the original authors within the same paper succeeded 88.7% of the time.

### E6

- Location: Press release
- Quote: "Overall, about half (48.2%) of all published replications are done by the research team that completed the original study."
- Key point: About half (48.2%) of all published replications were done by the team that completed the original study.

### E7

- Location: Press release
- Quote: "These include revising journal editorial policies to explicitly encourage submission of replication studies, creating a new article type in journals for replications, adopting universal standards for conducting replications, and introducing replication training into graduate and undergraduate student projects."
- Key point: The authors recommend that journals explicitly encourage submission of replication studies, create a dedicated article type for them, adopt common replication standards, and include replication training in graduate and undergraduate programs.

### E8

- Location: Press release
- Quote: "The authors identified eight reasons, or biases, that help explain the lack of replication in the education research field."
- Key point: The authors identify eight reasons, or biases, that help explain the lack of replication in education research.

### E9

- Location: Press release
- Quote: "These include submission bias, funding bias, editor/reviewer bias, journal publication policy bias, hiring bias, promotion bias, journals-analyzed bias, and “novelty equals creativity” bias."
- Key point: The eight biases include submission, funding, editor/reviewer, journal publication policy, hiring, and promotion biases, as well as a bias that equates novelty with creativity.
