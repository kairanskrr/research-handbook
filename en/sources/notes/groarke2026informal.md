### E2

- Location: §3 Standardizing Arguments
- Quote: "discarding irrelevant and distracting digressions, repetition, and remarks ("noise") which do not play a role in the reasoning the argument contains;" / "clarifying incomplete, vague or ambiguous claims and utterances;" / "recognizing components of the argument which are not explicitly expressed."
- Key point: Standardizing includes discarding digressions and repetition that play no role in the reasoning ("noise"), clarifying incomplete, vague, or ambiguous claims, and recognizing components of the argument that are not stated explicitly.

### E3

- Location: §3.1 Implicit Premises and Conclusions
- Quote: "Recognizing an argument's implicit premises prepares the way for argument evaluation, for these premises, like an argument's explicit premises, need to be evaluated when the argument is assessed."
- Key point: Implicit premises, like explicit ones, must be evaluated when you assess an argument.

### E4

- Location: §3.1 Implicit Premises and Conclusions
- Quote: "One principle often used in choosing between alternative interpretations of an implicit argument component is the "Principle of Charity." It favors an interpretation of an argument which makes the argument as credible as possible."
- Key point: A principle often used to choose among interpretations of an implicit component is the "Principle of Charity": prefer the interpretation that makes the argument as credible as possible.

### E5

- Location: §3.1 Implicit Premises and Conclusions
- Quote: "In many situations, this can best be accomplished by assigning the argument a "logically minimum" implicit premise, understood as the weakest component needed to successfully connect the argument's premises to its conclusion."
- Key point: A common approach is to supply a "logically minimum" implicit premise: the weakest component needed to connect the premises to the conclusion.

### E6

- Location: §3.3 "Supplemented" Standardizations
- Quote: "It is a foundational kernel of the argument, but becomes an argument in the full sense only when it engages the dialectical tier, weighing alternative points of view, addressing the objections to the conclusion they suggest."
- Key point: (Summarizing Johnson 2000) Premises plus conclusion are only the kernel of an argument; it becomes an argument in the full sense only when it engages the "dialectical tier," weighing alternative points of view and addressing the objections they raise.

### E7

- Location: §4.1 AV Criteria
- Quote: "making "Acceptability, Relevance and Sufficiency" the requirements for good argument. On this account, premises are acceptable when they are true or acceptable in some other way; relevant when they provide some (i.e. any) support for the conclusion of the argument; and sufficient when they provide enough support to warrant its acceptance"
- Key point: Many informal logic frameworks use "Acceptability, Relevance and Sufficiency" as the criteria for good argument: premises are true or otherwise acceptable, provide some support for the conclusion, and provide enough support to accept it.
