# Vandenbroucke et al. 2007: Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE): Explanation and Elaboration

- Source: `vandenbroucke2007strobe`, [PLoS Medicine](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.0040297) (CC BY)
- Version: PLoS Med 4(10): e297, 2007. The explanation and elaboration paper that accompanies STROBE.

### E1

- Location: Box 1. Main study designs covered by STROBE
- Quote: "In cohort studies, the investigators follow people over time. They obtain information about people and their exposures at baseline, let time pass, and then assess the occurrence of outcomes."
- Key point: Cohort studies: record a group of people's exposures at baseline, follow them over time, and then assess whether outcomes occur.

### E2

- Location: Box 1. Main study designs covered by STROBE
- Quote: "In case-control studies, investigators compare exposures between people with a particular disease outcome (cases) and people without that outcome (controls)."
- Key point: Case-control studies: compare past exposures between people with a disease (cases) and people without it (controls).

### E3

- Location: Box 1. Main study designs covered by STROBE
- Quote: "In cross-sectional studies, investigators assess all individuals in a sample at the same point in time, often to examine the prevalence of exposures, risk factors or disease."
- Key point: Cross-sectional studies: assess everyone in a sample at the same point in time, often to examine the prevalence of exposures, risk factors, or disease.

### E4

- Location: Box 1. Main study designs covered by STROBE
- Quote: "A difficulty that can occur in any design but is particularly clear in cross-sectional studies is to establish that an exposure preceded the disease"
- Key point: Cross-sectional studies make it especially hard to establish that the exposure preceded the disease.

### E5

- Location: Box 3. Bias
- Quote: "Bias and confounding are not synonymous. Bias arises from flawed information or subject selection so that a wrong association is found. Confounding produces relations that are factually right, but that cannot be interpreted causally because some underlying, unaccounted for factor is associated with both exposure and outcome"
- Key point: Bias and confounding are not the same. Bias comes from flawed information or subject selection; confounding produces an association that is factually real but cannot be read as causal, because an unaccounted factor is related to both exposure and outcome.

### E6

- Location: Item 8 (Data sources/measurement), Explanation
- Quote: "Measurement error and misclassification of exposures or outcomes can make it more difficult to detect cause-effect relationships, or may produce spurious relationships. Error in measurement of potential confounders can increase the risk of residual confounding"
- Key point: Measurement error and misclassification of exposures or outcomes can make real cause-effect relationships harder to detect or produce spurious ones; error in measuring confounders leaves "residual confounding."

### E7

- Location: Item 19 (Limitations), Explanation
- Quote: "The inability to precisely measure true values of an exposure tends to result in bias towards unity: the less precisely a risk factor is measured, the greater the bias."
- Key point: Imprecise exposure measurement tends to bias the estimated effect toward "no association" (toward unity); the less precise the measurement, the greater the bias.

### E8

- Location: Item 20 (Interpretation), Explanation
- Quote: "The doubling in the risk of ovarian cancer associated with eating 2 to 4 eggs per week is not immediately credible, since dietary [...] habits are associated with a large number of lifestyle factors as well as SES"
- Key point: An association between eating 2 to 4 eggs a week and a doubled risk of ovarian cancer is not immediately credible, because dietary habits are associated with many lifestyle factors and socioeconomic status.

### E9

- Location: Item 16 (Main results), Explanation
- Quote: "we and others do not advise selecting confounders based solely on statistical significance testing"
- Key point: Do not select confounders to adjust for based solely on statistical significance testing.

### E10

- Location: Item 8 (Data sources/measurement), Explanation
- Quote: "Rather than simply citing validation studies (as in the first example), we advise that authors give the estimated validity or reliability, which can then be used for measurement error adjustment or sensitivity analyses"
- Key point: Rather than only citing validation studies, give the estimated validity or reliability of the measurement method, so it can be used for measurement error adjustment or sensitivity analyses.

### E11

- Location: Item 13 (Participants), Explanation
- Quote: "the association between young maternal age and leukaemia in offspring, which has been observed in some case-control studies [...] was explained by differential participation of young women in case and control groups. Young women with healthy children were less likely to participate than those with unhealthy children"
- Key point: Example: young mothers with healthy children were less willing to take part in studies, so the "young maternal age and childhood leukemia" association was actually caused by differential participation.

### E12

- Location: STROBE checklist, Item 20
- Quote: "Give a cautious overall interpretation of results considering objectives, limitations, multiplicity of analyses, results from similar studies, and other relevant evidence"
- Key point: Give a cautious overall interpretation of results, considering objectives, limitations, multiplicity of analyses, results from similar studies, and other relevant evidence.

### E13

- Location: Item 13 (Participants), Explanation
- Quote: "Although low participation does not necessarily compromise the validity of a study, transparent information on participation and reasons for non-participation is essential."
- Key point: Low participation does not necessarily compromise a study's validity, but participation and reasons for non-participation must be reported transparently.

### E14

- Location: Box 1. Main study designs covered by STROBE
- Quote: "Controls represent the cohort or population of people from which the cases arose."
- Key point: In a case-control study, the controls represent the cohort or population from which the cases arose.

### E15

- Location: Box 1. Main study designs covered by STROBE
- Quote: "Some cross-sectional studies are analytical and aim to quantify potential causal associations between exposures and disease." / "A difficulty that can occur in any design but is particularly clear in cross-sectional studies is to establish that an exposure preceded the disease"
- Key point: Some cross-sectional studies are analytical and aim to quantify possible causal associations between exposure and disease, but cross-sectional studies make it especially hard to establish that exposure preceded disease.

### E16

- Location: Box 1. Main study designs covered by STROBE
- Quote: "Investigators may assess several different outcomes, and examine exposure and outcome variables at multiple points during follow-up."
- Key point: Cohort studies can assess several outcomes and measure exposures and outcomes at multiple points during follow-up.

### E17

- Location: Item 4 (Study design), Explanation
- Quote: "We recommend that authors refrain from simply calling a study ‘prospective’ or ‘retrospective’ because these terms are ill defined" / "Most importantly, we recommend that authors describe exactly how and when data collection took place."
- Key point: Don't just label a study "prospective" or "retrospective," since these terms are ill defined; most importantly, describe exactly how and when the data were collected.

### E18

- Location: Item 6 (Participants), Explanation
- Quote: "In general, controls should reflect the population from which the cases arose."
- Key point: In general, controls should reflect the population from which the cases arose.

### E19

- Location: Item 6 (Participants), Explanation
- Quote: "Controls with other diseases may have advantages over population-based controls, in particular for hospital-based cases, because they better reflect the catchment population of a hospital, have greater comparability of recall and ease of recruitment. However, they can present problems if the exposure of interest affects the risk of developing or being hospitalized for the control condition(s)"
- Key point: Using people with other diseases as controls (hospital controls) has advantages for hospital-based cases: they better reflect the hospital's catchment population, give more comparable recall, and are easier to recruit; but problems arise if the exposure studied affects the onset of, or hospitalization for, the controls' diseases.

### E20

- Location: Box 2. Matching in case-control studies
- Quote: "Matching is intuitively appealing, but the complexities involved have led methodologists to advise against routine matching in case-control studies."
- Key point: Matching is intuitively appealing, but its complexities have led methodologists to advise against routine matching in case-control studies.

### E21

- Location: Box 2. Matching in case-control studies
- Quote: "Matching will introduce irremediable bias if the matching variable is not a confounder but in the causal pathway between exposure and disease. For example, in vitro fertilization is associated with an increased risk of perinatal death, due to an increase in multiple births and low birth weight infants" [...] "Matching on plurality or birth weight will bias results towards the null, and this cannot be remedied in the analysis."
- Key point: Matching introduces irremediable bias if the matching variable is not a confounder but lies on the causal pathway from exposure to disease. Example: in vitro fertilization is associated with higher perinatal mortality because it increases multiple births and low birth weight; matching on plurality or birth weight biases the result toward no association, and the analysis cannot fix this.

### E22

- Location: Box 2. Matching in case-control studies
- Quote: "A crude analysis of the data will produce odds ratios that are usually biased towards unity if the matching factor is associated with the exposure. The solution is to perform a matched or stratified analysis"
- Key point: When the matching factor is associated with the exposure, a crude analysis that ignores the matching gives odds ratios usually biased toward 1; do a matched or stratified analysis.

### E23

- Location: STROBE checklist, Item 7 (Variables)
- Quote: "Clearly define all outcomes, exposures, predictors, potential confounders, and effect modifiers. Give diagnostic criteria, if applicable."
- Key point: Clearly define all outcomes, exposures, predictors, potential confounders, and effect modifiers; give diagnostic criteria where applicable.

### E24

- Location: Box 4. Grouping
- Quote: "Readers should be informed if cut-points are selected post hoc from several alternatives. In particular, if the cut-points were chosen to minimise a P value the true strength of an association will be exaggerated"
- Key point: Tell readers if cut-points were chosen post hoc from several alternatives; in particular, choosing cut-points to minimize a P value exaggerates the strength of the association.

### E25

- Location: Item 8 (Data sources/measurement), Explanation
- Quote: "if an interviewer first questions all the cases and then the controls, or vice versa, bias is possible because of the learning curve; solutions such as randomising the order of interviewing may avoid this problem."
- Key point: If an interviewer questions all cases first and then the controls (or the reverse), the learning curve can introduce bias; randomizing the order of interviews can avoid this.

### E26

- Location: Box 3. Bias
- Quote: "In case-control studies information bias occurs if cases recall past exposures more or less accurately than controls without that disease, or if they are more or less willing to report them (also called ‘recall bias’). ‘Interviewer bias’ can occur if interviewers are aware of the study hypothesis and subconsciously or consciously gather data selectively" / "Some form of blinding of study participants and researchers is therefore often valuable."
- Key point: In case-control studies, recall bias arises when cases recall or are willing to report past exposures differently from controls; interviewers who know the hypothesis may consciously or unconsciously collect data selectively (interviewer bias). Some form of blinding of participants and researchers is therefore often valuable.

### E27

- Location: Box 3. Bias
- Quote: "Patients receiving a drug that causes non-specific stomach discomfort may undergo gastroscopy more often and have more ulcers detected than patients not receiving the drug – even if the drug does not cause more ulcers." / "One way to assess its influence is to measure the intensity of medical surveillance in the different study groups, and to adjust for it in statistical analyses."
- Key point: Patients taking a drug that causes stomach discomfort may undergo gastroscopy more often and so have more ulcers detected, even if the drug does not cause ulcers (detection bias). You can measure how intensively each group receives medical examinations and adjust for it in the analysis.

### E28

- Location: Box 3. Bias
- Quote: "Such bias may be countered by using cases and controls that were referred in the same way to the diagnostic service"
- Key point: Using cases and controls referred to the diagnostic service in the same way can counter this kind of selection bias.

### E29

- Location: Box 5. Confounding
- Quote: "Investigators should think beforehand about potential confounding factors. This will inform the study design and allow proper data collection by identifying the confounders for which detailed information should be sought. Restriction or matching may be used."
- Key point: Researchers should think about potential confounders beforehand; this guides the study design and identifies which confounders need detailed data collection. At the design stage, restriction or matching can be used.

### E30

- Location: Box 5. Confounding
- Quote: "In the analysis phase, investigators may use stratification or multivariable analysis to reduce the effect of confounders."
- Key point: In the analysis phase, stratification or multivariable analysis can reduce the effect of confounders.

### E31

- Location: Item 16 (Main results), Explanation
- Quote: "Decisions about excluding or including variables should be guided by knowledge, or explicit assumptions, on causal relations. Inappropriate decisions may introduce bias, for example by including variables that are in the causal pathway between exposure and disease"
- Key point: Whether to put a variable in the model should be guided by knowledge of, or explicit assumptions about, causal relations; wrong decisions can introduce bias, for example including variables on the causal pathway from exposure to disease.

### E32

- Location: Item 16 (Main results), Explanation
- Quote: "If the decision to include a variable in the model was based on the change in the estimate, it is important to report what change was considered sufficiently important to justify its inclusion."
- Key point: If variables were included based on how much they change the estimate, report what size of change was considered enough to justify inclusion.

### E33

- Location: Item 12 (Statistical methods), Explanation
- Quote: "investigators should pre-determine analyses at least for the primary study objectives in a study protocol." / "When a study is reported, authors should tell readers whether particular analyses were suggested by data inspection."
- Key point: Pre-specify in the study protocol at least the analyses for the primary study objectives; when reporting, say which analyses were suggested by looking at the data.

### E34

- Location: Item 16 (Main results), Explanation
- Quote: "Readers can compare unadjusted measures of association with those adjusted for potential confounders and judge by how much, and in what direction, they changed. Readers may think that ‘adjusted’ results equal the causal part of the measure of association, but adjusted results are not necessarily free of random sampling error, selection bias, information bias, or residual confounding"
- Key point: Reporting both unadjusted and adjusted estimates lets readers see how much, and in which direction, adjustment changed the result; adjusted results are not "the causal part" and can still contain random error, selection bias, information bias, or residual confounding.

### E35

- Location: Box 6. Missing data: problems and possible solutions
- Quote: "Although such ‘complete-case’ analyses are unbiased in many circumstances, they can be biased and are always inefficient" / "The plausibility of assumptions made in missing data analyses is generally unverifiable." / "Such analyses are therefore best viewed in the spirit of sensitivity analysis"
- Key point: Analyzing only participants with complete data (complete-case analysis) is unbiased in many situations but can be biased and is always inefficient; the assumptions behind missing-data analyses are generally unverifiable, so such analyses are best treated as sensitivity analyses.

### E36

- Location: Item 12 (d) Cohort study: loss to follow-up, Explanation
- Quote: "Investigators therefore need to decide, ideally at the stage of planning the study, how they will deal with loss to follow-up."
- Key point: Ideally, decide how to deal with loss to follow-up when planning the study.

### E37

- Location: Item 12 (e) Sensitivity analyses, Explanation
- Quote: "Sensitivity analyses are useful to investigate whether or not the main results are consistent with those obtained with alternative analysis strategies or assumptions" / "Issues that may be examined include the criteria for inclusion in analyses, the definitions of exposures or outcomes"
- Key point: Sensitivity analyses check whether the main results hold under alternative analysis strategies or assumptions; they can examine inclusion criteria, definitions of exposure or outcome, and more (the original also lists which confounders to adjust for, handling of missing data, possible selection and measurement bias, and treatment of quantitative variables).

### E38

- Location: Item 12 (e) Sensitivity analyses, Explanation
- Quote: "In 1959 Cornfield et al. famously showed that a relative risk of 9 for cigarette smoking and lung cancer was extremely unlikely to be due to any conceivable confounder, since the confounder would need to be at least nine times as prevalent in smokers as in non-smokers"
- Key point: In 1959 Cornfield et al. showed that a relative risk of 9 for smoking and lung cancer was extremely unlikely to be due to any conceivable confounder, because the confounder would need to be at least nine times as prevalent in smokers as in nonsmokers.

### E39

- Location: Item 12 (e) Sensitivity analyses, Explanation
- Quote: "One important, perhaps under recognised, use of sensitivity analysis is when a study shows little or no association between an exposure and an outcome and it is plausible that confounding or other biases toward the null are present."
- Key point: A possibly under-recognized use of sensitivity analysis: when a study finds little or no association between exposure and outcome, and confounding or other biases toward the null are plausible.

### E40

- Location: Item 14 (Descriptive data), Explanation
- Quote: "In case-control studies potential confounders cannot be judged by comparing cases and controls. Control persons represent the source population and will usually be different from the cases in many respects."
- Key point: In case-control studies, confounding cannot be judged by comparing cases and controls; controls represent the source population and usually differ from cases in many respects.

### E41

- Location: Item 19 (Limitations), Explanation
- Quote: "It is important not only to identify the sources of bias and confounding that could have affected results, but also to discuss the relative importance of different biases, including the likely direction and magnitude of any potential bias"
- Key point: When discussing limitations, don't just list possible sources of bias and confounding; discuss the relative importance of the different biases, including their likely direction and magnitude.

### E42

- Location: Item 20 (Interpretation), Explanation
- Quote: "Authors should address the real range of uncertainty in estimates, which is larger than the statistical uncertainty reflected in confidence intervals."
- Key point: Authors should address the real range of uncertainty in estimates, which is larger than the statistical uncertainty reflected in confidence intervals.

### E43

- Location: Item 20 (Interpretation), Explanation
- Quote: "These criteria should not, however, be applied mechanically. For example, some have argued that relative risks below 2 or 3 should be ignored" / "Although a causal effect is more likely with a relative risk of 9, it does not follow that one below 3 is necessarily spurious."
- Key point: The Bradford Hill criteria should not be applied mechanically; some have argued that relative risks below 2 or 3 should be ignored, but a relative risk below 3 is not necessarily spurious.

### E44

- Location: Item 10 (Study size), Explanation
- Quote: "If an analysis is performed on data that were already available for other purposes, the main question is whether the analysis of the data will produce results with sufficient statistical precision to contribute substantially to the literature, and sample size considerations will be informal." / "Do not bother readers with post hoc justifications for study size or retrospective power calculations"
- Key point: When analyzing data already collected for other purposes, the key question is whether the results will be precise enough, and sample size considerations can be informal; do not justify the sample size after the fact or run post hoc power calculations.

### E45

- Location: Item 8 (Data sources/measurement), Example 2
- Quote: "Samples pertaining to matched cases and controls were always analyzed together in the same batch and laboratory personnel were unable to distinguish among cases and controls"
- Key point: Example: samples from matched cases and controls were always analyzed together in the same batch, and laboratory staff could not tell cases from controls.

### E46

- Location: Box 6. Missing data: problems and possible solutions
- Quote: "Inserting a missing category indicator for a confounder may increase residual confounding"
- Key point: Adding a "missing" category indicator for a confounder may increase residual confounding.

### E47

- Location: Item 20 (Interpretation), Explanation
- Quote: "Variables used to measure SES (income, education, or occupation) are surrogates for other undefined and unmeasured exposures, and the true confounder will by definition be measured with error"
- Key point: Income, education, and occupation are only surrogates for socioeconomic status, so the true confounder is by definition measured with error.
