# Boateng et al. 2018: Best Practices for Developing and Validating Scales for Health, Social, and Behavioral Research: A Primer

- Source: `boateng2018best`, [Original (open access on PubMed Central)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6004510/)
- Version: Frontiers in Public Health 6:149 (2018), open access. Locations are given as steps and subheadings.

### E1

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "Scales are a manifestation of latent constructs; they measure behaviors, attitudes, and hypothetical scenarios we expect to exist as a result of our theoretical understanding of the world, but cannot assess directly"
- Key point: Scales are a manifestation of latent constructs: they measure behaviors, attitudes, and the like that theory says should exist but that cannot be measured directly.

### E2

- Location: Scale development overview
- Quote: "There are three phases to creating a rigorous scale—item development, scale development, and scale evaluation" / "The last phase, scale evaluation, requires: (7) tests of dimensionality, (8) tests of reliability, and (9) tests of validity."
- Key point: Scale development has three phases (item development, scale development, scale evaluation) and nine steps; the last phase tests dimensionality, reliability, and validity.

### E3

- Location: Step 1, Domain identification
- Quote: "the domain being examined should be decided upon and defined before any item activity" / "Where there is a similar instrument in existence, you need to justify why the development of a new instrument is appropriate and how it will differ from existing instruments."
- Key point: Define the domain (construct) before writing any items; if a similar instrument already exists, justify why a new one is needed and how it will differ.

### E4

- Location: Step 1, Item generation
- Quote: "It is considered best practice to combine both deductive and inductive methods to both define the domain and identify the questions to assess it."
- Key point: Best practice combines deductive item generation (from the literature and existing scales) with inductive generation (from target-population responses in interviews, focus groups, and the like).

### E5

- Location: Step 1, Item generation
- Quote: "Kline and Schinka et al. note that the initial pool of items developed should be at minimum twice as long as the desired final scale"
- Key point: The initial item pool should be at least twice as long as the final scale.

### E6

- Location: Step 1, Item generation
- Quote: "items should be worded simply and unambiguously. Items should not be offensive or potentially biased in terms of social identity, i.e., gender, religion, ethnicity, race, economic status, or sexual orientation"
- Key point: Word items simply and unambiguously; they must not be offensive or biased with respect to social identity.

### E7

- Location: Step 1, Item generation (common pitfalls)
- Quote: "One pitfall in the identification of domain and item generation is the improper conceptualization and definition of the domain(s)." / "Caution should also be taken to avoid construct underrepresentation, which is when a scale does not capture important aspects of a construct because its focus is too narrow"
- Key point: A common pitfall is poor conceptualization and definition of the construct; also avoid construct underrepresentation, where the scale is too narrow and misses important aspects of the construct.

### E8

- Location: Step 2, Evaluation by experts
- Quote: "Ideally, one should combine expert and target population judgment." / "These expert judges should be independent of those who developed the item pool." / "Multiple judges have been used (typically ranging from 5 to 7)"
- Key point: Content validity is best judged by both experts and the target population; expert judges should be independent of those who wrote the items, typically 5 to 7 of them.

### E9

- Location: Step 3, Cognitive interviews
- Quote: "Cognitive interviewing entails the administration of draft survey questions to target populations and then asking the respondents to verbalize the mental process entailed in providing such answers"
- Key point: Cognitive interviewing: give draft questions to members of the target population, then ask them to verbalize their thought process while answering.

### E10

- Location: Step 3, Cognitive interviews
- Quote: "A range of 5–15 interviews in two to three rounds, or until saturation, or relatively few new insights emerge is considered ideal for pre-testing" / "Outcomes of cognitive interviews should always be reported, along with solutions used to remedy the situation."
- Key point: Pre-testing ideally uses 5-15 interviews in two to three rounds, or continues until saturation; always report the outcomes of cognitive interviews and the changes made in response.

### E11

- Location: Step 4, Establishing the sample size
- Quote: "The rule of thumb has been at least 10 participants for each scale item" / "In sum, there is no single item-ratio that works for all survey development scenarios."
- Key point: A common rule of thumb is at least 10 participants per item, but no single participant-to-item ratio works for every situation.

### E12

- Location: Step 4, Determining the type of data to use
- Quote: "the ideal procedure is to develop the scale on sample A, whether cross-sectional or longitudinal, and then test it on an independent sample B."
- Key point: Ideally, develop the scale on sample A and test it on an independent sample B.

### E13

- Location: Step 5, Inter-item and item-total correlations
- Quote: "However, the adjusted item-total correlation, which examines the correlation between the item and the sum score of the rest of the items excluding itself is preferred" / "Items with very low adjusted item-total correlations (< 0.30) are less desirable and could be a cue for potential deletion from the tentative scale."
- Key point: In item analysis, prefer the adjusted item-total correlation (the item's correlation with the sum of the other items); items below 0.30 can be considered for removal.

### E14

- Location: Step 6, Extraction of factors
- Quote: "A number of scales developed stop at this phase and jump to tests of reliability, but the factors extracted at this point only provide a hypothetical structure of the scale."
- Key point: Many scales jump straight from exploratory factor extraction to reliability, but the factors at that point are only a hypothetical structure that still needs testing.

### E15

- Location: Step 6, Extraction of factors
- Quote: "Where feasible, researchers could also assess the optimal number of factors to be drawn from the list of items using either parallel analysis"
- Key point: Where feasible, use methods such as parallel analysis to decide how many factors to retain.

### E16

- Location: Step 7, Confirmatory factor analysis
- Quote: "These techniques include the chi-square test of exact fit, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA ≤ 0.06), Tucker Lewis Index (TLI ≥ 0.95), Comparative Fit Index (CFI ≥ 0.95), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR ≤ 0.08)"
- Key point: The authors list common fit indices and cutoffs for confirmatory factor analysis.

### E17

- Location: Step 8, Cronbach's alpha
- Quote: "An alpha coefficient of 0.70 has often been regarded as an acceptable threshold for reliability" / "reliability statistics such as Raykov's rho, ordinal alpha, and Revelle's beta, which are debated to have improvements over Cronbach's alpha, are beginning to gain acceptance."
- Key point: An alpha coefficient of 0.70 is often treated as the acceptable threshold; some reliability statistics argued to improve on alpha are starting to gain acceptance.

### E18

- Location: Step 8, Test–retest reliability
- Quote: "The test–retest reliability, also known as the coefficient of stability, is used to assess the degree to which the participants' performance is repeatable, i.e., how consistent their sum scores are across time"
- Key point: Test-retest reliability checks whether the same people's sum scores stay consistent when they are tested again after a time interval.

### E19

- Location: Step 9, Tests of validity
- Quote: "validation is an ongoing process that starts with the identification and definition of the domain of study (Step 1) and continues to its generalizability with other constructs (Step 9)"
- Key point: Validation is an ongoing process that starts with defining the construct and continues through testing its relations to other constructs.

### E20

- Location: Step 9, Construct validity
- Quote: "Evidence of convergent validity of a construct can be provided by the extent to which the newly developed scale correlates highly with other variables designed to measure the same construct" / "The newly developed construct can be invalidated by too high correlations with other tests which are intended to differ in their measurements"
- Key point: Convergent validity: a new scale should correlate highly with other instruments measuring the same construct. Discriminant validity: too high a correlation with constructs that should be different invalidates the new scale.

### E21

- Location: Step 9, Construct validity (known-groups comparison)
- Quote: "we found households with E. coli present in their drinking water had higher mean water insecurity scores than households that had no E. coli in drinking water."
- Key point: A known-groups example: households with E. coli in their drinking water scored higher on the water insecurity scale, consistent with existing knowledge.

### E22

- Location: Step 9, Criterion validity
- Quote: "Predictive validity can be estimated by examining the association between the scale scores and the criterion in question."
- Key point: Predictive validity is estimated from the association between scale scores and the relevant criterion.
