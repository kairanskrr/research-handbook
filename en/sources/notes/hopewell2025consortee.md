# Hopewell et al. 2025: CONSORT 2025 explanation and elaboration: updated guideline for reporting randomised trials

- Source: `hopewell2025consortee`, [BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-081124); open-access full text at [PubMed Central PMC11995452](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11995452/)
- Version: BMJ 2025;389:e081124. The explanation and elaboration companion to the CONSORT 2025 statement, explaining the meaning, rationale, and empirical evidence for each checklist item. The web version has no page numbers; locations give checklist item numbers.

### E1

- Location: Item 17a, Box 5 (Treatment allocation in trials)
- Quote: "Firstly, when properly implemented, it eliminates selection bias, balancing both known and unknown prognostic factors, in the assignment of treatments."
- Key point: Properly implemented randomization eliminates selection bias in assigning treatments, balancing known and unknown prognostic factors across groups.

### E2

- Location: Item 9 (Trial design), Explanation
- Quote: "the main alternative designs are multi-arm parallel, crossover, cluster, and factorial designs, with each having their own specific CONSORT extensions"
- Key point: Besides the two-group parallel design, the main alternative designs are multi-arm parallel, crossover, cluster, and factorial designs, each with its own CONSORT extension.

### E3

- Location: Item 7 (Specific objectives), Explanation
- Quote: "The PICO framework, which requires defining the patient population (P); the experimental intervention (I); the comparator intervention or condition (C); and the outcome or outcomes (O) of interest, has been proposed to help define the research question."
- Key point: The PICO framework, which asks you to define the patient population (P), experimental intervention (I), comparator intervention or condition (C), and outcomes of interest (O), helps frame the research question.

### E4

- Location: Item 7, Explanation
- Quote: "The objectives should be phrased using neutral wording (eg, “to compare the effect of treatment A versus treatment B on outcome X for persons with condition Y”) rather than in terms of a particular direction of effect."
- Key point: State objectives in neutral wording (compare the effect of A versus B on outcome X in people with condition Y), without presupposing the direction of the effect.

### E5

- Location: Item 7, Explanation
- Quote: "Authors should clarify whether the aim is to establish superiority of the experimental intervention, or non-inferiority or equivalence, as compared with the comparator intervention."
- Key point: Make clear whether the aim is to show the experimental intervention is superior (superiority), or non-inferior or equivalent to the comparator.

### E6

- Location: Item 7, Explanation
- Quote: "Finally, trials can be designed to study the effect of the experimental intervention under different conditions, often described on a spectrum from ideal conditions (explanatory trial) to standard clinical care conditions (pragmatic trial)."
- Key point: Trials can study an intervention under different conditions, on a spectrum from ideal conditions (explanatory trial) to routine clinical care (pragmatic trial).

### E7

- Location: Item 14 (Outcomes), Explanation
- Quote: "The primary outcome is the prespecified outcome considered to be of greatest importance to relevant stakeholders (such as patients, policy makers, clinicians, and funders) and should be the one used in the sample size calculation (item 16)."
- Key point: The primary outcome is the pre-specified outcome of greatest importance to stakeholders such as patients, policymakers, clinicians, and funders, and the sample size should be calculated for it.

### E8

- Location: Item 14, Explanation
- Quote: "Most trials have a single primary outcome. Having several primary outcomes can incur potential problems of interpretation associated with multiplicity of analyses"
- Key point: Most trials have a single primary outcome; several primary outcomes raise interpretation problems from multiplicity of analyses.

### E9

- Location: Item 14, Explanation
- Quote: "For each outcome, it is important to detail: (1) the specific measurement variable" [...] "(2) the specific participant level analysis metric" [...] "(3) the method of aggregation" [...] "and (4) the measurement time point of interest for analysis."
- Key point: For each outcome, state four things: the specific measurement variable, the participant-level analysis metric (such as change from baseline), the method of aggregation (such as mean or proportion), and the time point analyzed.

### E10

- Location: Item 14, Explanation
- Quote: "A core outcome set is an agreed standardised set of outcomes that should be measured and reported, as a minimum, in all clinical trials in specific areas of health or health care."
- Key point: A core outcome set is an agreed standard set of outcomes that all clinical trials in a given area should measure and report, as a minimum.

### E11

- Location: Item 7 (Specific objectives), Explanation
- Quote: "Trials whose primary objective is to evaluate benefits of an intervention may not be powered to detect harms, but authors should still report whether they considered harms outcomes when planning the trial."
- Key point: Trials designed mainly to evaluate benefits may not be powered to detect harms, but should still report whether harms outcomes were considered when planning the trial.

### E12

- Location: Item 14, Explanation
- Quote: "Evidence shows important discrepancies between the outcome reported in the registry or protocol and outcomes reported in final publications, frequently in favour of statistically significant results."
- Key point: Evidence shows that outcomes in registries or protocols often differ from those reported in final publications, frequently in favor of statistically significant results.

### E13

- Location: Item 10 (Changes to trial), Explanation
- Quote: "A comparison of protocols and publications of 102 randomised trials found that 62% of trial reports had at least one primary outcome that was changed, introduced, or omitted compared with the protocol."
- Key point: A comparison of protocols and publications of 102 randomized trials found that 62% of reports had at least one primary outcome changed, introduced, or omitted compared with the protocol.

### E14

- Location: Item 16a (Sample size), Explanation
- Quote: "A trial should therefore be sufficiently large to have a high probability (power) of identifying a clinically important difference of a prespecified size that meets a criterion of statistical significance, if such a difference exists."
- Key point: A trial should be large enough to have a high probability (power) of detecting a clinically important difference of a pre-specified size, if such a difference exists.

### E15

- Location: Item 16a, Explanation
- Quote: "Elements of the sample size calculation that need to be specified are the primary outcome (and time point) on which the calculation was based" [...] "the statistical test; the α (type I error) value and whether it is two sided; the statistical power (or the β (type II error) value); and the resulting target sample size per trial group"
- Key point: The sample size calculation should state the primary outcome (and time point) it is based on, the expected outcome values in each group (i.e., the target difference), the statistical test, the alpha level and whether it is two-sided, the power, and the target sample size per group.

### E16

- Location: Item 16a, Explanation
- Quote: "Details should be given of any inflation of the sample size made for attrition or non-adherence during the study."
- Key point: Give details of any inflation of the sample size for attrition or non-adherence.

### E17

- Location: Item 16a, Explanation
- Quote: "larger sample sizes are needed to detect smaller differences. Moreover, the inverse relationship is not linear: very small differences require enormous sample sizes to have good power to detect."
- Key point: The smaller the difference, the larger the sample needed; the relationship is not linear, and very small differences require enormous samples.

### E18

- Location: Item 16a, Explanation
- Quote: "There is no value in conducting and reporting a post hoc calculation of statistical power using the results of a trial, for example, as a pretext to explain non-significant findings; this may even mislead and confuse readers."
- Key point: Post hoc power calculations based on the trial's results have no value (for example, as a pretext to explain non-significant findings) and may even mislead readers.

### E19

- Location: Item 17a, Explanation
- Quote: "Authors should specify the method of sequence generation, such as a random-number table or a computerised random number generator."
- Key point: Specify how the sequence was generated, for example with a random-number table or a computerized random number generator.

### E20

- Location: Item 17a, Explanation
- Quote: "“random” is sometimes used inappropriately in the literature to describe trials in which non-random, “deterministic” allocation methods were used, such as alternation, hospital numbers, or date of birth." / "Even the term “quasi-random” is unacceptable for describing such trials."
- Key point: Alternation, or allocation by hospital number or date of birth, is not a random method; it cannot be called "random," or even "quasi-random."

### E21

- Location: Item 17b (Type of randomisation), Explanation
- Quote: "In trials of several hundred participants or more, simple randomisation can usually be trusted to generate similar numbers in the two trial groups" [...] "For smaller trials of fewer than around 200 participants," [...] "some form of restricted randomisation procedure to help achieve balance between groups in size or characteristics may be useful"
- Key point: In trials of several hundred participants or more, simple randomization can usually be trusted to produce similar group sizes; for small trials of fewer than about 200 participants, some form of restricted randomization may help balance the groups.

### E22

- Location: Item 17b, Box 6 (Randomisation and minimisation)
- Quote: "Although the order of interventions varies randomly within each block, a person running the trial could deduce some of the next treatment allocations if they discovered the block size." / "Blinding the interventions, using larger block sizes, and randomly varying the block size can ameliorate this problem."
- Key point: In block randomization, anyone who knows the block size can deduce some of the next allocations; blinding the interventions, using larger blocks, and randomly varying block sizes help.

### E23

- Location: Item 17b, Box 6
- Quote: "Stratification by centre is common in multicentre trials. Stratification requires some form of restriction, such as blocking within strata. Stratification without some form of restriction is ineffective."
- Key point: Multicenter trials often stratify by center; stratification must be combined with some form of restriction (such as blocking within strata), or it is ineffective.

### E24

- Location: Item 17b, Box 6
- Quote: "Minimisation offers the only acceptable alternative to randomisation, and some have argued that it is superior."
- Key point: Minimization is the only acceptable alternative to randomization.

### E25

- Location: Item 17a, Explanation
- Quote: "Successful randomisation hinges on two steps: generation of an unpredictable allocation sequence and concealment of this sequence from the investigators enrolling participants (item 18)."
- Key point: Successful randomization depends on two steps: generating an unpredictable allocation sequence and concealing it from the investigators enrolling participants.

### E26

- Location: Item 18 (Allocation concealment), Explanation
- Quote: "The decision to accept or reject a participant should be made, and informed consent should be obtained from the participant, in ignorance of the next assignment in the sequence."
- Key point: Deciding whether to accept a participant, and obtaining their informed consent, should both happen without knowing the next assignment.

### E27

- Location: Item 18, Explanation
- Quote: "Centralised or third party assignment is especially desirable." / "Use of a pharmacy or central computer or telephone randomisation system are common techniques."
- Key point: Centralized or third-party assignment is especially desirable; pharmacies, central computers, or telephone randomization systems are common.

### E28

- Location: Item 18, Explanation
- Quote: "Enclosing assignments in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes can be a good allocation concealment mechanism if it is developed and monitored diligently." / "Investigators should ensure that the envelopes are opaque when held to the light, and are opened sequentially and only after the participant’s name and other details are written on the appropriate sequentially numbered sealed envelope."
- Key point: Sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes can be a good concealment mechanism if developed and monitored diligently; make sure they cannot be read against the light, are opened in order, and have the participant's name and details written on them before opening.

### E29

- Location: Item 19 (Implementation), Explanation
- Quote: "investigators should strive for complete separation of the people involved with generation and allocation concealment from the people who implement assignments."
- Key point: The people who generate the sequence and conceal the allocation should be completely separate from the people who implement the assignments.

### E30

- Location: Item 18, Explanation
- Quote: "Trials in which the allocation sequence had been inadequately or unclearly concealed yielded larger estimates of treatment effects than did trials in which authors reported adequate allocation concealment."
- Key point: Trials with inadequate or unclear allocation concealment yielded larger estimates of treatment effects than trials with adequate concealment.

### E31

- Location: Item 18, Explanation
- Quote: "Allocation concealment should not be confused with blinding (item 20). Allocation concealment seeks to prevent selection bias (box 5), protects the assignment sequence before and until allocation, and can always be successfully implemented." / "In contrast, blinding seeks to prevent ascertainment bias, protects the sequence after allocation, and cannot always be implemented."
- Key point: Allocation concealment is not blinding: concealment prevents selection bias, protects the sequence before allocation, and can always be done; blinding prevents measurement bias, protects information after allocation, and is not always possible.

### E32

- Location: Item 20a (Blinding), Explanation
- Quote: "The term “blinding” (masking) refers to withholding information about the assigned interventions from people involved in the trial who may potentially be influenced by this knowledge."
- Key point: Blinding means withholding information about the assigned interventions from people in the trial who might be influenced by it.

### E33

- Location: Item 20a, Explanation
- Quote: "Unblinded outcome assessors may differentially assess subjective outcomes, and unblinded data analysts may introduce bias through the choice of analytical strategies, such as the selection of favourable time points or outcomes and by decisions to remove patients from the analyses."
- Key point: Unblinded outcome assessors may assess subjective outcomes differently between groups; unblinded data analysts may introduce bias by choosing favorable time points or outcomes, or by excluding patients.

### E34

- Location: Item 20a, Explanation
- Quote: "In certain trials, especially surgical trials, blinding of participants and healthcare providers is often difficult or impossible, but blinding of data collectors and outcome assessors for both benefits and harms is often achievable and recommended."
- Key point: In trials such as surgical trials, blinding participants and healthcare providers is often impossible, but blinding data collectors and outcome assessors is often feasible and recommended.

### E35

- Location: Item 20b, Explanation
- Quote: "they should provide information about the mechanism used to establish blinding (eg, placebo identical to the experimental intervention, sham intervention, sham surgery)."
- Key point: Describe how blinding was achieved, for example a placebo identical to the experimental intervention, a sham intervention, or sham surgery.

### E36

- Location: Item 20b, Explanation
- Quote: "Use of a fixed code will increase the risk of unblinding because unblinding a participant could result in unblinding several or all trial participants."
- Key point: Using a fixed code (such as A = group 1) increases the risk of unblinding, because unblinding one participant can unblind several or all of them.

### E37

- Location: Item 20a, Box 7 (Blinding terminology)
- Quote: "a study of 200 randomised trials reported as double blind demonstrated 18 different combinations of groups actually blinded when the authors of these trials were surveyed" / "Authors should instead explicitly report the blinding status of the people involved for whom blinding may influence the validity of a trial."
- Key point: When the authors of 200 trials reported as "double blind" were surveyed, 18 different combinations of groups had actually been blinded; so state explicitly whether each relevant group was blinded.

### E38

- Location: Item 3 (Protocol and statistical analysis plan), Explanation
- Quote: "The protocol should be signed off by the trial steering committee before the allocation of any participants and data collection. Similarly, the full statistical analysis plan should be signed off by the trial steering committee before the dataset is closed for analysis."
- Key point: The trial steering committee should sign off the protocol before any participants are allocated and data collected, and sign off the full statistical analysis plan before the database is locked and analysis begins.

### E39

- Location: Item 16b (Interim analyses and stopping), Explanation
- Quote: "If an intervention demonstrates exceptional efficacy, the study might require early termination on ethical grounds. To mitigate this concern, assessing results as data accumulates is advisable, ideally through an independent data monitoring committee (DMC), sometimes referred to as a data and safety monitoring board (DSMB)."
- Key point: If an intervention shows exceptional efficacy, the trial may need to stop early on ethical grounds; so it is advisable to assess results as data accumulate, ideally through an independent data monitoring committee.

### E40

- Location: Item 16b, Explanation
- Quote: "Further to stopping early for efficacy, interim analyses can be used to evaluate (1) futility, to assess whether a trial is likely to meet its objectives; or (2) safety, to assess whether there is evidence for increased risk of harms"
- Key point: Besides stopping early for efficacy, interim analyses can assess futility (whether the trial is likely to meet its objectives) and safety (whether there is evidence of increased risk of harm).

### E41

- Location: Item 16b, Explanation
- Quote: "Various group sequential statistical approaches exist to adjust for multiple looks (ie, analyses) at the data, and these should be predetermined in the trial protocol"
- Key point: Various group sequential methods adjust for multiple looks at the data, and they should be pre-specified in the trial protocol.

### E42

- Location: Item 10, Explanation
- Quote: "Authors should report the nature and timing of protocol changes because changes made at different times (eg, before or after breaking the blinding) might be associated with different risks of bias."
- Key point: Report the nature and timing of protocol changes, because changes made at different times (e.g., before or after breaking the blinding) carry different risks of bias.

### E43

- Location: Item 16b, Explanation
- Quote: "For instance, examining data from a trial at five interim analyses using a P value of 0.05 would elevate the overall false-positive rate closer to 19% rather than the expected 5%."
- Key point: Five interim analyses each using P = 0.05 push the overall false-positive rate close to 19%, not the expected 5%.

### E44

- Location: Item 21b (Analysis population), Explanation
- Quote: "To preserve the benefits of randomisation, all randomised participants are included in the analysis and retained in the group to which they were allocated. Meeting these two conditions defines an intention-to-treat analysis—which is widely recommended as the preferred analysis strategy."
- Key point: To preserve the benefits of randomization, include all randomized participants in the analysis and keep them in their allocated groups; meeting both conditions defines an intention-to-treat analysis, which is widely recommended as the preferred approach.

### E45

- Location: Item 21b, Explanation
- Quote: "Excluding participants may compromise the randomisation and lead to biased estimates of treatment effects if appropriate methods are not used." / "authors should clearly define which participants are included in each analysis and in which intervention group and avoid terms such as “modified intention-to-treat” or “per protocol” analysis."
- Key point: Excluding participants can compromise randomization and bias the effect estimates; state who is included in each analysis and in which group, rather than using only labels such as "modified intention-to-treat" or "per-protocol."

### E46

- Location: Item 21c (Missing data), Explanation
- Quote: "While a few missing outcomes will not cause a problem, many trials have more than 10% of randomised patients with missing outcomes."
- Key point: A few missing outcomes are not a problem, but many trials have outcomes missing for more than 10% of randomized patients.

### E47

- Location: Item 21b, Explanation
- Quote: "Striving for intention-to-treat analysis by imputing values for participants with missing outcomes may lead to use of inadequate methods such as last observation carried forward."
- Key point: Imputing missing outcomes just to achieve an intention-to-treat analysis can lead to inadequate methods such as last observation carried forward.

### E48

- Location: Item 21a (Statistical methods), Explanation
- Quote: "it is often advisable to make adjustments for stratification variables" / "In the context of randomised trials, the decision to make adjustments should not be based on whether there are baseline covariates that are statistically significantly different between randomised groups."
- Key point: It is often advisable to adjust for stratification variables; whether to adjust for a covariate should not depend on whether it differs significantly between groups.

### E49

- Location: Item 21d (Additional analyses), Explanation
- Quote: "It is incorrect to infer a subgroup effect (interaction) from one significant (in one subgroup) and one non-significant P value (in another subgroup)." / "Post hoc subgroup comparisons (analyses done after looking at the data) are especially likely not to be confirmed by further studies."
- Key point: One significant subgroup and one non-significant subgroup do not show a subgroup effect; subgroup comparisons made after looking at the data are especially unlikely to be confirmed by later studies.

### E50

- Location: Item 25 (Baseline data), Explanation
- Quote: "Significance testing of baseline differences is not recommended and should not be reported." / "providing the randomisation has not been subverted or comprised, any differences are caused by chance."
- Key point: Significance tests of baseline differences are not recommended and should not be reported; as long as randomization was not subverted, any baseline differences are due to chance.

### E51

- Location: Item 26, Explanation
- Quote: "Results should be reported for all planned primary and secondary outcomes and at each time point, not just for analyses that were statistically significant or thought to be interesting."
- Key point: Report results for all planned primary and secondary outcomes at every time point, not just those that were significant or "interesting."

### E52

- Location: Item 26 (Outcomes and estimation), Explanation
- Quote: "For all outcomes, authors should provide a CI to indicate the precision (uncertainty) of the estimated effect size." / "results should not be reported solely as P values."
- Key point: Give confidence intervals for the effect sizes of all outcomes; do not report P values alone.

### E53

- Location: Item 26, Explanation
- Quote: "When the primary outcome is binary, both the relative effect (risk ratio (relative risk) or odds ratio), and the absolute effect (risk difference) should be reported (with CIs)"
- Key point: When the primary outcome is binary, report both the relative effect (risk ratio or odds ratio) and the absolute effect (risk difference), with confidence intervals.

### E54

- Location: Item 22a (Participant flow), Explanation
- Quote: "Erroneous conclusions can be reached if participants are excluded from analysis, and imbalances in such omissions between groups may be especially indicative of bias."
- Key point: Excluding participants from analysis can lead to wrong conclusions; unequal exclusions between groups are especially indicative of bias.

### E55

- Location: Item 29 (Interpretation), Explanation
- Quote: "some discussion sections can be overly optimistic when discussing the trial findings (interpretation bias; spin)."
- Key point: Some discussion sections are overly optimistic about the findings: interpretation bias, or "spin."

### E56

- Location: Item 20a, Explanation
- Quote: "Regardless of whether blinding is possible, authors can and should always state who was blinded"
- Key point: Whether or not blinding is possible, always state who was blinded.

### E57

- Location: Item 2, Explanation (citing the World Health Organization's 2015 statement on public disclosure of trial results)
- Quote: "the key outcomes are to be made publicly available within 12 months of study completion by posting to the results section of the primary clinical trial registry."
- Key point: The World Health Organization's 2015 statement requires key outcomes to be posted to the results section of the primary clinical trial registry within 12 months of study completion.

### E58

- Location: Item 20a, Explanation
- Quote: "In pragmatic trials (trials that try to make the experience as close as real life so as to understand real world effectiveness), blinding of participants and healthcare providers would decrease the pragmatism of the trials, since patients in real life are not blinded."
- Key point: In pragmatic trials, blinding participants and healthcare providers makes the trial less like real life, because patients are not blinded in practice.

### E59

- Location: Item 20a, Explanation
- Quote: "For example, blinding of data collectors and outcome assessors is unlikely to matter for objective outcomes, such as death from any cause."
- Key point: For objective outcomes such as death from any cause, blinding data collectors and assessors is unlikely to matter.

### E60

- Location: Item 20b, Explanation
- Quote: "Thus, given the uncertainty this type of information provides, the usefulness of tests of blinding has been questioned."
- Key point: The usefulness of "tests of blinding," which ask participants at the end to guess their group, has been questioned.
