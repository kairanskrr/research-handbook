# Carletto, Gourlay & Winters 2013: From Guesstimates to GPStimates: Land Area Measurement and Implications for Agricultural Analysis

- Source: `carletto2013guesstimates`, [Original (World Bank Open Knowledge Repository)](https://hdl.handle.net/10986/15910), DOI [10.1596/1813-9450-6550](https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-6550)
- Version: World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 6550, July 2013, licensed CC BY 3.0 IGO; PDF downloaded from the World Bank Open Knowledge Repository on 2026-10-08. A working paper (not peer reviewed by a journal); the views are the authors' own, not the World Bank's. Data come from the Living Standards Measurement Study-Integrated Surveys on Agriculture (LSMS-ISA) in Malawi, Uganda, Tanzania, and Niger. Locations are given as subsections or the abstract.

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "commonly employed self-reported land area measures used in most analysis are not only potentially measured with error, but these errors may be correlated with agricultural outcomes."
- Key point: The farmer self-reported land areas used in most analyses may not only be measured with error; the errors may also be correlated with agricultural outcomes.

### E2

- Location: §4 Conclusions
- Quote: "The data indicate that small plot areas are generally over reported by farmers while larger plot areas are generally under reported."
- Key point: The data show that farmers generally over-report the area of small plots and under-report the area of large plots.

### E3

- Location: §4 Conclusions
- Quote: "This suggests that in assessing agricultural relationships using control variables will not address measurement error."
- Key point: In analyzing agricultural relationships, adding control variables does not fix this measurement error.

### E4

- Location: §4 Conclusions
- Quote: "The final conclusion then reinforces the arguments made by Gibson and McKenzie (2007) that GPS information should become a routine part of survey data collection since they tend to provide better measure and help to identify causal relationships."
- Key point: The authors conclude that GPS information should become a routine part of survey data collection, because it gives better measures and helps identify causal relationships.

### E5

- Location: §1, footnote 3
- Quote: "Traversing is completed using a tape and compass and is considered the gold standard of land measurement, but its implementation is time-consuming and costly." / "Traversing is therefore infeasible in the context of national household surveys."
- Key point: Traversing (measuring the plot boundary with tape and compass) is considered the gold standard of land measurement, but it is time-consuming and costly and infeasible in national household surveys.

### E6

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "Measures employing Global Positioning Systems, on the other hand, while not perfect especially on smaller plots, are likely to provide more precise measures and errors less correlated with agricultural outcomes."
- Key point: GPS measures are not perfect, especially on small plots, but are likely more precise, with errors less correlated with agricultural outcomes.

### E7

- Location: §1
- Quote: "Meeting these objectives is possible because of recently collected, multicountry data coming out of the Living Standards Measurement Study--Integrated Surveys on Agriculture (LSMS-ISA)." / "We use data from four of the most recently completed surveys--Malawi, Uganda, Tanzania and Niger."
- Key point: The study uses data from the Living Standards Measurement Study-Integrated Surveys on Agriculture (LSMS-ISA) in four countries: Malawi, Uganda, Tanzania, and Niger.

### E8

- Location: §1
- Quote: "Finally, we compare the use of self-reported and GPS land measures in estimating the inverse farm-size productivity relationship to determine the implications for measurement error on agricultural analysis."
- Key point: The authors estimate the inverse farm-size productivity relationship (smaller farms produce more per unit area) with self-reported and with GPS areas and compare the results, to see how measurement error affects agricultural analysis.
