# Kramer, Paparozzi, Stroup 2016: Statistics in a Horticultural Journal: Problems and Solutions

- Source: `kramer2016statistics`, [original](https://doi.org/10.21273/JASHS03747-16)
- Version: Journal of the American Society for Horticultural Science 141(5): 400–406, 2016, CC BY-NC-ND open access. The authors checked the statistics in all 86 papers from every 2014 issue and the first 2015 issue of the journal, one by one. The text was taken from the publisher's page as saved by the Internet Archive. Locations give subheadings.

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "Slightly fewer than half appeared to have problems."
- Key point: Of the 86 papers examined, slightly fewer than half appeared to have statistical problems.

### E2

- Location: Results and Discussion, Statistical problems
- Quote: "The most common problem (30 articles) was inappropriate analysis of data from multiple dependent variables on the same unit of observation."
- Key point: The most common problem (30 articles) was inappropriate analysis of data from multiple dependent variables measured on the same unit of observation.

### E3

- Location: Results and Discussion, Statistical problems
- Quote: "In 11 cases, inspection of the figures revealed an obvious relationship between the mean and variance. Typically, the variance increased with larger means, yet the statistical analysis used a method that requires the assumption of no mean-variance relationship. This suggests a larger problem of failure to verify assumptions."
- Key point: In 11 papers the figures showed an obvious mean-variance relationship (variance rising with the mean), yet the analysis used a method that assumes no such relationship, which points to a failure to verify assumptions.

### E4

- Location: Results and Discussion, Statistical problems
- Quote: "The second most frequent problem listed in Table 2 , (seven instances) concerned inconsistencies between how the data were described (the study design) and how they were analyzed. For example, there may have been constraints on the randomization of the observations, such as blocking in a randomized complete block design, by locations (plots of land) or by occasions (different years), but the analysis used a method that failed to account for these sources of variation."
- Key point: The design imposed restrictions on randomization (blocks, locations, years), but the analysis did not include these sources of variation in the model (seven instances).

### E5

- Location: Results and Discussion, Statistical problems
- Quote: "This requires a confidence interval, or at least a properly estimated standard error of the difference (not a standard error of the mean—they are not interchangeable)."
- Key point: Reporting how much treatments differ requires a confidence interval, or at least a properly estimated standard error of the difference, which is not interchangeable with the standard error of the mean.

### E6

- Location: Results and Discussion, Statistical problems
- Quote: "In many common designs (e.g., any design with blocking), there is no straightforward way to determine the standard error of the difference from the standard error of the mean."
- Key point: In many common designs (e.g., any design with blocking), there is no straightforward way to get the standard error of the difference from the standard error of the mean.

### E7

- Location: Results and Discussion, Software packages
- Quote: "Authors and reviewers should recognize that statistical software is a means of implementing a statistical analysis, not a statistical method in itself."
- Key point: Statistical software is a means of implementing a statistical analysis, not a statistical method in itself.

### E8

- Location: Results and Discussion, Underlying reasons
- Quote: "One good example of this is the concept and implementation of random effects, such as blocks and studies at multiple locations or occasions. These factors were typically modeled as fixed effects until software became available in the early 1990s to model them correctly as random effects."
- Key point: Factors such as blocks and trials at multiple locations or occasions were traditionally treated as fixed effects; only with software available in the early 1990s could they be properly modeled as random effects.

### E9

- Location: Results and Discussion, Underlying reasons
- Quote: "In other cases, reviewers whose knowledge on statistics is dated may provide well-meaning, but inappropriate feedback regarding statistical aspects of the manuscript being reviewed, or, even worse, incorrectly reject the manuscript thinking the statistics are flawed when in fact they are legitimate, just not understood by the reviewer."
- Key point: Reviewers whose statistical knowledge is dated may give well-meaning but inappropriate feedback, even rejecting a paper for supposed statistical errors when the method is correct and the reviewer simply does not know it.

### E10

- Location: Recommendations, Collaboration
- Quote: "plan before you plant! This is crucial: the statistical thinking that goes into planning a study—before any data are collected—whether it is a formally designed experiment, a survey, or an observational study, is the most important use of statistics in research."
- Key point: Plan before you plant. The statistical thinking that goes into planning a study before any data are collected is the most important use of statistics in research.

### E11

- Location: Results and Discussion, Software packages
- Quote: "Sometimes a method was given, but the software used was clearly not capable of implementing the analysis described (e.g., use of SAS PROC GLM to analyze data with random model effects)."
- Key point: Some papers named a method, but the software procedure used could not implement that analysis, e.g., SAS PROC GLM used to analyze data with random effects.

### E12

- Location: Methods
- Quote: "Eighty-six articles from JASHS (all issues in 2014 plus issue 1 in 2015) were examined to characterize the kinds of statistical methodology used and associated problems."
- Key point: The authors examined 86 articles: every 2014 issue of the journal plus issue 1 of 2015.

### E13

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "we suggest that researchers in this field actively seek out opportunities to improve and update their statistical knowledge throughout their careers and engage a statistician as a collaborator early when unfamiliar methods are needed to design or analyze a research study."
- Key point: The authors suggest that horticultural researchers keep updating their statistical knowledge throughout their careers and bring in a statistician as a collaborator early when the design or analysis needs unfamiliar methods.
