# McDonald, Schoenebeck & Forte 2019: Reliability and Inter-rater Reliability in Qualitative Research

- Source: `mcdonald2019reliability`, [version on the author's website](https://yardi.people.si.umich.edu/pubs/Schoenebeck_Reliability_CSCW19.pdf)
- Version: Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction 3 (CSCW), 1–23, 2019, DOI 10.1145/3359174; we read the version on the author's website (with preprint-style headers).

### E1

- Location: §1 Introduction; the second and third quoted passages are from §2.2
- Quote: "IRR is a statistical measure of agreement between two or more coders of data. IRR can be confusing because it merges a quantitative method, which has roots in positivism and objective discovery, with qualitative methods that favor an interpretivist view of knowledge." / "Common statistical calculations used in IRR include Cohen's kappa" / "Krippendorff's alpha"
- Key point: Interrater reliability (IRR) is a statistical measure of agreement between two or more coders, commonly Cohen's Kappa or Krippendorff's Alpha; it can be confusing because it merges a quantitative method rooted in positivism with qualitative methods that lean toward interpretivism.

### E2

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "notably, IRR is rare, occurring in around 1/9 of qualitative papers."
- Key point: Among qualitative papers at CSCW and CHI from 2016 to 2018, only about one in nine calculated interrater reliability.

### E3

- Location: §4.3 Approaches to Reliability by Data Collection Method
- Quote: "Of the 251 papers, 179 (71.3%) described conducting interviews"
- Key point: Of the 251 qualitative papers the authors analyzed, 71.3% used interviews.

### E4

- Location: §2.2 Reliability in Qualitative Research
- Quote: "there is general consensus that simply calculating percent agreement between coders is not acceptable because it does not account for the possibility of agreement by chance."
- Key point: There is general consensus that simply calculating percent agreement between coders is not acceptable, because it does not account for agreement by chance.

### E5

- Location: §2.2 Reliability in Qualitative Research
- Quote: "other techniques for communicating reliability include member checking (e.g., confirming/reviewing results with participants or other members of the community), re-interviewing participants, triangulation of data with secondary data sources (e.g., interviews plus field notes, photographs, etc.), making research process transparent" [...] "and communicating positionality of the researchers."
- Key point: Besides interrater reliability, ways to communicate reliability include member checking, follow-up interviews, corroboration with other data sources, transparency about the research process, and stating the researcher's positionality.

### E6

- Location: §2.2 Reliability in Qualitative Research
- Quote: "Many qualitative researchers reject validity and reliability altogether in favor of concepts like dependability, confirmability, credibility, and transferability that resonate with interpretivist accounts"
- Key point: Many qualitative researchers reject validity and reliability altogether in favor of interpretivist concepts such as dependability, confirmability, credibility, and transferability.

### E7

- Location: §2.3 Critiques and Limitations of Reliability
- Quote: "quantitative researchers have sometimes made "the mistake of evaluating qualitative research reports using the standards of quantitative research," expecting IRR regardless of the nature of the qualitative research"
- Key point: Quantitative researchers sometimes make "the mistake of evaluating qualitative research reports using the standards of quantitative research," demanding interrater reliability regardless of the nature of the study.

### E8

- Location: §2.3 Critiques and Limitations of Reliability
- Quote: "Although statistical measures can help confirm that interpretations are consistent between coders, they are not a substitute for interpretation and making meaning from the data."
- Key point: Statistical measures can help confirm that coders interpret consistently, but they are not a substitute for interpreting the data.

### E9

- Location: §2.3 Critiques and Limitations of Reliability
- Quote: "we argue that approaches to establishing and measuring reliability should be aligned with epistemological traditions the researcher draws on."
- Key point: How reliability is established and measured should align with the epistemological tradition the researcher draws on.

### E10

- Location: §1 Introduction (summary of contributions)
- Quote: "Pragmatic examples include when developing codes is part of the process, when there is a single researcher, when researchers are embedded in the research context, where analysis is driven by participants' own interpretations of their data, or when coding requires little interpretation."
- Key point: Situations where seeking agreement or computing reliability is not appropriate: developing codes is part of the analysis, there is a single researcher, the researcher is embedded in the research setting, the analysis is driven by participants' own interpretations, or coding requires little interpretation.

### E11

- Location: §1 Introduction (summary of contributions)
- Quote: "The use of agreement or IRR should be consistent with methodological choices and analytical goals, which might include ensuring consistency across multiple coders, for applying existing codebooks, and when researchers aim to report quantitative results."
- Key point: Situations where seeking agreement or computing reliability fits: ensuring consistency across multiple coders, applying an existing codebook, or reporting quantitative results.

### E12

- Location: §5.3.4 Describe results quantitatively
- Quote: "If findings hinge on frequency counts, there is a strong argument that IRR should be used."
- Key point: If findings hinge on frequency counts, there is a strong argument for using interrater reliability.

### E13

- Location: §5.1.2 Expert researcher
- Quote: "Many techniques such as memoing, reflection, and review are designed to support the single researcher process without requiring agreement"
- Key point: Techniques such as memoing, reflection, and review are designed for a single researcher and do not require agreement.

### E14

- Location: §5.2.3 Identifying points of disagreement
- Quote: "Disagreement can also prompt more reflection that ultimately results in a stronger codebook and coding process."
- Key point: Disagreement between coders can prompt reflection that ultimately yields a stronger codebook and coding process.

### E15

- Location: §5.3.6 Confirmation Bias

- Key point: Using multiple coders and agreement processes can help reduce a particular researcher's individual biases.
