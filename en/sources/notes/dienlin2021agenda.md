# Dienlin et al. 2021: An Agenda for Open Science in Communication

- Source: `dienlin2021agenda`, [Original PDF (White Rose open archive)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/158440/7/jqz052.pdf)
- Version: Journal of Communication 71(1), 1–26, 2021 (published online 2020), DOI 10.1093/joc/jqz052, open access under Creative Commons CC BY 4.0; locations give section names in the main text.

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "we propose an agenda for adopting open science practices in Communication, which includes the following seven suggestions: (1) publish materials, data, and code; (2) preregister studies and submit registered reports; (3) conduct replications; (4) collaborate; (5) foster open science skills; (6) implement Transparency and Openness Promotion Guidelines; and (7) incentivize open science practices."
- Key point: The authors propose seven open science suggestions for Communication: publish materials, data, and code; preregister studies and submit Registered Reports; conduct replications; collaborate; foster open science skills; implement the Transparency and Openness Promotion (TOP) Guidelines; and incentivize open science practices.

### E2

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "The field of Communication has not yet conducted a large-scale replication project. However, because we employ similar methods to the fields that have already acknowledged these problems, there is reason to believe we face similar issues."
- Key point: Communication has not yet run a large-scale replication project, but it uses methods similar to fields that have acknowledged replicability problems, so there is reason to believe it faces similar ones.

### E3

- Location: Replicability in Communication
- Quote: "studies in leading journals in Communication show evidence of the QRPs discussed above (Matthes et al., 2015)"
- Key point: Studies of leading Communication journals found evidence of questionable research practices.

### E4

- Location: Replicability in Communication
- Quote: "Of all p-values reported in 693 empirical Communication papers published between 2010 and 2012, 8.8% were incorrect; of those, 75% were too low (Vermeulen & Hartmann, 2015)."
- Key point: Of the p-values reported in 693 empirical Communication papers from 2010–2012, 8.8% were incorrect, and 75% of those were too low.

### E5

- Location: Replicability in Communication
- Quote: "from which we can extrapolate that effects are typically tested with 167 participants. However, for most study designs such a small sample size would be insufficient to reliably detect small or moderate effects"
- Key point: Extrapolating from Rains et al.'s 2018 review of Communication meta-analyses, the authors estimate that effects are typically tested with 167 participants, which for most designs is too few to reliably detect small or moderate effects.

### E6

- Location: Open science practices, 3. Conduct replications
- Quote: "In Communication, they represent approximately 1.8% of all published research (Keating & Totzkay, 2019)."
- Key point: In Communication, direct replications make up about 1.8% of published research.

### E7

- Location: Open science practices, 2. Preregister studies and submit registered reports
- Quote: "In Communication, at the time of writing, registered reports are accepted by Communication Research Reports, Computational Communication Research, and Journal of Media Psychology."
- Key point: At the time of writing (around 2020), the Communication journals accepting Registered Reports were Communication Research Reports, Computational Communication Research, and Journal of Media Psychology.

### E8

- Location: Open science practices, 1. Publish materials, data, and code
- Quote: "We recommend that researchers share study materials (e.g., items, stimuli, protocols, instructions, or codebooks for content- and meta-analyses), data (e.g., raw, aggregated, processed, or synthetic), code (e.g., data-gathering, -preparation, or -analysis)"
- Key point: The authors recommend sharing study materials (including codebooks for content analyses), data, and code (including data-collection code).

### E9

- Location: Objections (privacy and copyright)
- Quote: "Specific raw data such as news articles, video material, or data scraped from online platforms sometimes cannot be made public, for example because of copyright reasons"
- Key point: Raw data such as news articles, video, or data scraped from online platforms sometimes cannot be made public, for example for copyright reasons.

### E10

- Location: Objections (privacy and copyright)
- Quote: "Second, researchers can prevent others from identifying participants by (a) removing direct or indirect identifiers (e.g., date of birth), (b) binning (i.e., turning continuous values into categories), or (c) aggregating."
- Key point: Ways to prevent participants from being identified: remove direct or indirect identifiers (such as date of birth), bin continuous values into categories, and aggregate.

### E11

- Location: Objections (privacy and copyright)
- Quote: "A third solution to both privacy and copyright issues is non-consumptive data use (van Atteveldt et al., 2019), which means providing access to the data without physically copying it (e.g., by means of onsite visits)."
- Key point: A third solution is non-consumptive data use: giving others access to the data without copying it (e.g., through onsite visits).

### E12

- Location: Open science and qualitative research
- Quote: "we argue that open science practices can increase the quality of qualitative research primarily by improving transparency and traceability."
- Key point: Open science practices improve qualitative research mainly by increasing transparency and traceability.

### E13

- Location: Open science and qualitative research
- Quote: "Hence, preregistration of qualitative research can be fruitful, too: It increases transparency, tracks flexibility and modification during the research process, and, when submitted as a registered report, prevents publication bias"
- Key point: Qualitative research can be preregistered too: it increases transparency and tracks changes made during the research, and submitting it as a Registered Report prevents publication bias.

### E14

- Location: Objections (end)
- Quote: "Our agenda is not all or nothing: Even an incremental adoption will bring important benefits to our field, and some progress is better than no progress."
- Key point: The agenda is not all or nothing; even partial adoption brings benefits.

### E15

- Location: Replicability in Communication
- Quote: "Similarly, there exist several accounts of publication bias in Communication (Franco, Malhotra, & Simonovits, 2014; Keating & Totzkay, 2019), which indicates a preference for novel and statistically significant results."
- Key point: Communication also has documented publication bias, favoring novel and statistically significant results.

### E16

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "Although we focus mostly on confirmatory and quantitative research, we also (d) reflect on open science practices relevant to other approaches such as qualitative research."
- Key point: The article focuses mainly on confirmatory quantitative research but also discusses qualitative research.
