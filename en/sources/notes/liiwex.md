# Cornell LII Wex: legal entries (ratio decidendi, obiter dictum, distinguish, precedent, stare decisis, persuasive authority, civil law)

- Source: `liiwex`, Wex, the free legal dictionary of the Legal Information Institute (LII) at Cornell Law School (US). Entries read: [ratio decidendi](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/ratio_decidendi), [obiter dictum](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/obiter_dictum), [distinguish](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/distinguish), [precedent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/precedent), [stare decisis](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/stare_decisis), [persuasive authority](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/persuasive_authority), [civil law](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/civil_law)
- Version: read 2026-10-06; each entry notes when it was last reviewed (between 2020 and 2025). Wex is written in the US, and most examples involve **US** courts. Locations give the entry name.

### E1

- Location: ratio decidendi (reviewed August 2023)
- Quote: "The term refers to a key factual point or chain of reasoning in a case that drives the final judgment." / "To determine the ratio decidendi of a case, courts look to the material facts of the earlier case and the law used to justify the decision on those facts."
- Key point: The ratio decidendi is the key factual point or chain of reasoning in a case that drives the final judgment; to determine it, a court looks at the material facts of the earlier case and the law used to justify the decision on those facts.

### E2

- Location: ratio decidendi
- Quote: "Ratio decidendi is the basis for a court decision and creates binding precedent. Ratio decidendi differs from dictum which describes unnecessary observations that have no force as precedent."
- Key point: The ratio decidendi is the basis for the decision and creates binding precedent; dictum is unnecessary observation and has no precedential force.

### E3

- Location: obiter dictum (reviewed July 2020)
- Quote: "A comment, suggestion, or observation made by a judge in an opinion that is not necessary to resolve the case, and as such, it is not legally binding on other courts but may still be cited as persuasive authority in future litigation." / "A dissenting opinion is also generally considered obiter dictum."
- Key point: Obiter dictum is a comment made by a judge in an opinion that is not necessary to resolve the case; it is not binding on other courts but may later be cited as persuasive authority. Dissenting opinions are generally treated as dicta too.

### E4

- Location: obiter dictum
- Quote: "Legal scholars commonly disagree as to what exactly constitutes dicta as opposed to statements of binding precedent or authority in a given case."
- Key point: Legal scholars commonly disagree about what exactly counts as dicta rather than binding precedent in a given case.

### E5

- Location: obiter dictum
- Quote: "Dicta are frequently incorporated in later opinions and sometimes even serve as the basis of those opinions."
- Key point: Dicta are frequently incorporated into later opinions and sometimes even serve as their basis.

### E6

- Location: distinguish (reviewed June 2021)
- Quote: "A party usually distinguishes one case from another as part of the argument that because of a certain distinction, the court’s decision in the first case should not apply as precedent ( stare decisis ) to the second case, despite any factual similarities between the cases."
- Key point: To distinguish a case is to argue that some difference between two cases means the earlier decision should not apply as precedent to the later one, even though the facts are similar.

### E7

- Location: precedent (reviewed March 2024)
- Quote: "Therefore, a prior decision serves as precedent only for issues, given the particular facts, that the court explicitly considered in reaching its decision."
- Key point: A prior decision serves as precedent only for the issues, given its particular facts, that the court explicitly considered in reaching its decision.

### E8

- Location: persuasive authority
- Quote: "Whether a court decision is persuasive authority or mandatory authority depends on the rank and jurisdiction of the courts involved."
- Key point: Whether a decision is mandatory or merely persuasive authority depends on the rank and jurisdiction of the courts involved.

### E9

- Location: civil law (reviewed July 2025)
- Quote: "As a legal system, civil law refers to a framework based primarily on written codes and statutes , rather than common law , which is based on judicial decisions or precedent . In civil law systems, judges play a more investigative role, focusing on applying the law to the facts rather than interpreting prior rulings ."
- Key point: As a legal system, civil law is based primarily on written codes and statutes, whereas common law is based on judicial decisions or precedent; civil law judges act more as investigators, focused on applying the law to the facts rather than interpreting prior rulings.

### E10

- Location: stare decisis
- Quote: "The previous deciding-court must have binding authority over the court; otherwise, the previous decision is merely persuasive authority ."
- Key point: Under stare decisis, the earlier deciding court must have binding authority over the present court; otherwise the earlier decision is merely persuasive authority.
