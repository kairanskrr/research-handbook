# Albert & Wager 2003: How to handle authorship disputes: a guide for new researchers

- Source: `albert2003authorship`, [original](https://web.archive.org/web/20241128073038/https://publicationethics.org/files/2003pdf12_0.pdf) (Committee on Publication Ethics, The COPE Report 2003, pp. 32–34)
- Version: PDF hosted on the website of the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE); the site returned 403 to scripted access, so this note uses the Internet Archive snapshot of the same official file from 2024-11-28.

### E1

- Location: How to handle authorship disputes when they occur, (a) Disputes
- Quote: "In such cases you need to negotiate with the people involved." / "Support this with evidence, such as laboratory notebooks, manuscripts, ICMJE statement, Instructions to Authors etc. If you remain unhappy with your supervisor's decision, you may consider an appeal to someone more senior, such as the departmental head or dean. But you should do this in exceptional circumstances only - and make sure your supervisor knows what you are intending to do."
- Key point: For authorship disputes that come down to differing interpretations, first negotiate with the people involved, backed by evidence such as lab notebooks, manuscripts, and authorship guidelines; if you still disagree with your supervisor's decision, you can appeal to the head of department or dean, but only in exceptional cases, and let your supervisor know.

### E2

- Location: What you can do if authorship issues are not resolved
- Quote: "If you discover this only after publication you may contact the journal and ask for a correction." / "You could contact the journal but an editor is unlikely to add your name without the agreement of the other authors."
- Key point: If you discover after publication that you were listed as an author against your wishes, you can ask the journal for a correction; if you were left off, you can contact the journal, but an editor is unlikely to add your name without the other authors' agreement.

### E3

- Location: Key concepts in authorship, Appeals
- Quote: "However most editors are reluctant to get involved in disputes about omitted authors since they do not have enough information to judge such cases." / "Similarly, COPE only hears cases submitted by journal editors and is not an appeal body for cases of disputed authorship."
- Key point: Most editors are reluctant to get involved in disputes over omitted authors because they lack the information to judge; COPE only accepts cases submitted by journal editors and is not an appeals body for authorship disputes.
