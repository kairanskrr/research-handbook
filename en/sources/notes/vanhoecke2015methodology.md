# Van Hoecke 2015: Methodology of Comparative Legal Research

- Source: `vanhoecke2015methodology`, [original](https://doi.org/10.5553/REM/.000010)
- Version: Law and Method (an open access legal methodology journal from the Dutch publisher Boom), December 2015, full text on the publisher's website (marked open access), accessed 2026-10-08. The author was then Professor of Comparative Law at Queen Mary University of London, UK, and Professor of Jurisprudence and Comparative Law at Ghent University, Belgium. Locations give the section number. The article is about academic comparative law research, mainly comparing national legal systems.

### E1

- Location: §4 How does one compare?
- Quote: "On the basis of these writings, we may distinguish six different methods for comparative research: the functional method, the structural method, the analytical method, the law-in-context method, the historical method and the common-core method."
- Key point: The author distinguishes six methods of comparative law research: functional, structural, analytical, law-in-context, historical, and common-core.

### E2

- Location: §7 Conclusion
- Quote: "It has to be repeated: the choice of method or level for comparison will mainly depend on the research question(s) guiding the research project."
- Key point: The choice of comparative method and level of comparison depends mainly on the research question.

### E3

- Location: §2 Choice of legal systems to be compared
- Quote: "The wording of the research question will be the main criterion for the choice of legal systems to be compared."
- Key point: Which legal systems to compare depends mainly on how the research question is worded.

### E4

- Location: §2 Choice of legal systems to be compared
- Quote: "Even when one’s knowledge of languages will be the prime reason for choosing or eliminating some country, the researcher should be able to give good reasons why her choice is acceptable from a scholarly point of view."
- Key point: Even when language skills are the main reason for choosing a country, you should be able to give a scholarly justification for the choice.

### E5

- Location: §2 Choice of legal systems to be compared
- Quote: "for a thorough comparative research a good reading knowledge of the local language" / "is an absolute requirement" / "it is highly recommended to check whether one’s own interpretation is correct and whether there have been any recent changes in that law, by contacting a local scholar who speaks English or another language the researcher masters."
- Key point: Thorough comparative research requires a good reading knowledge of the local language; if you can read only English sources, you are strongly advised to contact local scholars to check that your interpretation is correct and whether the law there has changed recently.

### E6

- Location: §3 What has to be compared?
- Quote: "In all cases, however, comparison should never stop at the level of legislation, and even not at the level of case law, as the social reality may be more different than similar rules suggest (and sometimes more similar than different rules would suggest)."
- Key point: Comparison should never stop at the level of legislation, or even case law, because the social reality may be more different (and sometimes more similar) than the rules suggest.

### E7

- Location: §6 Tertium comparationis
- Quote: "We should not look at a foreign legal system with the eyes and doctrinal framework of our own legal system, but try to transcend it, by using external ‘neutral’ elements for comparing legal systems"
- Key point: Don't look at foreign law through the eyes and doctrinal framework of your own legal system; try to transcend it by comparing with external "neutral" elements.

### E8

- Location: §7 Conclusion
- Quote: "Hence, just copying foreign law could hardly be considered to be a ‘method’. It is rather the typical example of lack of method in comparative law."
- Key point: Just copying foreign law is hardly a method; it is rather the typical example of a lack of method in comparative law.

### E9

- Location: §4.5 The historical method
- Quote: "Fully understanding the law as it functions today in some society, is only possible when one knows where it comes from and why it is as it is today."
- Key point: Fully understanding how law functions in a society today requires knowing where it comes from and why it is as it is.

### E10

- Location: §4 How does one compare?
- Quote: "Comparative researchers should become professionals rather than be tourists."
- Key point: Comparative law researchers should be professionals, not tourists who only notice that "things are different over there."

### E11

- Location: §7 Conclusion
- Quote: "The functional method is looking at the actual societal problem (e.g., a train accident) and the way this is solved in different jurisdictions"
- Key point: The functional method looks at an actual societal problem (for example, a train accident) and how different jurisdictions solve it.

### E12

- Location: §5.3 Law in action vs law in the books
- Quote: "in any substantive research in comparative law, both legislation and case law will have to be studied, as, in all legal systems, common law and civil law alike, they are both of decisive importance for knowing the law."
- Key point: Any substantive comparative law research must study both legislation and case law, because in all legal systems, common law and civil law alike, both are decisive for knowing the law.
