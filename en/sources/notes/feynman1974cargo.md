# Feynman 1974: Cargo Cult Science

- Source: `feynman1974cargo`, [original](https://calteches.library.caltech.edu/51/2/CargoCult.htm)
- Version: Caltech 1974 commencement address, as printed in Engineering and Science magazine (Caltech Library web version).

### E1

- Location: opening
- Quote: "Then a method was discovered for separating the ideas—which was to try one to see if it worked, and if it didn’t work, to eliminate it. This method became organized, of course, into science."
- Key point: Science began with a method for separating ideas: try one to see if it works, and if it does not, eliminate it.

### E2

- Location: the Cargo Cult Science metaphor
- Quote: "So I call these things Cargo Cult Science, because they follow all the apparent precepts and forms of scientific investigation, but they’re missing something essential, because the planes don’t land."
- Key point: Research that follows all the apparent forms of science but is missing something essential is "Cargo Cult Science".

### E3

- Location: after the Cargo Cult Science metaphor, on scientific integrity
- Quote: "It’s a kind of scientific integrity, a principle of scientific thought that corresponds to a kind of utter honesty—a kind of leaning over backwards. For example, if you’re doing an experiment, you should report everything that you think might make it invalid—not only what you think is right about it"
- Key point: What is missing is scientific integrity: when you do an experiment, report everything that might make it invalid, not only what supports it.

### E4

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "If you make a theory, for example, and advertise it, or put it out, then you must also put down all the facts that disagree with it, as well as those that agree with it."
- Key point: If you put out a theory, you must also put down all the facts that disagree with it.

### E5

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "In summary, the idea is to try to give all of the information to help others to judge the value of your contribution; not just the information that leads to judgment in one particular direction or another."
- Key point: Give all the information that helps others judge the value of your contribution, not just the information that leads toward one conclusion.

### E6

- Location: the Wesson Oil example
- Quote: "So it’s the implication which has been conveyed, not the fact, which is true, and the difference is what we have to deal with."
- Key point: Every statement can be true while the implication conveyed is misleading; scientific integrity requires honesty in the implication too.

### E7

- Location: after the Millikan oil-drop experiment
- Quote: "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself—and you are the easiest person to fool."
- Key point: The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool.

### E8

- Location: explaining research to the public
- Quote: "If you’re representing yourself as a scientist, then you should explain to the layman what you’re doing—and if they don’t want to support you under those circumstances, then that’s their decision."
- Key point: A cosmologist worried that saying "there are no applications" would cost him funding; Feynman considers inventing applications dishonest and says you should tell it as it is.

### E9

- Location: publishing results
- Quote: "If you’ve made up your mind to test a theory, or you want to explain some idea, you should always decide to publish it whichever way it comes out. If we only publish results of a certain kind, we can make the argument look good."
- Key point: Once you decide to test a theory, publish the result whichever way it comes out; publishing only results of a certain kind makes the argument look better than it is.

### E10

- Location: the Cornell psychology student example
- Quote: "I explained to her that it was necessary first to repeat in her laboratory the experiment of the other person—to do it under condition X to see if she could also get result A—and then change to Y and see if A changed. Then she would know that the real difference was the thing she thought she had under control."
- Key point: Before changing the conditions of someone else's experiment, first repeat the original experiment under your own conditions; only then do you know that a difference really comes from the factor you changed.

### E11

- Location: main text, the paragraph on scientific integrity
- Quote: "if you’re doing an experiment, you should report everything that you think might make it invalid—not only what you think is right about it: other causes that could possibly explain your results; and things you thought of that you’ve eliminated by some other experiment, and how they worked"
- Key point: When you do an experiment, report everything that might make it invalid: other causes that could explain the results, and which of them you have eliminated by other experiments, and how.

### E13

> Withdrawn: duplicate of [E10](#e10) (its quote is fully contained in E10).

- Location: Main text of the talk, the example of the Cornell psychology student's rat experiment
- Quote: "it was necessary first to repeat in her laboratory the experiment of the other person—to do it under condition X to see if she could also get result A—and then change to Y and see if A changed. Then she would know that the real difference was the thing she thought she had under control."
- Key point: To study the effect of condition Y, first repeat the other person's experiment under condition X in your own laboratory and check that you get the same result, then change only to Y. Only then do you know that the real difference is the thing you had under control.

### E14

- Location: Main text of the talk, the example of the history of measuring the electron charge after Millikan's oil-drop experiment
- Quote: "When they got a number that was too high above Millikan’s, they thought something must be wrong—and they would look for and find a reason why something might be wrong. When they got a number closer to Millikan’s value they didn’t look so hard."
- Key point: Looking hard for something wrong when a result disagrees with expectations, but not looking so hard when it agrees, is an asymmetry that lets errors persist for a long time.

### E15

- Location: Main text of the talk, the example of Young's 1937 rat-corridor experiment
- Quote: "it uncovers the clues that the rat is really using—not what you think it’s using."
- Key point: A good experiment uncovers and rules out the clues that are really at work, instead of assuming it is the factor you think it is.
