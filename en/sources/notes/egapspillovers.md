# EGAP Methods Guide: 10 Things to Know About Spillovers

- Source: `egapspillovers`, [original web page](https://methods.egap.org/guides/data-collection/spillovers_en.html)
- Version: Evidence in Governance and Politics (EGAP) methods guide by Alexander Coppock; accessed October 2026. Locations give the section number.

### E1

- Location: §1 What they are
- Quote: "Spillovers refer to a broad class of instances in which a given subject is influenced by whether other subjects are treated."
- Key point: Spillovers are cases in which a subject is influenced by whether other subjects are treated.

### E2

- Location: §1 What they are
- Quote: "Politics: Election monitoring at some polling stations may displace fraud to neighboring polling stations." / "Economics: Lowering the cost of production for one firm may change the market price faced by other firms."
- Key point: Examples: monitoring elections at some polling stations may displace fraud to neighboring stations; lowering one firm's production costs may change the market prices other firms face.

### E3

- Location: §2 If ignored, spillovers may "bias" treatment effect estimates
- Quote: "In this case, positive spillovers cause a negative bias and vice-versa. This is because when spillovers are positive, the control group mean is inflated, so the difference-in-means is smaller than it otherwise would have been."
- Key point: Positive spillovers inflate the control group mean, shrinking the gap between groups and causing a negative bias; negative spillovers do the reverse.

### E4

- Location: §2 If ignored, spillovers may "bias" treatment effect estimates
- Quote: "Often, evaluators are trying to estimate what would happen if a program were rolled out to everyone. Evidence from an RCT that ignores spillover could greatly over or underestimate the total effects of the intervention."
- Key point: Evaluators often want to know what would happen if a program were rolled out to everyone; a randomized controlled trial (RCT) that ignores spillovers could greatly overstate or understate the total effect.

### E5

- Location: §3 Most experimental analyses implicitly or explicitly assume that there are no spillovers
- Quote: "The assumption that there are no spillovers is known as the non-interference assumption; it is part of a somewhat more elaborate assumption sometimes referred to as the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (or SUTVA)"
- Key point: Most experimental analyses assume no spillovers, the non-interference assumption, which is part of the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA).

### E6

- Location: §8 Sometimes you can avoid spillovers with "buffer rows"
- Quote: "One approach to addressing the problem of spillovers is to ensure that other units’ treatment assignments cannot interfere with potential outcomes, by including “buffer rows” between experimental units."
- Key point: One approach is to leave "buffers" between experimental units, so that their treatment assignments cannot interfere with each other.

### E7

- Location: §9 There are other design-based approaches for detecting spillover effects
- Quote: "First, it requires the researcher to stipulate a non-interference assumption ex ante, so there can be no question of fiddling around with interference assumptions until a statistically significant result pops up."
- Key point: Multilevel randomized designs require you to state the non-interference assumption in advance, so you cannot fiddle with the assumption after the fact until a significant result appears.

### E8

- Location: §9 There are other design-based approaches for detecting spillover effects
- Quote: "As ever, the difficulty is that the non-interference assumption used in the design stage could be wrong."
- Key point: The difficulty is that the non-interference assumption used at the design stage could itself be wrong.
