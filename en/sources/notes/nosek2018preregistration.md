# Nosek et al. 2018: The preregistration revolution

- Source: `nosek2018preregistration`, [original (open full text on PubMed Central)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5856500/)
- Version: PNAS 115(11):2600–2606. Web version; locations give section headings.

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "An effective solution is to define the research questions and analysis plan before observing the research outcomes—a process called preregistration."
- Key point: Definition of preregistration: defining the research questions and analysis plan before observing the research outcomes.

### E2

- Location: Mental Constraints on Distinguishing Predictions and Postdictions
- Quote: "The problem with this is understood as post hoc theorizing or hypothesizing after the results are known"
- Key point: Presenting a post hoc explanation as an advance prediction is hypothesizing after the results are known (HARKing).

### E3

- Location: Mental Constraints on Distinguishing Predictions and Postdictions
- Quote: "good intentions are not sufficient to overcome the fallibility of memory, motivated reasoning, and cognitive biases that can occur outside of conscious awareness or control"
- Key point: Good intentions are not enough. Memory is fallible, people engage in motivated reasoning, and these biases often operate outside conscious awareness.

### E4

- Location: Preregistration Distinguishes Prediction and Postdiction
- Quote: "Preregistration does not favor prediction over postdiction; its purpose is to make clear which is which."
- Key point: Preregistration does not rank prediction above postdiction; its purpose is to make clear which is which.

### E5

- Location: Challenge 1: Changes to Procedure During Study Administration
- Quote: "Jolene can transparently report changes that were made and why." / "Compared with the situation in which Jolene did not preregister at all, preregistration with reported deviations provides substantially greater confidence in the resulting statistical inferences."
- Key point: Deviating from the plan is common in practice. As long as you transparently report what changed and why, a preregistration plus a record of deviations is still far more credible than no preregistration at all.

### E6

- Location: Challenge 6: A Program of Research
- Quote: "all outcomes of that analysis plan must be reported to avoid the problem of selective reporting."
- Key point: After preregistering, report the outcomes of every analysis in the plan; otherwise it is still selective reporting.

### E7

- Location: Making Preregistration the Norm — Advancing the Motive for Preregistration
- Quote: "With Registered Reports, authors submit their research question and methodology to the journal for peer review before observing the outcomes of the research." / "At second-stage review, reviewers do not evaluate the perceived importance of the outcomes. Rather, they evaluate the quality of study execution and adherence to the preregistered plan."
- Key point: Registered Reports: authors submit their research question and methods to a journal for peer review before seeing the results; the second-stage review does not judge whether the results are "important," only whether the study was done well and followed the plan.

### E8

- Location: Challenge 2: Discovery of Assumption Violations During Analysis (the decision-tree paragraph)
- Quote: "Preregistration does not eliminate the possibility of poor statistical practices, but it does make them detectable."
- Key point: Preregistration does not eliminate poor statistical practices, but it makes them detectable.

### E9

- Location: Preregistration Distinguishes Prediction and Postdiction
- Quote: "there is not yet sufficient experimental evidence establishing its superiority for reproducibility."
- Key point: The authors acknowledge that preregistration's benefit for reproducibility is well grounded in theory and has some correlational evidence, but there is not yet sufficient experimental evidence.

### E10

- Location: Mental Constraints on Distinguishing Predictions and Postdictions
- Quote: "The dynamism of the research enterprise and limits of human reasoning make it easy to mistake postdiction as prediction." / "It is an example of circular reasoning––generating a hypothesis based on observing data, and then evaluating the validity of the hypothesis based on the same data."
- Key point: The iterative nature of research and the limits of human reasoning make it easy to mistake postdiction for prediction; using observed data to generate a hypothesis and then testing it on the same data is circular reasoning.

### E11

- Location: Challenge 7: Few a Priori Expectations
- Quote: "Part of the problem is that researchers are incentivized to present postdictions and exploratory results as if they had expected them in advance." / "dressing up discovery as tests of theoretical predictions undermines the credibility of all science by making it impossible to distinguish hypothesis generation from hypothesis testing"
- Key point: Researchers are incentivized to present postdictions and exploratory results as if they had expected them; dressing exploration up as a test makes it impossible to tell hypothesis generation from hypothesis testing.

### E12

- Location: Challenge 7: Few a Priori Expectations
- Quote: "In a process known as cross-validation, the dataset is split in two. One part is used for exploratory analysis to develop the models and predictions; the other part is sealed until exploration is complete"
- Key point: Cross-validation: split the dataset in two, use one part for exploration to develop predictions, and hold the other back until exploration is finished.

### E13

- Location: Challenge 5: Many Experiments
- Quote: "In such scenarios, researchers can achieve confirmatory research via replication. All initial experiments are treated as exploratory research. When something of interest is observed, then the initial design and analysis script become the preregistration for testing a prediction by running the experiment again."
- Key point: When data are easy to collect, confirmation can come through replication: treat all initial experiments as exploratory; once you see something interesting, fix the original design and analysis scripts as the plan and run the experiment again to test it.
