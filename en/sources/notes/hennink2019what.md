# Hennink, Kaiser & Weber 2019: What Influences Saturation? Estimating Sample Sizes in Focus Group Research

- Source: `hennink2019what`, [original (author manuscript on PubMed Central)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6635912/)
- Version: Qualitative Health Research 29(10):1483–1496 (2019); read the author manuscript on PubMed Central (NIHMS1031312), accessed 2026-10-17. Public health. The study data are 10 focus groups with South Asian adults in Atlanta, USA, about diet, exercise and diabetes.

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "Four focus groups were sufficient to identify a range of new issues (code saturation), but more groups were needed to fully understand these issues (meaning saturation)."
- Key point: In this study, four groups were enough to identify most new issues (code saturation), but more groups were needed to fully understand them (meaning saturation).

### E2

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "Group stratification influenced meaning saturation, whereby one focus group per stratum was needed to identify issues; two groups per stratum provided a more comprehensive understanding of issues, but more groups per stratum provided little additional benefit."
- Key point: Stratification affected meaning saturation: one group per stratum identified the issues, two per stratum gave a fuller understanding, and more added little.

### E3

- Location: end of the Introduction
- Quote: "we assessed the sample size needed to reach code saturation, which we define as the point when no additional issues are identified in data and the codebook has stabilized. Second, we assessed the sample size needed to reach meaning saturation, which we define as the point at which we fully understand the issues identified and when no further insights or nuances are found."
- Key point: Code saturation is when no new issues appear and the codebook has stabilized; meaning saturation is when the issues are fully understood and no further insights or nuances emerge.

### E4

- Location: Discussion
- Quote: "The first focus group generated 60% of all new codes with a sharp decline thereafter, regardless of the order in which focus groups were conducted."
- Key point: Whatever order the groups were taken in, the first group produced 60% of all new codes, with a sharp drop afterward.

### E5

- Location: Discussion
- Quote: "the prevalence of issues in data does not indicate their significance in understanding the study phenomenon."
- Key point: How often an issue appears in the data does not show how important it is for understanding the phenomenon.

### E6

- Location: Discussion
- Quote: "researchers doing a focus group study stratified by one characteristic (e.g., sex) would need to conduct two groups to include both strata but should ideally conduct two groups for each of these strata - thereby making a sample size of 4 focus groups."
- Key point: When stratifying by one characteristic (such as sex), you need at least two groups to cover both strata, and ideally two per stratum, for four groups in total.

### E7

- Location: Discussion
- Quote: "Guest et al (2016) show that a homogenous study population where focus groups are not stratified can reach saturation in three to six focus groups"
- Key point: As reported by Hennink et al., Guest et al. found that a homogeneous, unstratified population can reach saturation in three to six groups.

### E8

- Location: Discussion
- Quote: "Therefore, providing universal sample size recommendations for focus groups studies is not useful." / "often this is achieved by identifying a range rather a fixed number when a sample size is proposed in advance (e.g., 4–6 focus groups)."
- Key point: A universal number of groups for focus group studies is not useful; when proposing a sample size in advance, give a range (such as 4–6 groups) rather than a fixed number, so you can adjust during data collection.
