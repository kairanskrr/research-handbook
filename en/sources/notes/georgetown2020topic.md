# Georgetown Law Writing Center 2020: Choosing a Successful Paper Topic

- Source: `georgetown2020topic`, [Original PDF (Writing Center, Georgetown University Law Center, US; free)](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Handout_3-Kaplan_Woods_Choosing_a_Successful_Paper_Topic.pdf)
- Version: Handout from the Writing Center at Georgetown University Law Center, first drafted by Lauren H. Uhlig in 2012 and revised by Jane Kaplan and Devlin Woods in 2020. Written for **US law students** writing seminar papers, student notes, and scholarly articles. 4 pages; locations are given as handout subheadings.

### E1

- Location: Opening
- Quote: "Your topic must interest both you and your audience. Furthermore, it must be manageable and thesis worthy (i.e., the topic allows for a thesis that is original and significant)."
- Key point: A topic must interest both you and your readers, be manageable, and be "thesis worthy": it must allow an original and significant thesis.

### E2

- Location: 1. Choose a General Topic Area
- Quote: "Are there recent developments in the law or new cases that pique your interest?"
- Key point: When looking for a topic, watch for recent developments in the law and new cases.

### E3

- Location: 2. Narrow Your Topic to a Manageable Scope
- Quote: "Do you want to examine the application of a law or issue in one jurisdiction? Do you want to challenge the current legal definition of a problem or legal term? Do you want to compare one issue to another issue?"
- Key point: Ways to narrow a topic: examine how a law applies in one jurisdiction; challenge the current legal definition of a problem or term; or compare one issue with another.

### E4

- Location: 2. Narrow Your Topic to a Manageable Scope
- Quote: "Even if you are narrowing your topic from a very broad topic to something more manageable, do not narrow your topic too much at the very beginning. Your narrower topic should still be broad enough such that you are able to come up with several thesis statements."
- Key point: Don't narrow the topic too much at the start; the narrower topic should still be broad enough to yield several possible theses.

### E5

- Location: 3. Pick a Thesis
- Quote: "You are only preempted when someone has published a paper with the exact same thesis and defends it for the exact same reasons, or if the facts you have based your thesis on change (i.e., a new case comes out that changes your entire argument)."
- Key point: You are preempted only if someone has published a paper with the exact same thesis defended for the exact same reasons, or if the facts your thesis rests on change (for example, a new case comes out).

### E6

- Location: 3. Pick a Thesis
- Quote: "Ask yourself: so, what? What is significant about your thesis? Why should the reader be interested?"
- Key point: Ask yourself "so what?": what is significant about your thesis, and why should readers care?
