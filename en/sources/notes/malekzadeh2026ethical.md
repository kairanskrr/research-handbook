# Malekzadeh et al. 2026: Ethical guidelines for geoprivacy: a framework for researchers and ethics committees

- Source: `malekzadeh2026ethical`, [full text on PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13092162/), doi:10.1186/s12942-026-00460-y
- Version: International Journal of Health Geographics 25:23, 2026 (submitted August 2025, accepted February 2026), open access (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0). The authors are from geography and public health research institutions in Finland, Canada, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. The paper lists numbered questions (Q1, Q2, ...) organized by the research life cycle (collection, storage, sharing, analysis, dissemination), for researchers' self-assessment and ethics committee review. The authors note that the "importance" assigned to each question is their expert judgment, a heuristic suggestion rather than a hard standard. Full text read via Europe PMC. Locations give the section or question number.

### E1

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "Even anonymized trajectory data can often be re-identified by linking patterns of movement"
- Key point: Even anonymized trajectory data can often be re-identified by linking patterns of movement.

### E2

- Location: Data collection, Q2
- Quote: "Geographic data should only be collected when it is essential to answering the research question. [...] For instance, neighborhood-level analysis may not require exact home coordinates, and studying monthly activity patterns may not justify collecting GPS data at minute-by-minute intervals. If coarser spatial or temporal resolution is sufficient, high-resolution data collection may be ethically unjustified."
- Key point: Collect geographic data only when it is essential to answering the research question. Neighborhood-level analysis may not need precise home coordinates, and studying monthly activity patterns does not justify minute-by-minute GPS logging. When coarser resolution suffices, collecting high-resolution data may be ethically indefensible.

### E3

- Location: Data collection, Q4
- Quote: "Informed consent is not just about listing data types; it must include an explanation of what can be inferred from the data. For example, even if researchers do not directly ask for home or workplace locations, these can often be derived from repeated GPS traces. Participants should be made aware of such risks and implications in plain language."
- Key point: Informed consent is not just a list of data types; it must explain what can be inferred from the data. For example, even without asking for home and work addresses, these can often be inferred from recurring GPS traces; explain such risks to participants in plain language.

### E4

- Location: Q24 (Anonymization and de-identification)
- Quote: "Anonymization of geographic data is limited by several key factors: spatial uniqueness, where distinctive movement patterns such as daily commutes can identify individuals even after direct identifiers are removed"
- Key point: Anonymizing geographic data has inherent limits, such as spatial uniqueness: distinctive movement patterns like a daily commute can identify a person even after names and other direct identifiers are removed.

### E5

- Location: Results dissemination, Q59
- Quote: "Even when raw data is anonymized, spatial outputs can inadvertently expose individuals or communities. Researchers should consider: Aggregating results to coarser geographic levels (e.g., neighborhood, district)."
- Key point: Even when the raw data are anonymized, spatial outputs such as maps can inadvertently expose individuals or communities; consider aggregating results to coarser geographic levels (e.g., neighborhoods, districts).

### E6

- Location: Results dissemination, Q59
- Quote: "Avoiding small-area mapping when findings relate to vulnerable or stigmatized populations."
- Key point: Avoid small-area mapping when findings concern vulnerable or stigmatized populations.

### E7

- Location: introduction to the Ethical questions section
- Quote: "For example, commonly used aggregated units such as ZIP codes or administrative areas may present low risk in some contexts, but may become highly sensitive in others, such as when linked to small populations or specific environmental or health exposures."
- Key point: Aggregated units such as ZIP codes or administrative areas may be low risk in some contexts but become highly sensitive when linked to sparsely populated areas or specific environmental or health exposures.

### E10

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "In reality, many projects struggle with delays, revisions, or outright rejections by ethics committees due to insufficient attention to the ethical implications of collecting, using and sharing geographic data."
- Key point: The authors observe that many projects face delays, revisions, or outright rejection in ethics review because they did not give enough attention to the ethics of collecting, using, and sharing geographic data.

### E11

- Location: Data sharing, Q34
- Quote: "In many cases, it may be appropriate to release aggregated or downsampled versions of the data for general access while retaining finer-grained versions under restricted conditions."
- Key point: In many cases you can release aggregated or downsampled versions of the data openly while keeping finer-grained versions available under restricted conditions.

### E12

- Location: Anonymization and de-identification, Q27
- Quote: "There is no universally applicable privacy-preserving method for geographic data. Techniques such as differential privacy, spatial cloaking, k-anonymity, or geomasking offer varying levels of protection, but differ in their assumptions, computational requirements, and impact on data fidelity. Researchers must provide a rationale for their selected approach"
- Key point: There is no universally applicable privacy-preserving method for geographic data; techniques differ in protection, assumptions, and impact on data fidelity, so researchers should justify their choice.

### E13

- Location: Anonymization and de-identification, Q26
- Quote: "Techniques like generalization, masking, or aggregation can reduce identifiability but also risk distorting spatial patterns or excluding vulnerable groups."
- Key point: Generalization, masking, and aggregation reduce identifiability but can distort spatial patterns or exclude vulnerable groups from the analysis.

### E14

- Location: Data collection, Q11
- Quote: "Platforms using third-party plugins (e.g. basemap providers) often transmit locational metadata to external servers (e.g., to render a map). Even if the core dataset remains secure, these interactions may introduce vulnerabilities."
- Key point: Platforms using third-party plugins (e.g., online basemap providers) often send location metadata to external servers; even if the core data are secure, this can create vulnerabilities.
