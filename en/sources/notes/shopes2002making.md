# Shopes 2002: Making Sense of Oral History

- Source: `shopes2002making`, [original PDF](https://historymatters.gmu.edu/mse/oral/oral.pdf), [web version](https://historymatters.gmu.edu/mse/oral/what.html)
- Version: A guide by Linda Shopes (former president of the Oral History Association in the US) for the "Making Sense of Evidence" series on George Mason University's "History Matters" website; the PDF was created in 2002. Accessed 2026-10-06. Locations give section names.

### E1

- Location: Interpreting Oral History
- Quote: "For all their considerable value, oral history interviews are not an unproblematic source. Although narrators speak for themselves, what they have to say does not."
- Key point: Oral interviews are valuable, but they are not unproblematic sources. Interviewees speak for themselves, but what they say does not interpret itself.

### E2

- Location: Interpreting Oral History
- Quote: "The first step in assessing an interview, then, is to consider the reliability of the narrator and the verifiability of the account."
- Key point: The first step in evaluating an interview is to consider whether the narrator is reliable and whether the account can be verified.

### E3

- Location: Interpreting Oral History
- Quote: "The narrator's relationship to the events under discussion, personal stake in presenting a particular version of events, physical and mental state at the time of the events under discussion and at the moment of the interview, as well as the overall attention and care the narrator brings to the interview and the internal consistency of the account all figure into the narrator's reliability as a source."
- Key point: To judge a narrator's reliability, look at their relationship to the events, what stake they have in telling a particular version, their physical and mental state at the time of the events and of the interview, how engaged they are in the interview, and whether their account is internally consistent.

### E4

- Location: Interpreting Oral History
- Quote: "The veracity of what is said in an interview can be gauged by comparing it both with other interviews on the same subject and with related documentary evidence."
- Key point: You can compare an interview with other interviews on the same topic and with related documents to judge whether its statements are accurate.

### E5

- Location: Interpreting Oral History
- Quote: "If, however, it conflicts with other evidence or is incompatible with it, the historian needs to account for the disparities" / "Might intervening events--for example, ideological shifts between the time of the events under discussion and the time of the interview or subsequent popular cultural accounts of these events--have influenced later memories?"
- Key point: When an interview conflicts with other evidence, the historian has to account for the discrepancy, for example: did later shifts in thinking or popular-culture versions of events shape later memories?

### E6

- Location: Interpreting Oral History
- Quote: "Although Kennedy was elected with just 49.7% of the vote in the fall of 1960, almost two-thirds of all Americans remembered voting for him when they were asked about it in the aftermath of his assassination."
- Key point: Kennedy won only 49.7% of the vote in 1960, but when asked after his assassination, nearly two-thirds of Americans remembered voting for him.

### E7

- Location: Interpreting Oral History
- Quote: "An interview is inevitably an act of memory, and while individual memories can be more or less accurate, complete, or truthful, in fact interviews routinely include inaccurate and imprecise information, if not outright falsehoods. Narrators frequently get names and dates wrong, conflate disparate events into a single event, recount stories of questionable truthfulness."
- Key point: Interviews are acts of memory; they often contain inaccurate or imprecise information, and even falsehoods. Interviewees often get names and dates wrong and conflate several events into one.

### E8

- Location: Interpreting Oral History
- Quote: "Narrators manipulated the facts of Trastulli's death to render it less senseless and more comprehensible to them" / "errors, inventions, and myths lead us through and beyond facts to their meanings"
- Key point: Portelli studied the many accounts of the death of the Italian worker Trastulli: narrators manipulated the facts so the death seemed less senseless and more comprehensible to them; "errors, inventions, and myths" lead us through and beyond facts to their meanings.

### E9

- Location: Interpreting Oral History
- Quote: "an interview is a storied account of the past recounted in the present, an act of memory shaped as much by the moment of telling as by the history being told."
- Key point: An interview is a story about the past told in the present; memory is shaped both by the history being told and by the moment of telling.
