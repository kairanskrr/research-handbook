# Krippendorff 2004: Reliability in Content Analysis: Some Common Misconceptions and Recommendations

- Source: `krippendorff2004reliability`, [author manuscript PDF (University of Pennsylvania ScholarlyCommons)](https://repository.upenn.edu/handle/20.500.14332/1886)
- Version: author's accepted manuscript (postprint); published in Human Communication Research 30(3), pp. 411–433, 2004 (DOI 10.1111/j.1468-2958.2004.tb00738.x, checked against Crossref). Locations give page numbers of the manuscript PDF. A response to Lombard, Snyder-Duch, and Bracken's 2002 reliability guidelines, from communication research. The manuscript sets Greek letters in a special font that was lost in text extraction; the α in the quotes below was restored from the PDF layout.

### E1

- Location: Relation Between Agreement and Reliability, p. 3
- Quote: "To be clear, agreement is what we measure; reliability is what we wish to infer from it. In content analysis, reproducibility is arguably the most important interpretation of reliability"
- Key point: Agreement is what we measure and reliability is what we want to infer from it; in content analysis the most important sense of reliability is reproducibility: other coders following the same rules should produce the same data.

### E2

- Location: Relation Between Agreement and Reliability, p. 3
- Quote: "Many methodological problems in testing reliability stem from violating the requirement for coders to be truly independent, being given coding instructions they cannot follow, or applying them to data that they fail understand."
- Key point: Many problems in reliability testing come from three sources: coders who are not truly independent, coding instructions they cannot follow, and data they do not understand.

### E3

- Location: p. 10
- Quote: "The use of %-agreement should be actively discouraged, especially as a fallback criterion. Instead, I recommend that only chance-corrected agreement coefficients that satisfy (2) and (3) be used for inferring the reliability of data."
- Key point: Percent agreement should be actively discouraged, especially as a fallback; only chance-corrected agreement coefficients that meet the author's stated conditions should be used to infer reliability.

### E4

- Location: p. 10
- Quote: "When all coders use only one category, there is no variation and, hence, no evidence of reliability."
- Key point: When all coders use only one category, there is no variation and therefore no evidence of reliability, even if percent agreement is 100%.

### E5

- Location: Recommendations, (i), p. 12
- Quote: "Reliability data, the sample of data from which the trustworthiness of a population of data is to be inferred, have to be generated by coders that are widely available, follow explicit and communicable instructions (a data language), and work independently of each other. Reliability data must be representative of the data whose reliability is in question (not of the population of ultimate research interest)" / "Coders must be interchangeable, may code different subsamples of data, provided there is enough duplication or overlap."
- Key point: Reliability data must be produced by widely available coders who follow explicit, communicable instructions and work independently, and must represent the data whose reliability is in question; coders must be interchangeable and may code different subsamples, provided there is enough overlap.

### E6

- Location: Recommendations, (ii), p. 12
- Quote: "α can handle multiple coders, nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio, and other metrics, missing data, and small sample sizes." / "Content analyses that assess reliability in terms of any association coefficient, Pearson's r, for example, [...] Cronbach's (1951) alpha, [...] and %-agreement should be rejected as these measures are incompatible with reliability concerns in content analysis."
- Key point: Krippendorff's α handles multiple coders, any level of measurement, missing data, and small samples; the author argues that content analyses assessing reliability with association coefficients such as Pearson's r, with Cronbach's alpha, or with percent agreement should be rejected (the omitted passages also name Cohen's kappa, among others).

### E7

- Location: Recommendations, (iii), p. 12
- Quote: "An acceptable level of agreement below which data are to be rejected as too unreliable must be chosen depending on the costs of drawing invalid conclusions from these data." / "I suggested elsewhere to require α ≥ .800, and where tentative conclusions are still acceptable, α ≥ .667" / "Except for perfect agreement, there are no magical numbers, however."
- Key point: The acceptable level of agreement should depend on the cost of drawing invalid conclusions; the author suggests requiring α ≥ .800, or α ≥ .667 where tentative conclusions are acceptable, but apart from perfect agreement there are no magic numbers.

### E8

- Location: Recommendations, (iv), p. 13
- Quote: "All distinctions that matter should be tested for their reliability. Where a system of several variables is intended to support a conclusion [...], the reliability of each variable should be measured and the smallest among them should be taken as the reliability of the whole system. Averaging the agreement measures of several variables, especially when they include easily coded clerical ones, can easily mislead researchers about the reliability of variables that matter."
- Key point: Every distinction that matters should be tested for reliability; when several variables support a conclusion, measure each and take the lowest as the reliability of the whole, because averaging in easily coded variables misleads.

### E9

- Location: Recommendations, (iv), p. 13
- Quote: "After data have been generated, reliability may be improved by discarding unreliable distinctions, recoding or lumping categories or dropping variables that do not meet the criterion adopted in (iii). Resolving disagreements by majority among three or more coders may make researchers feel better about their data, but does not affect the measured reliability"
- Key point: If reliability falls short, you can drop unreliable distinctions, recode or merge categories, or remove variables that miss the criterion; resolving disagreements by majority vote among three or more coders may be reassuring but does not change measured reliability.

### E10

- Location: Recommendations, (iii), p. 12
- Quote: "To ensure that the measured agreement is representative of the data in question, confidence intervals should be consulted. Testing the null-hypothesis that observed agreement deviates from chance has no bearing on reliability, which concerns deviations from perfect agreement or 1.000."
- Key point: Confidence intervals should be consulted to make sure measured agreement represents the data; testing whether agreement exceeds chance says nothing about reliability, which concerns how far agreement falls short of perfect.
