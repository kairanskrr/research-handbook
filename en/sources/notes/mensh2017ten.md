# Mensh & Kording 2017: Ten simple rules for structuring papers

- Source: `mensh2017ten`, [full text](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619)
- Version: PLOS Computational Biology 13(9): e1005619, open access. Page numbers are PDF page numbers.

### E1

- Location: Rule 1, p. 2
- Quote: "Focus on a single message; papers that simultaneously focus on multiple contributions tend to be less convincing about each and are therefore less memorable."
- Key point: Focus a paper on a single message; papers that push multiple contributions at once are less convincing about each and less memorable.

### E2

- Location: Rule 1, p. 2
- Quote: "Your communication efforts are successful if readers can still describe the main contribution of your paper to their colleagues a year after reading it."
- Key point: Test of successful communication: a year after reading, readers can still describe your paper's main contribution to their colleagues.

### E3

- Location: Rule 3, p. 3
- Quote: "This structure reduces the chance that the reader will wonder “Why was I told that?” (if the context is missing) or “So what?” (if the conclusion is missing)."
- Key point: The context-content-conclusion structure keeps readers from wondering "Why was I told that?" (missing context) or "So what?" (missing conclusion).

### E4

- Location: Rule 3, p. 3
- Quote: "Applying C-C-C at the paragraph scale, the first sentence defines the topic or context, the body hosts the novel content put forth for the reader’s consideration, and the last sentence provides the conclusion to be remembered."
- Key point: The structure works at the scale of the whole paper and of each paragraph: the first sentence sets the topic, the body gives the content, and the last sentence gives the conclusion to remember.

### E5

- Location: Rule 3, p. 3
- Quote: "They do not care about the chronological path by which you reached a result; they just care about the ultimate claim and the logic supporting it"
- Key point: Readers do not care about the chronological path by which you reached a result, only about the final claim and the logic supporting it; do not write the paper in chronological order.

### E6

- Location: Rule 6, p. 6
- Quote: "The introduction should not contain a broad literature review beyond the motivation of the paper."
- Key point: Build the introduction around the gap: narrow step by step to what is missing in the literature and why it matters, and summarize in the last paragraph how the paper fills it; do not include a broad literature review in the introduction.

### E7

- Location: Rule 7, p. 6
- Quote: "sketch out the logical structure of how your results support your claim and convert this into a sequence of declarative statements that become the headers of subsections within the results section"
- Key point: Sketch the logic of how your results support your claim as a sequence of declarative statements, and use them as the subsection headings of the results section.

### E8

- Location: Rule 8, p. 7
- Quote: "The discussion section explains how the results have filled the gap that was identified in the introduction, provides caveats to the interpretation, and describes how the paper advances the field by providing new opportunities."
- Key point: The discussion explains how the results filled the gap, gives caveats to the interpretation, and describes how the paper advances the field.

### E9

- Location: Rule 9, p. 7
- Quote: "The title, abstract, and figures are viewed by far more people than the rest of the paper, and the methods section is read least of all. Budget accordingly."
- Key point: The title, abstract, and figures are viewed far more than the rest; budget your time according to how many readers each part gets.

### E10

- Location: Rule 10, p. 8
- Quote: "In many cases, trashing entire paragraphs and rewriting is a faster way to produce good text than incremental editing."
- Key point: Don't be too attached to what you have written; trashing whole paragraphs and rewriting is often a faster way to good text than incremental editing.

### E11

- Location: Rule 10, p. 8
- Quote: "if you, as the writer, cannot describe the entire outline of a paper to a colleague in a few minutes, then clearly a reader will not be able to."
- Key point: If you cannot describe the outline of the whole paper in a few minutes, readers will not be able to either; the story still needs refining.

### E12

- Location: Rule 5, p. 4
- Quote: "The abstract is, for most readers, the only part of the paper that will be read."
- Key point: For most readers the abstract is the only part they will read, so it must tell the whole story.

### E13

- Location: Rule 2, p. 2
- Quote: "Avoid abbreviations and acronyms so that readers do not have to go back to earlier sections to identify them."
- Key point: Write for readers who don't know your work: define terms clearly and avoid abbreviations and acronyms.

### E14

- Location: Rule 10, p. 8
- Quote: "Non-specific feedback and unenthusiastic reviews often imply that the reviewers did not “get” the big picture story line."
- Key point: Non-specific, unenthusiastic feedback often means readers did not get the big-picture story line; very specific feedback usually points to a paragraph whose logic is weak.

### E15

- Location: Rule 5, p. 4
- Quote: "The context must communicate to the reader what gap the paper will fill." / "The content (“Here we”) first describes the novel method or approach that you used to fill the gap or question. Then you present the meat—your executive summary of the results." / "the conclusion interprets the results to answer the question that was posed at the end of the context section."
- Key point: Write the abstract as context-content-conclusion: the context says what gap the paper fills; the content first describes the new method, then summarizes the results; the conclusion interprets the results and answers the question raised at the end of the context.

### E16

- Location: Rule 5, p. 4
- Quote: "This structure helps you avoid the most common mistake with the abstract, which is to talk about results before the reader is ready to understand them."
- Key point: The most common mistake in abstracts is talking about results before the reader is ready to understand them.

### E17

- Location: Rule 1, p. 2
- Quote: "The title is the ultimate refinement of the paper’s contribution. Thinking about the title early—and regularly returning to hone it—can help not only the writing of the paper but also the process of designing experiments or developing theories."
- Key point: The title is the ultimate refinement of the paper's contribution; thinking about it early and honing it regularly helps both the writing and the design of experiments.

### E18

- Location: Rule 6, p. 6
- Quote: "The last paragraph of the introduction is special: it compactly summarizes the results, which fill the gap you just established."
- Key point: The last paragraph of the introduction compactly summarizes the results and how they fill the gap just established.

### E19

- Location: Rule 7, p. 6
- Quote: "figures are often viewed by readers who skip directly from the abstract in order to save time. Thus, the title of the figure should communicate the conclusion of the analysis, and the legend should explain how it was done."
- Key point: Many readers skip straight from the abstract to the figures, so a figure's title should state the conclusion of the analysis and the legend should explain how it was done.

### E20

- Location: Rule 7, pp. 6–7
- Quote: "Each paragraph convinces the reader of the answer given in its last sentence. This makes it easy to find the paragraph in which a suspicious conclusion is drawn and to check the logic of that"
- Key point: Each results paragraph opens with the question it answers and gives the answer in its last sentence; this makes it easy to find the paragraph where a suspicious conclusion is drawn and check its logic.

### E21

- Location: Rule 9, p. 7
- Quote: "Make an outline. We like to write one informal sentence for each planned paragraph."
- Key point: Make an outline, writing one informal sentence for each planned paragraph.

### E22

- Location: Rule 8, p. 7
- Quote: "Each of the following paragraphs in the discussion section starts by describing an area of weakness or strength of the paper. It then evaluates the strength or weakness by linking it to the relevant literature."
- Key point: Each discussion paragraph covers one weakness or strength of the paper and evaluates it in light of the related literature.
