# Waltman 2012: An empirical analysis of the use of alphabetical authorship in scientific publishing

- Source: `waltman2012empirical`, [original](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4863)
- Version: Full text of the arXiv:1206.4863 preprint; formally published in Journal of Informetrics 6(4): 700-711, 2012, DOI 10.1016/j.joi.2012.07.008.

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "In 2011, the authors of less than 4% of all publications intentionally chose to list their names alphabetically. The use of alphabetical authorship is most common in mathematics, economics (including finance), and high energy physics."
- Key point: In 2011, authors of less than 4% of all publications intentionally listed their names alphabetically; alphabetical authorship is most common in mathematics, economics (including finance), and high energy physics.

### E2

- Location: §3 Empirical analysis
- Quote: "Our analysis takes into account all publications in the Science Citation Index Expanded, the Social Sciences Citation Index, and the Arts & Humanities Citation Index in the period 1981" / "Only the document types article, note, and review are considered." / "All results presented in this paper relate to multi-author publications."
- Key point: The data are publications from 1981-2011 in three Web of Science citation indexes, counting only the document types article, note, and review; all results concern papers with two or more authors.

### E3

- Location: §3.2 Disciplinary differences
- Quote: "we rely on the Web of Science journal subject categories to define fields of science." / "Our focus is on publications from the period 2007"
- Key point: Fields are defined by the Web of Science journal subject categories; the comparison across fields uses publications from 2007-2011.

### E4

- Location: §2 Intentional vs. incidental alphabetical authorship
- Quote: "Correcting for the phenomenon of incidental alphabetical authorship requires a model of the way in which authors of publications decide on the order in which their names are listed."
- Key point: The share of intentionally alphabetical papers is not counted directly but estimated with a model that removes incidental alphabetical ordering.

### E5

- Location: §3.2, Table 1 (in the PDF, "methods" wraps to the next line; the four numbers are, in order, the average number of authors, the share of papers in alphabetical order, the estimated share of intentionally alphabetical papers, and the average alphabetization score)
- Quote: "Computer science, theory & 3.2 48.6% 32.6% 33.6% methods"
- Key point: The journal category "Computer science, theory & methods": 3.2 authors per paper on average, 48.6% of papers list authors alphabetically, and an estimated 32.6% do so intentionally.

### E6

- Location: §3.2, Table 1
- Quote: "Mathematics 2.4 83.3% 73.3% 73.7%"
- Key point: The "Mathematics" category: 83.3% of papers list authors alphabetically, and an estimated 73.3% do so intentionally.

### E7

- Location: §3.2, Table 1
- Quote: "Political science 2.4 60.8% 36.3% 36.9%"
- Key point: The "Political science" journal category: multi-author papers have 2.4 authors on average, 60.8% happen to list authors alphabetically, and the model estimates that 36.3% do so intentionally.

### E8

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "In the case of alphabetical authorship, nothing can be inferred about the relative contribution made by the different authors of a publication."
- Key point: With alphabetical authorship, nothing can be inferred from the order about the authors' relative contributions.

### E9

- Location: §3.2 main text (before Table 1)
- Quote: "The table shows the 25 subject categories that have at least 15% intentionally alphabetical publications."
- Key point: Table 1 lists all 25 subject categories with at least 15% intentionally alphabetical publications. Checking Table 1: the only computing category among them is "Computer science, theory & methods."
