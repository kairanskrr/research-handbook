# Eisner: How to Find Research Problems

- Source: `eisnerproblems`, [original](https://www.cs.jhu.edu/~jason/advice/how-to-find-research-problems.html)
- Version: Author's homepage; written in 1997 for new PhD students in computer science at the University of Pennsylvania, page last modified 2015-08-30.

### E1

- Location: main text, "small bones"
- Quote: "About the smallest bone that you can find in Computer Science is a reproduction or implementation of someone else's work. While this doesn't get you points for originality, it may be useful, both to your education and to the field."
- Key point: The smallest contribution is reproducing or implementing someone else's work: not original, but potentially useful both for your own learning and for the field.

### E2

- Location: main text, "small bones"
- Quote: "When reading papers, stay on the lookout for such bones. In particular, notice when the author may be making harmful simplifications or arbitrary choices in his/her approach. These are opportunities for you to try something different."
- Key point: When reading papers, look for harmful simplifications or arbitrary choices the author made; those are chances for you to do it differently.

### E3

- Location: main text, "big bones"
- Quote: "What problems (recognized or unrecognized) are obstructing progress in my field? Can I solve them? If not, can I at least formalize them? Can I prove to my colleagues that solving them would make a difference?"
- Key point: Ask yourself: what problems (recognized or unrecognized) are obstructing progress in my field? Can I solve them? If not, can I at least formalize them and make them clear?

### E4

- Location: main text, "ripe for the plucking"
- Quote: "Every field has its share of problems that everyone knows are "kinda important," and that may even get mentioned a lot, but on which no one has yet made a serious attempt. If you think you spot such a problem, use your colleagues and the library to make sure it hasn't been plucked yet."
- Key point: Every field has problems that everyone knows are "kinda important" but on which no one has made a serious attempt; when you spot one, ask colleagues and check the literature to confirm no one has done it yet.

### E5

- Location: main text, "identify new interesting problems"
- Quote: "Study existing (applied) systems and note what they do badly at. If your field is interdisciplinary, ask people in the other discipline what they think is interesting."
- Key point: To find new problems, study where existing systems do badly; if your field is interdisciplinary, ask people in the other discipline what they find interesting.

### E6

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "In many areas, the data have a way of suggesting their own problems."
- Key point: In many areas, the data suggest their own problems.

### E7

- Location: Keep in mind
- Quote: "serve a goal that will still seem worthy 6, 12, or 48 months from now?"
- Key point: One question for judging a time-consuming project: will the goal it serves still seem worthy 6, 12, or 48 months from now?

### E8

- Location: Finally
- Quote: "Don't spend eternity on background reading. Recognize that you will have to start your work in a state of partial ignorance: you don't have time to learn everything you need to know."
- Key point: Do not spend forever on background reading; you will have to start your work in a state of partial ignorance.

### E9

- Location: Finally
- Quote: "So start doing your own thinking early. You can alternate that with reading: just show your ideas periodically to someone who can warn you about related work and point you to relevant papers."
- Key point: Start your own thinking early and alternate it with reading; periodically show your ideas to someone knowledgeable who can warn you about related work.

### E10

- Location: main text, "big bones"
- Quote: "Sometimes finding a good solution involves changing the problem slightly."
- Key point: Sometimes finding a good solution involves changing the problem slightly.
