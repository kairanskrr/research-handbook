# Cronon et al. 2009: Learning to Do Historical Research: A Primer

- Source: `cronon2009learning`, [Original](https://www.williamcronon.net/researching/)
- Version: A website written by environmental historian William Cronon (University of Wisconsin–Madison) with the students of his fall 2008 graduate seminar; it focuses on environmental history but is written for anyone learning to do historical research. Last updated March 2009, read 2026-10-06. Pages used: home page (index.htm), What Are the Documents? (documents.htm), Note-taking (notetaking.htm), Manuscripts (manuscripts.htm), and Argument and Narrative (arguing.htm). Locations give the page name and subheading.

### E1

- Location: Home page, Finding the Documents paragraph
- Quote: "Unlike science, where one can set up a laboratory experiment or perform field investigations that describe the world as it exists right now, the past survives only as fragments that remain from a time that is no more. Studying history means collecting these fragments and figuring out what they mean."
- Key point: Science can run experiments and field observations, but the past no longer exists and survives only in fragments. Doing history means collecting these fragments and working out what they mean.

### E19

- Location: Argument and Narrative, list of questions for evaluating primary sources
- Quote: "Who was the author’s audience? How might the intended audience affect what was or wasn’t included in the document?"

### E26

- Location: Home page, Positioning Your Argument paragraph
- Quote: "Historians sometimes use the phrase "primary sources" to refer to the original documents that provide the building blocks for everything we know about the past. - Key point: Primary sources are the original documents that are the building blocks of everything we know about the past; secondary sources are works that scholars and others write using primary sources to explain the past.

### E35

- Location: Manuscripts, Ask Your Research Question and Lots of Others
- Quote: "Make sure you care about the question you go in with enough that you won't immediately abandon it after reading the first document. But don't be afraid to let your question transform itself in dialogue with the documents. Whatever you do, keep track of this thought process on paper. New questions or discarded ones could represent future projects."
- Key point: Go in with a question you care about enough not to drop after the first document, but don't be afraid to let it change in dialogue with the documents. Write down your thinking; new and abandoned questions may become future projects.
