# Sim & Waterfield 2019: Focus group methodology: some ethical challenges

- Source: `sim2019focus`, [article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11135-019-00914-5)
- Version: Quality & Quantity 53:3003–3022 (2019), open access (Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license). Research ethics of method in social and health research. Read on 2026-10-17 from the full text on the journal website; locations give section numbers.

### E1

- Location: §1.1 Definition of a focus group
- Quote: "A focus group can be defined broadly as ‘a type of group discussion about a topic under the guidance of a trained group moderator’ (Stewart 2018: p. 687)."
- Key point: A focus group can be broadly defined as a group discussion of a topic guided by a trained moderator.

### E2

- Location: §1.1 Definition of a focus group
- Quote: "It is recommended that data collection, and subsequent analysis, should take account of both the dialogue and the interaction that has occurred within the group"
- Key point: Data collection and analysis should take account of both what is said and how the group interacts.

### E3

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "Focus group methodology generates distinct ethical challenges that do not correspond fully to those raised by one-to-one interviews."
- Key point: Focus groups raise ethical challenges that do not fully match those of one-to-one interviews.

### E4

- Location: §2.1 Disclosure and consent
- Quote: "This applies even more in focus group research, because what takes place in the group depends in part upon other participants, who may spontaneously raise issues not necessarily intended, or predicted, by the moderator."
- Key point: What happens in a focus group depends partly on other participants, who may raise topics the moderator neither intended nor predicted, so it is hard to tell participants in advance what will happen.

### E5

- Location: §2.2 Consent as revocable
- Quote: "Withdrawing from a group discussion is a very public and potentially disruptive act that an individual may find hard to perform."
- Key point: Leaving a group discussion partway through is a public and potentially disruptive act that a person may find hard to do.

### E6

- Location: §2.2 Consent as revocable
- Quote: "Probably the only way to resolve this problem is to make it explicit in the consent process that whilst a participant can withdraw from the group, and may ask that his or her data are not quoted when the study is reported, withdrawing data prior to analysis is simply not possible."
- Key point: The authors suggest stating in the consent process that participants can leave the group and can ask not to be quoted, but cannot withdraw their data before analysis, since it is interwoven with what others said.

### E7

- Location: §3.1 Deductive disclosure and internal confidentiality
- Quote: "Clearly, external confidentiality can normally be assured by the researcher, as he or she is in control of what is reported from the study. Internal confidentiality relies on adherence to ground rules and observance of aspects of the consent process, over both of which the researcher has much more limited control."
- Key point: The researcher can assure external confidentiality, since they control what is reported, but internal confidentiality (whether members repeat things outside the group) depends on members following the ground rules, over which the researcher has little control.

### E8

- Location: §3.1 Deductive disclosure and internal confidentiality
- Quote: "This assists in preserving anonymity, but if participants are known to one another, their anonymity is clearly harder to maintain."
- Key point: Participants being strangers helps preserve anonymity; when they know one another, anonymity is harder to maintain.

### E9

- Location: §3.1 Deductive disclosure and internal confidentiality
- Quote: "In particular, it is much harder to ‘withdraw’ a comment in the public context of a focus group than in a one-to-one interview (Carey and Asbury 2012)."
- Key point: In the public setting of a focus group, a comment is much harder to take back than in a one-to-one interview.

### E10

- Location: §3.1 Deductive disclosure and internal confidentiality
- Quote: "The moderator can also minimize deductive disclosure, by omitting certain information about participants in a report, by attributing quotations to categories of participants rather than pseudonymized individuals, or by altering other information that is potentially identifying."
- Key point: To prevent deductive disclosure, where others piece together clues to identify a participant, a report can omit some details, attribute quotes to categories of participants rather than pseudonymized individuals, or alter other potentially identifying information.

### E11

- Location: §5 Practical strategies, The consent process
- Quote: "Thus, certain assurances can be given, though these can strictly only relate to those aspects of the focus group over which the researcher has a degree of influence (Carey and Asbury 2012)."
- Key point: The researcher can give some assurances, but strictly only about the aspects they can influence.

### E12

- Location: §5 Practical strategies, Briefing
- Quote: "The researcher can emphasize the public nature of a focus group and the need for confidentiality and anonymity, and provide guidance on subjects that might be felt unsuitable to be raised during the discussion"
- Key point: The briefing before the discussion can stress that a focus group is a public setting, that members need to respect each other's confidentiality and anonymity, and which topics are unsuitable to raise.

### E13

- Location: §5 Practical strategies, Conduct of the focus group
- Quote: "he or she can nonetheless try to ensure that all members of the group have an opportunity to contribute to the discussion, without being under pressure to do so if unwilling, and that one or more individuals do not dominate the group. Additionally, the moderator can be alert to distress, breaches of confidentiality, or over-disclosure, and seek to turn the discussion in another direction if appropriate."
- Key point: The moderator gives everyone a chance to contribute without pressuring the unwilling, stops one or two people from dominating, watches for distress, breaches of confidentiality, or over-disclosure, and steers the discussion elsewhere when needed.

### E14

- Location: §5 Practical strategies, Conduct of the focus group
- Quote: "If sensitive or emotionally difficult topics have been discussed, it may be helpful to finish the discussion on a more neutral or positive note"
- Key point: After sensitive or emotionally difficult topics, it helps to end the discussion on a neutral or positive note.

### E15

- Location: §5 Practical strategies, Debriefing
- Quote: "The moderator can stay in the room for a while at the end of the focus group, or otherwise make him- or herself available, to give individual participants the opportunity to address any issues or concerns"
- Key point: The moderator can stay a while after the session so individual participants have a chance to raise concerns privately.
