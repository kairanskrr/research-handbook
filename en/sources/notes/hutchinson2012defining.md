# Hutchinson & Duncan 2012: Defining and Describing What We Do: Doctrinal Legal Research

- Source: `hutchinson2012defining`, [original PDF (Deakin Law Review website, open access)](https://ojs.deakin.edu.au/index.php/dlr/article/download/70/75/151)
- Version: Deakin Law Review 17(1) (2012), pp. 83-119. The authors taught at Queensland University of Technology, Australia, and City University London, UK, and the article mainly discusses the situation in **Australia and the UK**. The original uses `...' for quotation marks, kept as is. Locations give section headings and journal page numbers.

### E1

- Location: §III Defining Legal Research Terminology, p. 101
- Quote: "1. Doctrinal research -- `Research which provides a systematic exposition of the rules governing a particular legal category, analyses the relationship between rules, explains areas of difficulty and, perhaps, predicts future developments.'"
- Key point: Australia's 1987 Pearce Committee report defined doctrinal research as research that systematically expounds the rules of a legal category, analyzes the relationships between rules, explains areas of difficulty, and perhaps predicts future developments.

### E2

- Location: §III, p. 101
- Quote: "2. Reform-oriented research -- `Research which intensively evaluates the adequacy of existing rules and which recommends changes to any rules found wanting.'"
- Key point: Reform-oriented research evaluates the adequacy of existing rules and recommends changes to rules found wanting.

### E3

- Location: §III, p. 101
- Quote: "3. Theoretical research -- `Research which fosters a more complete understanding of the conceptual bases of legal principles and of the combined effects of a range of rules and procedures that touch on a particular area of activity.'"
- Key point: Theoretical research fosters a fuller understanding of the conceptual bases of legal principles.

### E4

- Location: §III, p. 102
- Quote: "4 Fundamental research -- `Research designed to secure a deeper understanding of law as a social phenomenon, including research on the historical, philosophical, linguistic, economic, social or political implications of law.'"
- Key point: Canada's 1983 Arthurs Report added a fourth category, "fundamental research": understanding law as a social phenomenon, including its historical, philosophical, linguistic, economic, social, and political implications.

### E5

- Location: §III, p. 102
- Quote: "All the reports agreed that doctrinal legal research was the most accepted methodology in the discipline of law."
- Key point: All the reports agreed that doctrinal research is the most accepted methodology in law.

### E6

- Location: §III B Doctrinal Method as a Two Part Process, p. 110
- Quote: "Doctrinal method is normally a two-part process, because it involves first locating the sources of the law and then interpreting and analysing the text."
- Key point: Doctrinal research is normally a two-part process: first locate the sources of law, then interpret and analyze the text.

### E7

- Location: §III B, p. 110
- Quote: "It is at the next step where the law or rule is interpreted and analysed within a specific context that the outcome becomes `contingent' or conditional on the expertise, views and methods of the individual researcher."
- Key point: The outcome of the second step (interpreting and analyzing rules in a specific context) depends on the individual researcher's expertise, views, and methods.

### E8

- Location: §III B 1 The Literature Review as a Step within the Doctrinal Method, p. 112
- Quote: "One point that must be made is that doctrinal research is more than simply a literature review."
- Key point: Doctrinal research is more than a literature review.

### E9

- Location: §III B 1, p. 113
- Quote: "It is the location and analysis of the primary documents of the law in order to establish the nature and parameters of the law."
- Key point: At its core, doctrinal research locates and analyzes the primary documents of the law to establish the nature and parameters of the law.

### E10

- Location: §III B 1, p. 113
- Quote: "It includes that intricate step of `reading, analysing and linking' the new information to the known body of law."
- Key point: Doctrinal research includes the step of "reading, analysing and linking" new information to the known body of law.

### E11

- Location: §III B 1, pp. 112–113
- Quote: "`Testimony' can include the secondary literature -- texts, journal articles, government reports, policy documents, law reform documents and media reports."
- Key point: Secondary literature includes textbooks, journal articles, government reports, policy documents, law reform documents, and media reports.

### E12

- Location: §III B 3 Doctrinal Research Compared to Historical Research, p. 117
- Quote: "The legal researcher examines primary sources in order to draw logical conclusions about what the law is in those instances where it is not immediately self-evident from those sources. By contrast, the historical researcher examines primary sources as evidence of fact."
- Key point: Legal researchers read primary sources to draw conclusions about what the law is; historical researchers read primary sources as evidence of facts.

### E13

- Location: §III B 3, p. 117
- Quote: "doctrinal research for the most part focuses on `privileged voices'."
- Key point: Doctrinal research mostly focuses on "privileged voices": judges' decisions and parliament's legislation.

### E14

- Location: §III B 2 Is Doctrinal Research a Qualitative or a Quantitative Methodology?, p. 116
- Quote: "The outcome varies according to the expertise of the individual scholar and cannot be replicated exactly by another researcher."
- Key point: The outcome of doctrinal analysis varies with the individual scholar's expertise and cannot be replicated exactly by another researcher.

### E15

- Location: §II E Are HDR Students in Law Articulating Their Methodologies in Their Theses?, p. 99
- Quote: "Unfortunately the doctrinal method is often so implicit and so tacit that many working within the legal paradigm consider that it is unnecessary to verbalise the process."
- Key point: The doctrinal method is often so implicit and tacit that many lawyers think it unnecessary to put the process into words.

### E16

- Location: §II E, p. 100
- Quote: "Therefore, only 38 of the 60 law theses (63.3 per cent) include a discussion of the methodology as part of the thesis."
- Key point: In 2010 the author examined 60 law research theses in Australia's digital theses repository; only 38 (63.3%) discussed their methodology.

### E17

- Location: §II E, p. 98
- Quote: "At some point there must be a statement, however brief, concerning the method of collection of the data -- for example: `This study will include a doctrinal analysis of legislation and case law.'"
- Key point: A research thesis needs at least one statement on how the data were collected, for example "This study will include a doctrinal analysis of legislation and case law."

### E18

- Location: §III B 1, p. 112
- Quote: "perhaps this methodology is nothing more than the need for doctrinal research to follow accepted conventions, using clear rationales, and for the research to be `systematic and purposive with a robust framework'."
- Key point: Perhaps the methodology of doctrinal research is simply following accepted conventions, giving clear rationales, and being "systematic and purposive with a robust framework."

### E19

- Location: §III A Does the Research Method Differ among the Various Legal Research Genres?, p. 106
- Quote: "There is firstly the problem-based doctrinal research methodology used by practitioners and students."
- Key point: Practitioners and students use problem-based doctrinal research (steps: assembling the facts, identifying the legal issues, reading background material, finding primary materials, synthesizing, reaching a tentative conclusion).

### E20

- Location: §III A, p. 107
- Quote: "Academic researchers choose both the topic and the breadth and depth of investigation. The doctrinal method is similar to that being used by the practitioner or the judge, except that the academic researcher (or HDR student) is not constrained by the imperative to find a concrete answer for a client."
- Key point: Academic researchers choose the topic and the breadth and depth of the investigation; their doctrinal method resembles that of practitioners and judges, except that they need not find a specific answer for a client.

### E24

### E25

### E31

- Location: §III B, paragraph beginning "What is it like and unlike?", p. 114
- Quote: "but these are different because they are `legitimated' by the sovereignty of the source (parliament or court) rather than because they are the `naturally occurring', observable phenomena usually used in empirical work."
- Key point: Legislation and judgments carry weight because of the authority of the parliament or court that made them, not because they are naturally occurring, observable phenomena like empirical data.

### E32

- Location: §III A Does the Research Method Differ among the Various Legal Research Genres?, p. 106
- Quote: "Doctrinal legal research ranges from practical problem-solving to `straightforward descriptions of (new) laws, with some incidental interpretative comments', to `innovative theory building (systematisation)', with `the more "simple" versions of that research' being the `necessary building blocks for the more sophisticated ones'."
- Key point: Doctrinal research ranges from practical problem-solving and descriptions of (new) laws to innovative theory building (systematization); the "simpler" versions are the necessary basis for the more complex ones. (The author quotes a volume edited by Van Hoecke.)

### E33

- Location: §III A, p. 108 (citing Westerman)
- Quote: "Most ... take as a starting-point a certain new legal development, such as a new interpretation of a certain doctrine, or a new piece of European regulation, and just set out to describe how this new development fits in with the area of law they are working in, or, if it does not seem to fit in, how the existing system should be rearranged in order to accommodate for this novelty."
- Key point: The typical approach described by the scholar Westerman: start from a new legal development (a new interpretation of a doctrine, a new piece of European regulation), describe how it fits into its area of law, and, if it does not fit, how the existing system should be adjusted to accommodate it.

- Key point: Restatement contributions include distinguishing settled from emerging law and identifying the difference between majority practice and "better" practice, ideally explaining the criteria used.

### E37

- Key point: Critical projects expose unstated assumptions, internally inconsistent structures, or other tensions within a body of law.

### E38

- Location: §III, p. 104 (Minow's list, "Comparative and Historical Inquiries")
- Quote: "[Describe] an earlier era or contrasting legal regime" / "[Illuminate] differences, choices, or continuities when compared"
- Key point: Comparative and historical inquiries describe another era or another legal regime and compare it with current domestic practice to illuminate differences, choices, or continuities.

- Key point: The researcher's philosophical stance often determines the research questions, process, and possible conclusions, but this stance often goes unstated.

### E45

- Key point: Legal reasoning is often deductive because the general rules are "given," for example by legislation.

### E46

- Location: §III B, p. 111
- Quote: "Where the source of the rule is case law rather than legislation, `the lawyer will have to examine several cases to find a major premise which underlies them all'."
- Key point: When a rule comes from case law rather than legislation, you have to examine several cases to find the major premise underlying them all (inductive reasoning).

### E47

- Location: §III B, p. 111
- Quote: "Analogy, on the other hand, involves locating similar situations arising, for example, in common law cases, and then arguing that similar cases should be governed by the same principle and have similar outcomes."
- Key point: Reasoning by analogy: find similar situations (for example, in common law cases) and argue that similar cases should be governed by the same principle and have similar outcomes.

### E48

- Location: §III B 1, p. 113
- Quote: "The `screening criteria' for legal primary materials are necessarily more rule bound and intricate."
- Key point: The screening criteria for primary legal materials are necessarily more rule-bound and intricate than those of an ordinary literature review.

### E49

- Location: §III A, p. 106
- Quote: "This approach is directed to solving a specific legal problem and normally includes the following steps" / "Reading background material (including legal dictionaries, legal encyclopaedias, textbooks, law reform and policy papers, loose leaf services, journal articles)" / "Locating primary material (including legislation, delegated legislation and case law"
- Key point: Problem-based doctrinal research usually includes: assembling the facts, identifying the legal issues, analyzing the issues to search for the law, reading background material (legal dictionaries, encyclopedias, textbooks, law reform and policy documents, loose-leaf services, journal articles), locating primary sources (legislation, delegated legislation, case law), synthesizing in context, and reaching a tentative conclusion.

- Key point: On anything but the most banal question, the applicable legal norm is likely to be complex, nuanced, and contested.
