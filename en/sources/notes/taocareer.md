# Tao: Career advice (a series of web pages)

- Source: `taocareer`, [original](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/career-advice/)
- Version: The Career advice series of pages on Terence Tao's blog "What's new," accessed 2026-10-06. Each page is a standalone piece of advice; locations give page titles.

### E1

- Location: Be sceptical of your own work
- Quote: "In particular, the method may also be able to prove much stronger statements which are known to be false, which would imply that there is a flaw in the method."
- Key point: If a method seems to solve the problem easily, check whether it would also "prove" a stronger statement known to be false; if it would, the method has a flaw.

### E2

- Location: Be sceptical of your own work
- Quote: "if you are trying to prove some ambitious claim, you might try to first look for a counterexample; either you find one, which saves you a lot of time and may well be publishable in its own right, or else you encounter some obstruction, which should give some clue as to what one has to do in order to establish the claim positively"
- Key point: To prove a bold statement, first try to find a counterexample: if you find one, you save a great deal of time (and the counterexample may itself be publishable); if you do not, the obstacles you hit will suggest how the proof should go.

### E3

- Location: Be sceptical of your own work
- Quote: "A good way to stress-test this sort of false argument is to try to run the same argument without the initial assumption that X is false."
- Key point: One way to check a proof by contradiction: run the argument again without the assumption "suppose X is false"; if it still yields a contradiction, the problem lies in the argument itself.

### E4

- Location: Be sceptical of your own work
- Quote: "A final type of warning sign is if your approach is based on adapting a method that has been used frequently in the past by many experts in the field, but with an additional twist of your own that all the previous experts somehow “missed”."
- Key point: Warning sign: your approach adds to a method experts commonly use a small twist that "all the experts have missed"; that twist very likely hides a flaw the experts already know about and avoid.

### E5

- Location: Be sceptical of your own work
- Quote: "Isolate a toy model case x of major problem X."
- Key point: In the typical process the author describes for actually solving a big problem, the first step is to isolate a toy model (a simplified special case).

### E6

- Location: On the importance of partial progress
- Quote: "One of the secrets to mathematical problem solving is that one needs to place a high value on partial progress, as being a crucial stepping stone to fully solving the problem."
- Key point: One secret to solving mathematical problems is to value partial progress, which is a key stepping stone to solving the problem completely.

### E7

- Location: On the importance of partial progress
- Quote: "The point is that even if the technique is doomed to fail, the precise point in the argument at which it fails can be very instructive"
- Key point: Even when a technique is bound to fail, exactly where in the argument it fails is very instructive.

### E8

- Location: Ask yourself dumb questions – and answer them!
- Quote: "For instance, given a standard lemma in a subject, you can ask what happens if you delete a hypothesis, or attempt to strengthen the conclusion"
- Key point: For a standard lemma, you can ask: what happens if you drop a hypothesis? Can the conclusion be strengthened?

### E9

- Location: Learn the limitations of your tools
- Quote: "Thus, knowing a library of counterexamples, or easily analysed model situations, is very important, as well as knowing the type of obstructions that your tool can deal with, and which ones it has no hope of resolving."
- Key point: Build up a stock of counterexamples and easily analyzed model cases, and know which obstructions your tools can handle and which are hopeless.

### E10

- Location: Learn the limitations of your tools
- Quote: "While unintuitive, one should also discuss the known limitations of a tool when writing a paper that uses that tool."
- Key point: A paper should discuss the known limitations of the tools it uses.

### E11

- Location: There’s more to mathematics than rigour and proofs
- Quote: "The point of rigour is not to destroy all intuition; instead, it should be used to destroy bad intuition while clarifying and elevating good intuition."
- Key point: Rigor is not meant to destroy intuition, but to destroy bad intuition while clarifying and elevating good intuition.

### E12

- Location: There’s more to mathematics than rigour and proofs
- Quote: "It is only with a combination of both rigorous formalism and good intuition that one can tackle complex mathematical problems; one needs the former to correctly deal with the fine details, and the latter to correctly deal with the big picture."
- Key point: Rigorous formalism and good intuition are both indispensable: the former handles the details, the latter grasps the big picture.

### E13

- Location: Don’t base career decisions on glamour or fame
- Quote: "A famous unsolved problem is almost never solved ab nihilo."
- Key point: Famous hard problems are almost never solved out of nowhere; first build up techniques, intuition, partial results, and literature on simpler model problems.

### E14

- Location: Don’t prematurely obsess on a single “big problem” or “big theory”
- Quote: "spend most of your time on more feasible “low-hanging fruit”, which will build up your experience, mathematical power, and credibility for when you are ready to tackle the more ambitious projects."
- Key point: Until you have a solid publication record and a stable position, spend most of your time on more tractable problems to build experience, ability, and credibility before taking on grand problems.

### E15

- Location: Don’t prematurely obsess on a single “big problem” or “big theory”, appendix "on publishing proofs of famous open problems"
- Quote: "Can you state, in high-level terms (i.e. rising above all the technical details and computations), why the argument works?"
- Key point: Before releasing a major result, ask yourself: can you explain at a high level, away from the technical details, why the argument works?

### E16

- Location: Learn and relearn your field
- Quote: "Another useful way to learn more about one’s field is to take a key paper in that field, and perform a citation search on that paper (i.e. search for other papers that cite the key paper)."
- Key point: One way to get to know a field: run a citation search on a key paper to see which papers cite it.

### E17

- Location: Learn and relearn your field
- Quote: "Do you know why each of the hypotheses are necessary?"
- Key point: For a basic lemma, know why each hypothesis is necessary.

### E18

- Location: Write down what you’ve done
- Quote: "Eventually I resolved to write down (preferably on a computer) a sketch of any interesting argument I came across – not necessarily at a publication level of quality, but detailed enough that I could then safely forget about the details, and readily recover the argument from the sketch whenever the need arises."
- Key point: Write up interesting arguments you come across as drafts; they need not be publication quality, but should be detailed enough that you can reconstruct the whole argument from them later.

### E19

- Location: Use the wastebasket
- Quote: "Even an embarrassingly wrong piece of work (and I have a number of these, which fortunately have never made it as far as publication) should be stored privately somewhere, because you never know whether something salvageable can be extracted from it"
- Key point: Keep even embarrassingly wrong work archived privately; you may be able to salvage something useful from it later.

### E20

- Location: Solving mathematical problems
- Quote: "A good rule of thumb is that if you cannot adequately explain the solution of a problem to a classmate, then you haven’t really understood the solution yourself"
- Key point: If you cannot explain a solution clearly to a classmate, you do not really understand it yourself yet.

### E21

- Location: Be sceptical of your own work
- Quote: "Realise that special case y is related to (or at least analogous to) a problem z in another field of mathematics. Look up the literature on z, and ask experts in that field for the latest perspectives on that problem."
- Key point: One step in the typical process the author describes is discovering that a special case is related or similar to some problem in another field, then looking up the literature on that problem and consulting experts in that field.

### E22

- Location: Be sceptical of your own work
- Quote: "with many of the intermediate steps described here being significant publishable papers in their own right"
- Key point: In the course of solving a big problem, many of the intermediate steps are substantial, publishable papers in their own right.

### E23

- Location: Talk to your advisor
- Quote: "Your advisor is one of the best sources of guidance you have; not only in directly assisting you with your research topic, but in directing you (both explicitly and implicitly) to relevant researchers, conferences, publications, open problems, folklore, or other pieces of good mathematics."
- Key point: Your advisor is one of the best sources of guidance: not only helping with your project, but also pointing you to relevant researchers, conferences, literature, open problems, and the unwritten knowledge passed around in the community.

### E24

- Location: Talk to your advisor
- Quote: "If things get to the point that you are actively avoiding your advisor (or vice versa), that is a very bad sign."
- Key point: If you are actively avoiding your advisor (or vice versa), that is a very bad sign.

### E25

- Location: Talk to your advisor
- Quote: "If your advisor is unavailable, you should regularly discuss mathematical issues with at least one other mathematician instead, preferably an experienced one."
- Key point: When your advisor is unavailable, talk mathematics regularly with at least one other mathematician (ideally an experienced one).

### E26

- Location: Take the initiative
- Quote: "While you should talk to your advisor, you should not be completely reliant on him or her; after all, you are going to have to do mathematics primarily on your own once you graduate!"
- Key point: Talk with your advisor, but do not rely on your advisor completely; after graduating you will mostly have to do mathematics on your own.

### E27

- Location: Take the initiative
- Quote: "One specific suggestion I have is to subscribe (either by RSS, or by email) to be notified of new papers which appear on the arXiv in the subject areas that you are interested in."
- Key point: Subscribe (by RSS or email) to arXiv notifications of new papers in the areas you are interested in.

### E28

- Location: Make your work available
- Quote: "(Most journals now also have online availability, but given that the gap between preprint release and publication is measured in years, it still makes sense to have the preprint online too.)"
- Key point: The gap between posting a preprint and formal publication is measured in years, so preprints should also be put online.

### E29

- Location: Make your work available
- Quote: "the competition will come anyway, and this way you will at least have priority (note that submissions to servers such as the arXiv have reliable timestamps) and be acknowledged in citations."
- Key point: Posting a preprint publicly at least establishes priority (arXiv has reliable timestamps) and lets it be cited.

### E30

- Location: Make your work available
- Quote: "[If you don’t have a preprint of your work yet, or only have a non-publication-quality draft, then it is often better to hold off on any announcement until you have something more concrete to show to other mathematicians.]"
- Key point: If you do not yet have a preprint, only a draft not of publishable quality, it is best not to announce the result yet.

### E31

- Location: Make your work available
- Quote: "As to whether you should email your preprints to other experts in the field, I would only do this if the preprint is unquestionably of direct interest to that person (e.g. it solves a conjecture that they formulated)."
- Key point: Send a preprint to experts in the field only when it is genuinely directly relevant to them (for example, it resolves a conjecture they posed).

### E32

- Location: Attend talks and conferences, even those not directly related to your work
- Quote: "You need to know what’s going on elsewhere in mathematics, and what other mathematicians find interesting; this will often give valuable perspectives on your own work."
- Key point: Know what is happening elsewhere in mathematics and what other mathematicians find interesting; this often brings valuable perspective to your own work.

### E33

- Location: Attend talks and conferences, even those not directly related to your work
- Quote: "Oh, and don’t expect to understand 100% of any given talk, especially if it is in a field you are not familiar with; as long as you learn something, the effort is not wasted"
- Key point: Do not expect to understand all of a talk, especially in an unfamiliar area; if you learn even a little, it was not wasted.

### E34

- Location: Be patient
- Quote: "Any given problem generally requires months of effort in order to make satisfactory progress."
- Key point: A problem generally takes several months of effort before satisfactory progress is made.

### E35

- Location: Be patient
- Quote: "Thus it is not uncommon for months to pass with no visible progress; however by patiently eliminating fruitless avenues of attack, you are setting things up so that when the breakthrough does come, one can conclude the problem in relatively short order."
- Key point: It is not unusual to go months without visible progress; patiently ruling out dead ends means that when the breakthrough comes, you can wrap things up fairly quickly.

### E36

- Location: Don’t be afraid to learn things outside your field
- Quote: "One specific trick I have found very useful in this regard is to start with a paper that you know to be relevant, and either look through the introduction of the paper to find an important earlier paper in that subject in the references, or to do a citation search (for instance via Mathscinet) to find an important future paper in the subject that cites the paper you already know about."
- Key point: Start from a paper you know is relevant; go backward through its introduction and references to find earlier important papers, and forward with a citation search (for example, MathSciNet) to find important papers that cite it.

### E37

- Location: Take the initiative
- Quote: "Ultimately, it is better to follow the mathematics than to follow a mathematician."
- Key point: In the end, it is better to follow the mathematics itself than to follow a particular mathematician.
