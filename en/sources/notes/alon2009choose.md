# Alon 2009: How To Choose a Good Scientific Problem

- Source: `alon2009choose`, [original](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molcel.2009.09.013) (PDF on the author's lab website: [link](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/mcb/UriAlon/sites/mcb.UriAlon/files/uploads/nurturing/howtochoosegoodproblem.pdf))
- Version: Molecular Cell 35, 2009; the "in press" version hosted on the author's website, 3 pages.

### E1

- Location: The Two Dimensions of Problem Choice (p. 1)
- Quote: "The second axis is interest: the increase in knowledge expected from the project. We generally value science that ventures deep into unknown waters. Problems can be ranked in terms of the distance from the known shores, by the amount in which they increase verifiable knowledge."
- Key point: The value of a problem can be measured by how much new verifiable knowledge it is expected to add, that is, how far it ventures from the "shore" of the known.

### E2

- Location: The Two Dimensions of Problem Choice (p. 1)
- Quote: "We will compare problems by imagining two axes. The first is feasibility--that is, whether a problem is hard or easy, in units such as the expected time to complete the project. This axis is a function of the skills of the researchers and of the technology in the lab."
- Key point: The first axis for comparing problems is feasibility: how hard or easy a problem is, measured in expected time to completion, which depends on the researcher's skills and the lab's resources.

### E3

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "It is important to remember that problems that are easy on paper are often hard in reality, and that problems that are hard on paper are nearly impossible in reality."
- Key point: Problems that are easy on paper are often hard in reality, and problems that are hard on paper are nearly impossible in reality.

### E5

- Location: same as above (p. 1)
- Quote: "many projects in current research are of the easy-but-not-too-interesting variety, also known as ``low-hanging fruit.'' Many other projects in science today are unfortunately both difficult and have low interest, partially stemming from a view that hard equals good."
- Key point: Many projects are of the easy-but-not-too-interesting "low-hanging fruit" variety; many others are both hard and not very interesting, partly because of the notion that hard means good.

### E7

- Location: same as above (p. 1)
- Quote: "a beginning graduate student needs a problem that is easy; positive feedback can thus be rapidly provided, bolstering confidence."
- Key point: A beginning graduate student needs an easy problem, so that positive feedback comes quickly and builds confidence; later problems can move toward the "interesting" side.

### E8

- Location: Take Your Time (p. 1)
- Quote: "A common mistake made in choosing problems is taking the first problem that comes to mind. Since a typical project takes years even it if seems doable in months, rapid choice leads to much frustration and bitterness in our profession. It takes time to find a good problem, and every week spent in choosing one can save months or years later on."
- Key point: A common mistake is taking the first problem that comes to mind; a project usually takes years, so a hasty choice brings much frustration; spending an extra week choosing may save months or years later.

### E9

- Location: Take Your Time (pp. 1–2)
- Quote: "Do not commit to a problem before 3 months have elapsed. In these 3 months the new student or postdoc reads, discusses, [...] and plans."
- Key point: A rule in Alon's lab: new students and postdocs do not commit to a problem for 3 months after joining, but read, discuss, and plan.

### E10

- Location: Take Your Time (p. 2)
- Quote: "The author is under no illusion that everyone is free to choose their own problems, or has the time needed for an extended search."
- Key point: The author acknowledges that not everyone is free to choose their own problems or has time for an extended search.

### E11

- Location: The Subjectivity of the Interest Axis (p. 2)
- Quote: "The confusion is due to the mixing of two voices--one is a loud voice of the interests of those around us, in conferences, in our department, etc. The other is a faint voice in our breast, that says, ``This is interesting to me.''"
- Key point: When judging whether a problem is interesting, there are two voices: the loud voice of the people around you (at conferences, in your department), and your own quiet inner voice saying "I find this interesting."

### E12

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "One way to help listening to the inner voice is to ask: ``If I was the only person on earth, which of these problems would I work on?''"
- Key point: A self-test question: if I were the only person on earth, which of these problems would I work on?

### E13

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "Another good sign of the inner voice are ideas and questions that come back again and again to your mind for months or years."
- Key point: Ideas and questions that keep coming back to your mind for months or years are often better project candidates than ones that only just occurred to you.

### E14

- Location: same as above
- Quote: "In science, the more you interest yourself, the larger the probability that you will interest your audience."
- Key point: The more you interest yourself, the more likely you are to interest your audience.
