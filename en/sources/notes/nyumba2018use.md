### E2

- Location: §1 Introduction, p. 21
- Quote: "In contrast, in a focus group discussion, researchers adopt the role of a "facilitator" or a "moderator." In this setting, the researcher facilitates or moderates a group discussion between participants and not between the researcher and the participants."
- Key point: In a focus group the researcher acts as facilitator or moderator, guiding a discussion among participants rather than a question-and-answer exchange between researcher and participants.

### E3

- Location: §1 Introduction, p. 21
- Quote: "Consequently, there are cases where authors have confused and conflated these two distinctive methods (Parker & Tritter, 2006)."
- Key point: Some authors have confused and conflated focus groups and interviews, two distinct methods.

### E4

- Location: §2.1 Research design, p. 22
- Quote: "Thereafter, participant identification is perhaps the most critical step since the technique is largely based on group dynamics and synergistic relationships among participants to generate data"
- Key point: Identifying participants may be the most critical step, because the method relies on group dynamics and the way participants spark one another to generate data.

### E5

- Location: §2.1 Research design, p. 23
- Quote: "One potential drawback in focus group discussion is the lack of guarantee that all those recruited will attend the discussion. To overcome this, Rabiee (2004) recommends that researchers may over-recruit by 10–25%."
- Key point: Not everyone recruited will attend, so researchers may over-recruit by 10–25%.

### E6

- Location: §2.1 Research design, p. 23
- Quote: "With more than 12 members, the group becomes difficult to manage and may disintegrate into two or even three small groups, each having their own independent discussion."
- Key point: With more than 12 members a group becomes hard to manage and may split into two or three smaller groups with their own conversations.

### E7

- Location: §2.2 Data collection, p. 23
- Quote: "Focus group discussion requires a team consisting of a skilled facilitator and an assistant (Burrows & Kendall, 1997; Krueger, 1994)." / "the assistant's role includes observing non-verbal interactions and the impact of the group dynamics, and documenting the general content of the discussion"
- Key point: A focus group needs a skilled facilitator and an assistant; the assistant observes non-verbal interaction and the effect of group dynamics, and records the general content of the discussion.

### E8

- Location: §2.2 Data collection, p. 23
- Quote: "Participants are likely to suffer from fatigue when discussions are longer."
- Key point: Participants tire when discussions run long.

### E9

- Location: §3.7 Online focus groups, p. 24
- Quote: "However, these discussion platforms are only accessible to participants with access to the Internet and are prone to technical problems such as poor or loss of connectivity and failure to capture non-verbal data"
- Key point: Online focus groups reach only people with internet access, are prone to technical problems such as dropped connections, and struggle to capture non-verbal data.

### E10

- Location: §5 Results, p. 25
- Quote: "The number of participants per focus group ranged from 2 to 21 with a median of 10 participants. More than half of the studies (n = 101) did not report the sample size, whereas 55% (n = 93) did not report group size and 32% (n = 54) did not mention the number of focus group discussion sessions"
- Key point: Across the 170 conservation studies reviewed, groups ranged from 2 to 21 participants (median 10); more than half did not report sample size, 55% did not report group size, and 32% did not say how many sessions were held.

### E11

- Location: §7 Discussion, p. 27
- Quote: "However, the technique is also subject to "careless or inappropriate use," potential data manipulation, and exploitation of participants when researchers tend to assume that group consent represents individual consent (Kitzinger & Barbour, 1999)."
- Key point: The method can be misused, for example when researchers treat group consent as individual consent.

### E12

- Location: §10 Advantages and caveats, p. 28
- Quote: "Since focus group discussion depends on participants' dynamics, it should be avoided where participants are uneasy with each other or where social stigmatisation due to the disclosure may arise" / "Focus group discussion provides depth and insight, but cannot produce useful numerical results, hence must not be used where statistical data are required"
- Key point: Avoid focus groups when participants are uneasy with each other or disclosure could bring social stigma; they offer depth and insight but no useful numerical results, so they must not be used where statistical data are needed.

### E13

- Location: §10 Advantages and caveats, p. 29
- Quote: "However, focus group participants are sometimes reluctant to deal with sensitive topics in a discussion setting compared with an individual interview or a survey (18). Researchers must be aware of this constraint when planning and framing the group discussion questions."
- Key point: Compared with individual interviews or surveys, participants are sometimes reluctant to discuss sensitive topics in a group, which should be considered when designing questions.

### E14

- Location: §11 Recommendations, p. 29
- Quote: "Ability to remain impartial by getting involved while maintaining verbal and non-verbal objectivity."
- Key point: The facilitator must stay engaged while remaining verbally and non-verbally impartial.

### E15

- Location: §11 Recommendations, p. 30
- Quote: "These include dominance effect (a dominant individual shapes the discussion), halo effect (the perceived status of a group member influences the discussion), groupthink (the members in a group tend to think similarly to maintain group cohesion) among several others (Mukherjee et al., 2015). The facilitator (and/or assistant) should keep a keen eye out to spot and address such biases in the data collection phase."
- Key point: Group discussions are prone to biases such as the dominance effect (one strong individual shapes the discussion), the halo effect (a member's perceived status sways it), and groupthink (members converge to keep the peace); the facilitator and assistant should watch for and address them during data collection.

### E16

- Location: §11 Recommendations, p. 30
- Quote: "The review revealed that 144 out of 170 studies used focus group discussion alongside other techniques in the same study. In most of these studies, it was extremely difficult to tease out what component of the results and inferences were derived from the focus group discussion alone."
- Key point: Of the 170 studies, 144 combined focus groups with other techniques, and in most it was very hard to tell which results came from the focus groups alone.
