# Roodman 2003: Blind Analysis in Particle Physics

- Source: `roodman2003blind`, [original](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0312102)
- Version: PHYSTAT2003 conference paper (SLAC), arXiv:physics/0312102v1. The author later co-wrote the review Klein & Roodman 2005 (Annual Review of Nuclear and Particle Science), but we found no clearly legal open full text of it, so this project uses the conference paper instead.

### E1

- Location: §1 Introduction
- Quote: "A blind analysis is a measurement which is performed without looking at the answer. Blind analyses are the optimal way to reduce or eliminate experimenter's bias, the unintended biasing of a result in a particular direction."
- Key point: A blind analysis is a measurement done without looking at the answer; it is the best way to reduce experimenter's bias (unintentionally pushing a result in one direction).

### E2

- Location: §2 Experimenter's Bias
- Quote: "Experimenter's bias is defined as the unintended influence on a measurement towards prior results or theoretical expectations."
- Key point: Experimenter's bias is the unintended influence on a measurement toward prior results or theoretical expectations.

### E3

- Location: §2 Experimenter's Bias
- Quote: "If the cuts are set with knowledge of how that choice affects the results, experimenter's bias could occur."
- Key point: Setting selection cuts while knowing how they affect the result can introduce experimenter's bias.

### E4

- Location: §2 Experimenter's Bias
- Quote: "The numerical result, and how well it agrees with prior measurements or the Standard Model, contains no real information about the internal correctness of the measurement."
- Key point: The numerical result, and how well it agrees with earlier measurements or the Standard Model, says nothing about whether the measurement itself is correct.

### E5

- Location: §1 Introduction, Dunnington's example
- Quote: "A notable early use of a blind analysis in physics was in a measurement of the e/m of the electron, by Dunnington [...] Dunnington asked his machinist to arbitrarily choose an angle [...] Only when the analysis was complete, and Dunnington was ready to publish a result, did he accurately measure the hidden angle."
- Key point: An early example: measuring the electron's e/m, Dunnington had his machinist choose a key angle arbitrarily and measured it precisely only when the analysis was complete and ready for publication.

### E6

- Location: §3.1 Hidden Signal Box
- Quote: "The hidden signal box technique explicitly hides the signal region until the analysis is completed."
- Key point: The "hidden signal box" technique hides the region where the signal might appear until the analysis is complete.

### E7

- Location: §3.2 Hidden Answer
- Quote: "For precision measurements of parameters, a different technique for avoiding bias must be used. In this case, hiding the answer is often the appropriate method."
- Key point: For precision measurements of parameters, the usual approach is to hide the answer (for example, by adding an unknown offset to the result).

### E8

- Location: §3.4 Other Blind Methods
- Quote: "Other kinds of analyses are difficult to adapt to the methods described."
- Key point: Not every analysis adapts easily to these blind analysis methods.

### E9

- Location: §3.4 Other Blind Methods
- Quote: "One method is to fix the analysis on a sub-sample of the data, and then used the identical method on the full data sample."
- Key point: Another approach: fix the analysis on a sub-sample of the data, then apply the identical method to the full sample.

### E10

- Location: §4 Conclusion
- Quote: "While the collaboration had initial reservations about the blind analysis technique, it has now become a standard method for BABAR"
- Key point: The BABAR collaboration initially had reservations about blind analysis but later made it a standard method.
