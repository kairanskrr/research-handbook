# EGAP Methods Guide: 10 Things You Need to Know About Statistical Power

- Source: `egappower`, [original web page](https://methods.egap.org/guides/assessing-designs/power_en.html)
- Version: Evidence in Governance and Politics (EGAP) methods guide by Alexander Coppock; accessed October 2026. Locations give the section number.

### E1

- Location: §2 Why You Need It
- Quote: "Negative results from underpowered studies can be hard to interpret: Is there really no effect? Or is the study just not able to figure it out? Positive results from an underpowered study can also be misleading: conditional upon being statistically significant, an estimate from an underpowered study probably overestimates treatment effects."
- Key point: In underpowered studies, negative results are hard to interpret (is there really no effect, or can the study just not detect it?), and positive results can mislead, because significant estimates are likely to overstate the effect.

### E2

- Location: §5 When to Believe Your Power Analysis
- Quote: "The good news is that it is easy to find out how much your conclusions depend on your assumptions: simply vary your assumptions and see how the conclusions on power vary."
- Key point: Power calculations rest on assumptions; vary the assumptions and see how the power conclusions change to learn how much they depend on them.

### E3

- Location: §2 Why You Need It
- Quote: "Many disciplines have settled on a target power value of 0.80." / "While this convention is a useful benchmark, be sure that you are comfortable with the risks associated with an 80% expected success rate."
- Key point: Many disciplines target a power of 0.80; this is only a benchmark, so make sure you are comfortable with the risk implied by an 80% expected chance of success.

### E4

- Location: §6 How to Use Simulation to Estimate Power
- Quote: "We can tell our computers to literally run the experiment thousands of times and simply count how frequently our experiment comes up significant."
- Key point: You can estimate power by simulation: have the computer run the experiment thousands of times and count how often the result comes up significant.
