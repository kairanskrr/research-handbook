# Angrist & Pischke 2010: The Credibility Revolution in Empirical Economics

- Source: `angrist2010credibility`, [NBER working paper PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w15794/w15794.pdf); published version in Journal of Economic Perspectives 24(2), [journal page](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.24.2.3)
- Version: NBER Working Paper 15794 (March 2010). The journal PDF was blocked by the website, so excerpts follow the working paper; locations give section titles.

### E1

- Location: abstract
- Quote: "As we see it, the credibility revolution in empirical work can be traced to the rise of a design-based approach that emphasizes the identification of causal effects. Design-based studies typically feature either real or natural experiments and are distinguished by their prima facie credibility and by the attention investigators devote to making the case for a causal interpretation of the findings their designs generate."
- Key point: The authors argue that the "credibility revolution" in empirical economics comes from a design-based approach: an emphasis on identifying causal effects, usually with real or natural experiments, and a great deal of effort arguing why the results can be read causally.

### E2

- Location: abstract
- Quote: "Design-based studies are most often found in the microeconomic fields of Development, Education, Environment, Labor, Health, and Public Finance, but are still rare in Industrial Organization and Macroeconomics."
- Key point: Design-based studies are most common in the micro fields of development, education, environment, labor, health, and public finance, but still rare in industrial organization and macroeconomics (as of 2010).

### E3

- Location: introduction
- Quote: "But experiments are time consuming, expensive, and may not always be practical. It's difficult to imagine a randomized trial to evaluate the effect of immigrants on the economy of the host country. However, human institutions or the forces of nature can step into the breach with informative natural or quasi-experiments."
- Key point: Experiments are time-consuming, expensive, and sometimes impractical (for example, for studying the economic effects of immigration); then researchers can use "natural experiments" or "quasi-experiments" created by institutions or nature.

### E4

- Location: Better Research Design
- Quote: "The econometric methods that feature most prominently in quasi-experimental studies are instrumental variables, regression discontinuity methods, and differences-in-differences-style policy analysis."
- Key point: The three econometric methods most used in quasi-experimental studies are instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, and differences-in-differences.

### E5

- Location: Better Research Design
- Quote: "When using instrumental variables, for example, it's no longer enough to mechanically invoke a simultaneous equations framework, labeling some variables endogenous and others exogenous, without substantially justifying the exclusion restrictions and as-goodas-randomly-assigned assumptions that make instruments valid."
- Key point: With instrumental variables, it is not enough to label variables "endogenous" or "exogenous"; you must make a full case for the assumptions the instrument relies on: that it affects the outcome only through the channel of interest (the exclusion restriction), and that it is as good as randomly assigned.

### E6

- Location: Better Research Design
- Quote: "The regression discontinuity approach can be used when people are divided into groups based on a certain cutoff score, with those just above or just below the cutoff suddenly becoming eligible for a different treatment."
- Key point: Regression discontinuity applies when people are sorted into groups by a cutoff score: those just above and just below the cutoff abruptly receive different treatment.

### E7

- Location: Better Research Design
- Quote: "The key assumption that drives regression discontinuity estimation of causal effects is that individuals are otherwise similar on either side of the discontinuity"
- Key point: The key assumption of regression discontinuity: people on either side of the cutoff are otherwise similar, apart from treatment.

### E8

- Location: Better Research Design
- Quote: "Differences-in-differences policy analysis typically compares the evolution of outcomes in groups affected more and less by a policy change."
- Key point: Differences-in-differences compares how outcomes change before and after a policy in groups affected more and less by it.

### E9

- Location: Whither Sensitivity Analysis?
- Quote: "When using instrumental variables, we look at whether the instrument might have causal effects on the outcome in ways other than through the channel of interest" / "With differences-in-differences, we look for group-specific trends, since such trends can invalidate a comparison of changes across groups. In a regression discontinuity design, we look at factors like bunching at the cutoff point, which might suggest that the cutoff directly influenced behavior."
- Key point: Each design has its own weak spot to check: for instrumental variables, whether the instrument affects the outcome through other channels; for differences-in-differences, whether the groups already had different trends; for regression discontinuity, whether people bunch near the cutoff (a sign of manipulation).

### E10

- Location: More Transparent Discussion of Research Design
- Quote: "The query "What's your identification strategy?" and others like it are now heard routinely at empirical workshops and seminars."
- Key point: "What's your identification strategy?" is now a routine question at empirical workshops and seminars.

### E11

- Location: More Transparent Discussion of Research Design
- Quote: "Good designs have a beneficial side effect: they typically lend themselves to a simple explanation of empirical methods and a straightforward presentation of results."
- Key point: Good research designs have a side benefit: the methods are easy to explain and the results can be presented directly.

### E12

- Location: The Leamer Critique and His Proposed Remedies (paragraph on education production functions)
- Quote: "In what was arguably one of the better studies of the period, Summers and Wolfe (1977) report only the final results of an exhaustive specification search in their evaluation of the effect of school resources on achievement."
- Key point: An example of "specification search": trying all kinds of regression specifications and reporting only the final one chosen.

### E13

- Location: Whither Sensitivity Analysis?
- Quote: "Wide-net searches of this kind offer little basis for a causal interpretation."
- Key point: Wide-net searches that run many regressions to see which variables are significant offer little basis for a causal interpretation.

### E14

- Location: Mad About Macro
- Quote: "Many macroeconomists have abandoned traditional empirical work entirely, focusing instead on "computational experiments," as described in this journal by Kydland and Prescott (1996)." / "In a computational experiment, researchers choose a question, build a (theoretical) model economy, "calibrate" the model so that its behavior mimics the real economy along some key statistical dimensions, and then run a computational experiment by changing model parameters (for example, tax rates or the money supply rule) to address the original question." / "Whatever might be said in defense of this framework as a tool for clarifying the implications of economic models, it produces no direct evidence on the magnitude or existence of causal effects."
- Key point: Many macroeconomists abandoned traditional empirical work for "computational experiments": build a theoretical model economy, "calibrate" its parameters so it resembles the real economy on key statistics, then change policy parameters and see what happens. The authors think this clarifies a model's implications but does not directly provide evidence on causal effects.

### E15

- Location: Industrial Disorganization
- Quote: "A good structural model might tell us something about economic mechanisms as well as causal effects. But if the information about mechanisms is to be worth anything, the structural estimates should line up with those derived under weaker assumptions."
- Key point: A good structural model can tell us about both mechanisms and causal effects, but its estimates should agree with those from methods that rely on weaker assumptions.

### E16

- Location: Has the Research Design Pendulum Swung Too Far?
- Quote: "The first counterrevolutionary charge raises the question of external validity--the concern that evidence from a given experimental or quasi-experimental research design has little predictive value beyond the context of the original experiment. The second charge is that experimentalists are playing small ball while big questions go unanswered."
- Key point: Two main criticisms of the design-based approach: external validity (findings from one experiment may not hold in another setting), and focusing on small questions that are easy to identify while neglecting big ones.

### E17

- Location: Has the Research Design Pendulum Swung Too Far? / External Validity
- Quote: "A constructive response to the specificity of a given research design is to look for more evidence, so that a more general picture begins to emerge."
- Key point: The authors' response: when a single design is limited, look for more evidence, so that a more general picture gradually emerges.

### E18

- Location: Other Empirical Work in the Age of Heavy Metal
- Quote: "Indeed, we would be the first to admit that a well-done observational study can be more credible and persuasive than a poorly executed randomized trial."
- Key point: A well-done observational study can be more credible than a poorly executed randomized trial.

### E19

- Location: Industrial Disorganization
- Quote: "We find the empirical results generated by a good research design more compelling than the conclusions derived from a good theory, but we also hope to see industrial organization move towards stronger and more transparent identification strategies in a structural framework."
- Key point: The authors find empirical results from a good research design more compelling than conclusions derived from a good theory, while also hoping to see structural models adopt stronger, more transparent identification strategies.

### E20

- Location: Has the Research Design Pendulum Swung Too Far? / External Validity
- Quote: "The criticism here--made by a number of authors including Heckman (1997); Rosenzweig and Wolpin (2000); Heckman and Urzua (2009); and Deaton (2009)--is that in the quest for internal validity, design-based studies have become narrow or idiosyncratic."
- Key point: Heckman, Rosenzweig and Wolpin, Deaton, and others criticize that the design-based approach, in pursuit of internal validity, has made research narrow and idiosyncratic.

### E21

- Location: Industrial Disorganization
- Quote: "The first estimates a demand system for the product in question" / "Next, researchers postulate a model of market conduct, say, Bertrand–Nash price-based competition between different brands or products." / "Finally, industry behavior is simulated with and without the merger of interest."
- Key point: The dominant structural approach to merger analysis in industrial organization has three steps: estimate demand, postulate how firms compete, then simulate industry performance with and without the merger.
