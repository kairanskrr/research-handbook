# Sharp, Dittrich & de Souza 2016: The Role of Ethnographic Studies in Empirical Software Engineering

- Source: `sharp2016role`, [original](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.2016.2519887); author's accepted manuscript in the [ITU repository](https://researcher.itu.dk/ws/files/82419396/The_Role_of_Ethnographic_Studies_in_Empirical_Software_Engineering.pdf) (also in the Open University repository, [ORO 45166](https://oro.open.ac.uk/45166/))
- Version: IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering 42(8), 2016. We read the author's accepted manuscript, which may differ from the published version; locations give sections, and page numbers are manuscript pages.
- Note: Introduces ethnography to software engineering researchers: its main features, five dimensions of study design, and the role it can play in empirical software engineering.

### E1

- Location: §1 Introduction, p. 1
- Quote: "In the context of Computer Science ethnography has been adopted within the CSCW (Computer-Supported Cooperative Work) and HCI communities to conduct studies of the workplace and inform the design of computer applications"
- Key point: In computing, ethnography has been adopted by the computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW) and human-computer interaction (HCI) communities to study workplaces and inform application design.

### E2

- Location: §2.1, p. 3, footnote 1
- Quote: "First, people tend to say what they think their interviewer wants to hear. Second, they are inclined to create a rationalised account which may (or may not) reflect what is wanted, and to have selective memory."
- Key point: Interviews alone are not enough: interviewees tend to say what they think the interviewer wants to hear, to give rationalized accounts, and to have selective memory.

### E3

- Location: §2.3, p. 4
- Quote: "There are four main features of ethnographic research: the members’ point of view, a focus on the ordinary detail of life, the analytical stance, and production of ‘thick descriptions’ for academic accountability."
- Key point: Four main features of ethnography: the members' point of view, a focus on the ordinary detail of life, an analytical stance, and "thick descriptions" for academic accountability.

### E4

- Location: §2.3, The members' point of view, p. 4
- Quote: "The distinguishing feature of ethnography is its focus on the informants’ point of view, i.e., ethnography aims to understand what is, or is not, relevant, important, and painful for the informant."
- Key point: The distinguishing feature of ethnography is its focus on the informants' point of view: what is or is not relevant, important, and painful for them.

### E5

- Location: §2.3, The members' point of view, p. 4
- Quote: "Therefore, ethnographies do not need to last long anymore: in general, a couple of weeks is enough time to produce good results [84]. Assuming that studies require a long time to be conducted is a very common myth about ethnographic research."
- Key point: When studying a group from a culture close to your own, ethnography need not be long; a couple of weeks is usually enough for good results. "Ethnography always takes a long time" is a common myth.

### E6

- Location: §2.3, The members' point of view, p. 4
- Quote: "For instance, in Example Agile the ethnographic study of agile practices was conducted over a period of three weeks, with one week of full-time observation followed by two one-day visits over the following two weeks."
- Key point: Example: an ethnographic study of agile practices lasted three weeks: one week of full-time observation, then a one-day visit in each of the next two weeks.

### E7

- Location: §2.3, The members' point of view, p. 4
- Quote: "On the other hand, the ethnographer must be aware of his own assumptions and biases."
- Key point: When studying a familiar culture, the researcher must be aware of their own assumptions and biases.

### E8

- Location: §2.3, The ordinary detail of life, p. 4
- Quote: "This flexibility is often confounded with lack of rigour, a second myth about ethnographic research. [...] “The ethnographer enters the field with an open mind not an empty head.”"
- Key point: Data collection plans in ethnography need to be flexible, but flexibility does not mean lack of rigor; the researcher enters the field with an open mind, not an empty head.

### E9

- Location: §2.3, The analytical stance, p. 5
- Quote: "Ethnographers are not journalists who solely report what they have observed in a particular situation."
- Key point: An ethnographer is not a journalist who simply reports what they see and hear.

### E10

- Location: §2.3, The analytical stance, p. 5
- Quote: "In other words, there is a misconception that ethnography is purely descriptive; that it is only concerned with the description of what has been seen in the field. This is not correct."
- Key point: It is a misconception that ethnography only describes what is seen in the field; it also involves analysis.

### E11

- Location: §2.3, The analytical stance, p. 5
- Quote: "An ethnographic study in empirical software engineering will aim not simply to observe activity but to determine rationalities and explanations: what causes frustration, stops progress, or makes the team work well"
- Key point: Ethnography in software engineering goes beyond observing activities; it looks for the underlying rationale and explanations: what frustrates people, what hinders progress, what makes a team work well.

### E12

- Location: §2.3, Thick descriptions for academic accountability, p. 5
- Quote: "In other words, because of its analytical nature, the results of an ethnographic study are suitable for analytical generalization, but not suitable for statistical generalization."
- Key point: Ethnographic results support analytical generalization, not statistical generalization.

### E13

- Location: §2.3, Thick descriptions for academic accountability, p. 5
- Quote: "[Even if] one swallow does not make a summer, ... one swallow does prove the existence of swallows. And careful dissection of even one swallow may provide a great deal of reliable information about swallow anatomy"
- Key point: Citing Anzai & Simon: one swallow does not make a summer, but one swallow does prove that swallows exist, and carefully dissecting even one swallow can yield a great deal of reliable information about swallow anatomy.

### E14

- Location: §2.4, Participant or non-participant observation, p. 6
- Quote: "Observation is key to ethnographic studies and both participant and non-participant observation are legitimate forms of ethnography. Observation is almost always complemented with other forms of data collection such as interviews or document analysis."
- Key point: Observation is the core of ethnography, and it can be participant or non-participant; observation is almost always supplemented by other data collection methods such as interviews and document analysis.

### E15

- Location: §2.4, Participant or non-participant observation, p. 6
- Quote: "Participant observation in this context is more often interpreted as taking on a meaningful role within the community and engaging with the community’s everyday business. For instance, when observing software engineering practices, companies are very wary of letting non-employees access their sourcecode repositories"
- Key point: In software engineering, participant observation usually means taking on a meaningful role in the group and joining in day-to-day work; companies are often reluctant to give outsiders access to their code repositories.

### E16

- Location: §2.4, Participant or non-participant observation, p. 6
- Quote: "In this case the researcher might take over documentation tasks [129] or support the project through administration [75]."
- Key point: For example, the researcher might take on documentation work or provide administrative support to the project.

### E17

- Location: §2.4, The duration of the field study, p. 6
- Quote: "These examples illustrate that the researcher does not need to be located full-time at the study site, but that fewer days may be spent at the site, allowing time to start analyzing the data during the field study time."
- Key point: The researcher does not have to be on site full time; spending fewer days in the field lets you start analyzing data while the fieldwork is still going on.

### E18

- Location: §2.4, Space and location, p. 6
- Quote: "In global and open source software projects, online environments play a crucial role, and the concept of a field site changes: alongside the physical workplaces, the virtual environment needs to be regarded as a fieldsite"
- Key point: In globally distributed and open source projects, the online environment is also a field site.

### E19

- Location: §2.4, The ethnographers' intent, p. 7
- Quote: "Practitioners being studied may expect help with improving their practices, or addressing their problems and be puzzled if the researchers do not help them to improve, as they are perceived as peers with access to relevant knowledge about methods and tools."
- Key point: The practitioners being studied may expect the researcher to help them improve, and are puzzled when the researcher does not help.

### E20

- Location: §2.4, The ethnographers' intent, p. 7
- Quote: "It is therefore important that the researcher both is open about her research interest and her intent and also takes into account the way her research might impact the observed software development practice."
- Key point: Researchers should be open about their research interests and intentions, and consider the effect the study might have on the practices being observed.

### E21

- Location: §4.2 When is ethnography appropriate, p. 12
- Quote: "Ethnography puts cooperative, social and human aspects of software engineering practice in the centre, and is therefore very well-suited to any research question focusing on these aspects."
- Key point: Ethnography puts collaborative, social, and human factors at the center, which makes it well suited to research questions about these aspects.

### E22

- Location: §4.2, p. 12
- Quote: "For example, asking ‘How do software practitioners develop systems using XP?’ rather than ‘Is single programmer coding more productive than pair programming?’"
- Key point: Example: a question suited to ethnography is "how do practitioners develop systems using Extreme Programming," not "is solo programming more efficient than pair programming."

### E23

- Location: §4.2, p. 13
- Quote: "Radical changes in tools, techniques, methods and processes, cannot be supported by ethnographic research. Ethnographic methods rely on existing practices."
- Key point: Ethnography depends on existing practice, so it cannot support radical changes to tools, methods, and processes.

### E24

- Location: §4.3 Why a software engineer should consider being the ethnographer, p. 13
- Quote: "From the ethnographer’s point of view, a software engineer may be tempted to make assumptions or to be judgmental [115]. From the practitioners’ point of view, they will expect someone knowledgeable in the area to offer guidance"
- Key point: Drawbacks of a software engineer doing ethnography: the researcher may take things for granted or judge others, and practitioners will expect someone knowledgeable to give guidance.

### E25

- Location: §4.3, p. 13
- Quote: "Another is that access to the informants’ discussions and rationalities will be more meaningful, which in turn leads to more meaningful analysis."
- Key point: One advantage of a software engineer doing ethnography: they better understand the informants' discussions and thinking, which makes the analysis more meaningful.
