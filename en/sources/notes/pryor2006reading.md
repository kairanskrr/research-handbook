# Pryor: Guidelines on Reading Philosophy

- Source: `pryor2006reading`, [original](https://www.jimpryor.net/teaching/guidelines/reading.html)
- Version: Guide to reading on Jim Pryor's personal website; the page says it was last updated August 10, 2006; released under a Creative Commons license. Written for undergraduate philosophy students. Locations give subheadings.

### E1

- Location: Skim the Article to Find its Conclusion and Get a Sense of its Structure
- Quote: "A good way to begin when you're trying to read a difficult article is to first skim the article to identify what the author's main conclusion is."
- Key point: When reading a difficult article, skim it first to identify the author's main conclusion.

### E2

- Location: Skim the Article to Find its Conclusion and Get a Sense of its Structure
- Quote: "Try to identify these auxiliary arguments, and the claims they're intended to support; and try to avoid mistaking one of these auxiliary arguments for the author's main argument."
- Key point: Identify auxiliary arguments (arguments defending a premise) and the claims they support, and don't mistake an auxiliary argument for the main one.

### E3

- Location: Skim the Article to Find its Conclusion and Get a Sense of its Structure
- Quote: "The author's main conclusion is that Y's objection to the skeptic does not work. (Notice: the main conclusion isn't that skepticism is true.)"
- Key point: Example: an article's main conclusion is "Y's objection to the skeptic doesn't work," not "skepticism is true"; read precisely what the author is claiming.

### E4

- Location: Go Back and Read the Article Carefully
- Quote: "Take special notice of any unargued assumptions you think the author is relying on."
- Key point: Take special note of unargued assumptions the author relies on.

### E5

- Location: Go Back and Read the Article Carefully
- Quote: "In your notes, you might make a quick outline of the article's major argumentative "pieces." Draw arrows to diagram how you think those pieces fit together."
- Key point: In your notes, outline the article's major argumentative "pieces" and draw arrows showing how they fit together.

### E6

- Location: Go Back and Read the Article Carefully
- Quote: "You should expect to read a philosophy article more than once. I've been doing philosophy for more than ten years and I still have to read articles many times before I fully understand them."
- Key point: Expect to read a philosophy article more than once; after more than ten years in philosophy, Pryor still reads articles many times before fully understanding them.

### E7

- Location: Evaluate the Author's Arguments
- Quote: "Does he appeal to some premise which you think is false? (Why do you think it is false?) Is there some assumption which the author does not make explicit, but which you think is false? Does his argument equivocate or beg the question?"
- Key point: When evaluating an argument, ask: Is there a premise you think is false? An unstated assumption you think is false? Does the argument equivocate or beg the question?

### E8

- Location: Evaluate the Author's Arguments
- Quote: "You may never reach any definitive conclusion. But each time you try to make sense of the debate, you'll find you understand the issues a little bit better. That's the way we make progress in philosophy."
- Key point: You may never reach a definitive conclusion, but each attempt to make sense of the debate deepens your understanding; Pryor says that is how philosophy makes progress.

### E9

- Location: Evaluate the Author's Arguments
- Quote: "(This is why you often hear philosophers saying, "Even if we assume such-and-such for argument's sake, I still think X's argument fails, because...")"
- Key point: You can grant a premise for argument's sake and concentrate on attacking a more important one; philosophers often say "Even if we assume such-and-such for argument's sake, X's argument still fails."

### E10

- Location: Skim the Article to Find its Conclusion and Get a Sense of its Structure
- Quote: "Philosophers often provide auxiliary arguments, arguments for important premises they appeal to in support of their main conclusion."
- Key point: Philosophers often give auxiliary arguments: arguments for important premises their main conclusion relies on.

### E11

- Location: Evaluate the Author's Arguments
- Quote: "You'll start to get a sense of how the different views relate to each other and what their pros and cons are."
- Key point: With effort, you start to see how different views relate to each other and what their pros and cons are.

### E12

- Location: Go Back and Read the Article Carefully
- Quote: "Consider various interpretations of what he says. Are there any important ambiguities that his argument fails to take account of?"
- Key point: Consider different interpretations of what the author says; are there important ambiguities the argument fails to account for?

### E13

- Location: Evaluate the Author's Arguments
- Quote: "In such cases, you may want to provisionally accept one of the argument's premises, and move on to focus on other premises, which you think are more important or which are easier to criticize."
- Key point: You can provisionally accept one premise and focus your criticism on others that are more important or easier to criticize.
