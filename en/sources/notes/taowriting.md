# Tao: On writing (a series of web pages with advice on writing papers)

- Source: `taowriting`, [original](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/advice-on-writing-papers/)
- Version: The On writing series of pages on Terence Tao's blog "What's new," accessed 2026-10-06. Each page is a standalone piece of advice; locations give page titles.

### E1

- Location: Create lemmas
- Quote: "One should write the statement of a lemma in a way that makes it easy to use, rather than easy to prove."
- Key point: State lemmas so that they are easy to use, not so that they are easy to prove.

### E2

- Location: Create lemmas
- Quote: "Lemmas also provide a good opportunity to explicitly “recap” all the running hypotheses, assumptions, and notational conventions that have already been introduced in the argument"
- Key point: A lemma is a good place to restate all the current hypotheses and notational conventions.

### E3

- Location: Describe the results accurately
- Quote: "A paper should neither understate nor overstate its main results."
- Key point: A paper should neither understate nor overstate its main results.

### E4

- Location: Describe the results accurately
- Quote: "Conversely, if there are unsatisfactory aspects to the result (e.g. hypotheses too strong, or conclusions a little weaker than expected) these should also be stated honestly and openly, e.g. “We do not know if hypothesis H is actually necessary”."
- Key point: Be candid about where the results fall short (hypotheses too strong, conclusions somewhat weak).

### E5

- Location: Describe the results accurately
- Quote: "If for some reason you need to assert a non-trivial statement without proof or citation, it should be made clear that you are doing so"
- Key point: When you assert a nontrivial statement without proof or citation, tell the reader so explicitly.

### E6

- Location: Give appropriate amounts of detail
- Quote: "A paper should dwell at length (using plenty of English) on the most important, innovative, and crucial components of the paper, and be brief on the routine, expected, and standard components of the paper."
- Key point: Write the most important, novel, and crucial parts in detail (with plenty of explanation in words), and keep routine, standard parts brief.

### E7

- Location: Use good notation
- Quote: "A good rule of thumb is that any expression or concept which is used three or more times will probably benefit from introducing some notation to capture that expression or concept; conversely, an expression which is only used once probably does not need its own special notation."
- Key point: Rule of thumb: an expression or concept used three or more times is worth introducing notation for; one used only once usually is not.

### E8

- Location: Use good notation
- Quote: "One should strive to make one’s choices of notation compatible and consistent with notation already in the literature"
- Key point: Keep notation as consistent with the existing literature as possible.

### E9

- Location: Use good notation
- Quote: "Notation which is used globally should be defined in a notation section near the front of the paper, or in the introduction; notation which is only used locally (e.g. within a single section, or within a proof of a single lemma) should be defined close to where it is used"
- Key point: Define notation used throughout the paper together near the beginning; define notation used only locally near where it is used.

### E10

- Location: Use good notation
- Quote: "Try to avoid notation which is overly “cute” or “clever”."
- Key point: Avoid overly fancy, "clever" notation.

### E11

- Location: On "local" and "global" errors in mathematical papers, and how to detect them
- Quote: "This simple example illustrates the importance of getting key terms defined precisely in a mathematical argument."
- Key point: When an expression has two interpretations and the argument uses one in one place and the other elsewhere, the error cannot be pinned to any single step; key terms must be defined precisely.

### E12

- Location: On "local" and "global" errors in mathematical papers, and how to detect them
- Quote: "The other way to find an error in a proof is to obtain a “high level” or “global” objection, showing that the proof, if valid, would necessarily imply a further consequence that is either known or strongly suspected to be false."
- Key point: Another way to find errors is a "global" refutation: show that if the proof were valid, it would imply something known to be false or very likely false (for example, that a counterexample exists).

### E13

- Location: On "local" and "global" errors in mathematical papers, and how to detect them
- Quote: "Other examples of this type include proofs of some conclusion that mysteriously never use in any essential way a crucial hypothesis"
- Key point: If a proof never makes essential use of a key hypothesis, that is also a sign of a global error.

### E14

- Location: On "local" and "global" errors in mathematical papers, and how to detect them
- Quote: "It is generally good practice to try to structure a proof to be fault tolerant with respect to local errors"
- Key point: Organize proofs as far as possible to be robust to local errors: if one step is wrong, the whole paper does not collapse, and valuable partial results remain.

### E15

- Location: On "local" and "global" errors in mathematical papers, and how to detect them
- Quote: "But when one is testing an argument for errors or other objections, then it is perfectly acceptable to use heuristics, hand-waving, intuition, or other non-rigorous means to locate and describe errors."
- Key point: Make constructing a proof as rigorous as possible; but when checking an argument for errors, it is perfectly fine to use non-rigorous means such as heuristics and intuition.

### E16

- Location: Write a rapid prototype first
- Quote: "First, one writes a bare-bones “skeleton” or “prototype” of the paper as quickly as possible; this prototype has the (approximate) statements of all the key lemmas, propositions, and theorems, as well as all key definitions, but all the proofs are omitted, or sketched in very informal “notes to self”."
- Key point: First write the skeleton of the paper as quickly as possible: (rough) statements of all key definitions, lemmas, and theorems, with proofs omitted or only in sketch notes for yourself.

### E17

- Location: Submit to an appropriate journal
- Quote: "It is always tempting to submit a paper to a prestigious journal, but if the paper makes only a borderline case for publication in this journal, then the net result may be a lengthy process, critical reviews holding the paper to a very high standard, and ultimate rejection of the paper."
- Key point: Submitting a paper that is only borderline for a top journal may result in a long process, review to very high standards, and ultimately rejection.

### E18

- Location: Submit to an appropriate journal
- Quote: "It is also a good idea to check that the editorial board of the journal you are submitting to contains at least one member who is expert enough in the fields that your paper is in that he or she can judge its quality appropriately and send it to a good referee."
- Key point: Before submitting, check whether the journal's editorial board includes someone who knows your area, who can judge the quality and find suitable referees.

### E19

- Location: Submit to an appropriate journal
- Quote: "Is the paper written so that it is easy to check that the arguments are correct?"
- Key point: The first question in judging what level of journal a paper suits: is it written so that the correctness of the argument is easy to check?

### E20

- Location: Submit to an appropriate journal
- Quote: "Generally speaking, it is not recommended to simultaneously submit two unrelated papers to the same journal"
- Key point: It is generally not advisable to submit two unrelated papers to the same journal at the same time.

### E21

- Location: Submit a final draft, not a first draft
- Quote: "Even if you are for some reason in a hurry to publish your paper, a poorly prepared paper will have a greater chance of rejection, may incur demands for major revisions, and will in general slow down the processing of your paper to the extent that it would have been faster to wait until the paper was in a more final form before submission."
- Key point: Even if you are in a hurry to publish, a poorly prepared paper is more likely to be rejected or to need major revision, which ends up slower; better to wait until it is mature before submitting.
