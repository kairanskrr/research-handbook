# Torchiano et al. 2017: Lessons Learnt in Conducting Survey Research

- Source: `torchiano2017lessons`, [arXiv:1702.05744](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.05744)
- Version: arXiv v3 (2017-02-24), published at CESI@ICSE 2017

### E1

- Location: §II Survey Research: Basic Terms and Concepts
- Quote: "surveys do not entail an active intervention component – differently than, e.g., an experiment where one or more factors are applied and controlled."
- Key point: Surveys are observational and do not actively intervene; this differs from experiments, which manipulate and control factors.

### E2

- Location: §III-A-1, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "Too often, they use survey research to focus on aspects that can be quantiﬁed by numbers. Such a quantiﬁcation then can lead to the illusion of treating the data as if it was revealed via experimentations, seeing causal dependencies where there might be none and over-interpreting the ﬁndings."
- Key point: Novices often focus only on the quantifiable aspects, which creates the illusion that survey data are experimental data; they then see causal relationships that do not exist and over-interpret the results.

### E3

- Location: §III-A-1, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "That is, instead of using surveys to ask normative questions ("What should we do?"), they should ask descriptive questions ("What is happening in your environment?"), and especially explanatory questions ("Why is this happening?")."
- Key point: Surveys should not ask normative questions ("what should we do") but descriptive ones ("what is happening where you are"), and especially explanatory ones ("why is this happening").

### E4

- Location: §III-A-2, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "The question "Who can best provide you with the information you need?" should drive the selection rather than "Who are the subjects most likely available to participate?". It is easier to handle a small response rate than useless responses."
- Key point: When choosing the target population, ask "who can best provide the information I need," not "who is most likely to be available to take part"; a low response rate is easier to deal with than useless answers.

### E5

- Location: §III-B-1 Identifying the Unit of Analysis
- Quote: "Surveying a random sample composed of developers is different from surveying developers from a random sample of software houses." [...] "Having the wrong units of analysis may render the survey data useless for the addressed questions."
- Key point: Randomly sampling developers is not the same as surveying developers from randomly sampled companies; choose the wrong unit of analysis and the data may be useless for the research question.

### E6

- Location: §III-C Sampling
- Quote: "Suitable sampling frames are rarely available in SE research."
- Key point: Software engineering research rarely has a suitable sampling frame.

### E7

- Location: §III-C Sampling
- Quote: "not present any bias on including on its database preferentially only subsets of the target population. Unequal criteria for including search units mean unequal sampling opportunities,"
- Key point: A good source for the list should not be biased toward including certain subgroups of the target population; if inclusion criteria vary, people have different chances of being sampled.

### E8

- Location: §III-C Sampling
- Quote: "it is important to establish signiﬁcantly higher sample sizes, considering the expectation of a very low participation rate (typically less than 10%)."
- Key point: For voluntary surveys of industry practitioners, participation rates are usually below 10%, so plan to send out many more invitations.

### E9

- Location: §III-D-1 Simplicity, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "As much as possible, prefer to design short questions regarding a single concept and avoid double-barrelled questions, which can confound the answers." [...] "Passive voices and double negative questions should be avoided"
- Key point: Keep questions as short as possible and ask about only one concept per question, avoiding double-barreled questions; avoid passive voice and double negatives.

### E10

- Location: §III-D-1 Simplicity, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "two distinct constructs are merged in a single question is: "In your opinion, do you agree or disagree that code refactoring is a need as well as code smell detection?""
- Key point: Counterexample: cramming two concepts, refactoring and code smell detection, into one question.

### E11

- Location: §III-D-2 Clarity, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "An example of a biased item is: "Do you prefer working in projects following agile methods or those following usual non-agile approaches?". Avoiding bias can be achieved by only phrasing questions that do not suggest likely answers or responses."
- Key point: Leading questions hint at the answer (the example calls non-agile the "usual" practice); use only wording that does not suggest an answer.

### E12

- Location: §III-D-2 Clarity, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "Such sensitive questions can include in which company the respondents work, what respondents’ income are, or opinions about their organisation or their management." [...] "if useful to answer the particular research questions, the relation should be made clear to the respondents."
- Key point: Sensitive questions (the respondent's company, income, views on their organization and management, and so on) may make people drop out; when they are truly needed, explain to respondents how they relate to the research question.

### E13

- Location: §III-D-3 Effortless, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "the questions should deal with recent events rather than events that lie far in the past"
- Key point: Questions should ask about recent events, not things from long ago.

### E14

- Location: §III-D-4 Responsiveness, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "Whenever an ordinal scale is used, it is important to label exactly all the options." [...] "We also learnt that it is useful to use Likert scales when a construct can be represented as a general agreement with a statement. These come, however, as a measure with limited precision."
- Key point: With ordinal scales, label every option with clear words; Likert scales are suited to measuring agreement with a statement, but their precision is limited.

### E15

- Location: §III-D-4 Responsiveness, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "A better question would be "What is your experience with X?" with an ordinal response set ranging from "none" to "very high". In our experience, the best option is to use ranges as shown in Table I where clear reference points are deﬁned."
- Key point: When asking about experience, "how much experience do you have with X" is better than "I am very experienced with X (agree/disagree)"; ideally use ranges with clear reference points (such as "1 to 3 years").

### E16

- Location: §III-E-1 Controlling participation, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "it is impossible to control who will read the invitation message, thus, hindering the control of the recruited sample." [...] "In such a case, it was not possible to compute any response rates and an extra effort was required to compare the features of the responding population to that of the target population."
- Key point: Casting a wide net through mailing lists, forums, and the like gives no control over who sees the invitation, makes the response rate impossible to calculate, and requires an extra comparison of respondents' characteristics with the target population.

### E17

- Location: §III-E-1 Controlling participation, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "One way to deal with this problem is via technical mechanisms as most web-based questionnaire tools allow the deﬁnition of unique tokens to the population."
- Key point: You can use the survey tool to give each invitee a unique access code, to prevent invitations from being forwarded freely.

### E18

- Location: §III-E-2 Stimulating participation, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "However, if a tool is already mentioned in the invitation, then people not using it tend to self-exclude themselves: it brings to an under-estimation of the adoption rate."
- Key point: If the invitation mentions a particular tool, people who do not use it tend to opt out, distorting the measured adoption rate (the original says it is underestimated).

### E19

- Location: §III-E-2 Stimulating participation, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "it is possible to offer rewards (e.g. rafﬂes, payments, sharing results); this makes the less motivated participants more likely to be involved, thus avoiding a possible selfexclusion bias."
- Key point: Incentives such as prize draws, payments, or sharing the results can get less motivated people to take part too, avoiding self-exclusion bias.

### E20

- Location: §III-E-2 Stimulating participation, Lessons Learnt
- Quote: "while a ﬁrst reminder (typically between 4 and 8 days) may signiﬁcantly increase the response rate, a third reminder can improve the response rate by a small amount only. Avoid reminding those who already have participated"
- Key point: A first reminder (usually after 4 to 8 days) noticeably raises the response rate, while a third reminder has little effect; do not remind people who have already responded.
