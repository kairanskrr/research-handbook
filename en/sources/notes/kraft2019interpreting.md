# Kraft 2019: Interpreting Effect Sizes of Education Interventions

- Source: `kraft2019interpreting`, [original PDF](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/Interpreting%20Effect%20Sizes%20-%20August%2019%20FINAL_0.pdf)
- Version: EdWorkingPaper No. 19-10, updated August 2019 (a public working paper of the Annenberg Institute at Brown University). Published version: Educational Researcher 49(4), 2020; it is paywalled, so we read only the working paper. Locations give working paper page numbers.

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "Researchers commonly interpret effect sizes by applying benchmarks proposed by Cohen over a half century ago. However, effects that are small by Cohen's standards are large relative to the impacts of most field-based interventions."
- Key point: Researchers commonly interpret effect sizes using benchmarks Cohen proposed over half a century ago, but effects that are "small" by Cohen's standards are large relative to most field-based interventions.

### E2

- Location: pp. 2–3
- Quote: "The default approach to evaluating the magnitude of effect sizes is to apply a set of benchmarks proposed by Jacob Cohen over a half century ago (0.2 Small, 0.5 Medium, 0.8 Large) (Cohen, 1969)."
- Key point: The default approach applies Cohen's benchmarks: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.

### E3

- Location: p. 3
- Quote: "However, Cohen's standards are based on a handful of small, tightly controlled lab experiments in social psychology from the 1960s performed largely on undergraduates."
- Key point: Cohen's benchmarks come from a handful of small, tightly controlled social psychology lab experiments from the 1960s, mostly on undergraduates.

### E4

- Location: p. 3
- Quote: "Researchers design studies without sufficient statistical power to detect realistic effect sizes."
- Key point: One consequence of benchmarks that are too large: researchers design studies without enough statistical power to detect realistic effect sizes.

### E5

- Location: p. 6
- Quote: "For example, Lortie-Forgues and Inglis (2019) found an average effect size of only 0.06 SD among 141 RCTs funded by IES and the UK-based Education Endowment Foundation."
- Key point: Kraft cites Lortie-Forgues and Inglis 2019: across 141 randomized controlled trials funded by the US Institute of Education Sciences (IES) and the UK Education Endowment Foundation, the average effect size was only 0.06 standard deviations.

### E6

- Location: p. 8
- Quote: "But translations are not interpretations. They are simple unit conversions that leave the interpretation to the reader and allow considerable room for disagreement."
- Key point: Converting effect sizes into units such as "months of learning" is a translation, not an interpretation; it leaves the interpretation to the reader and much room for disagreement.

### E7

- Location: p. 9 (Guideline 1)
- Quote: "It is incumbent on researchers reporting effect sizes to clarify which type their statistic describes, and it is important that research consumers do not assume effect sizes inherently represent causal relationships."
- Key point: Researchers reporting effect sizes must make clear whether they describe correlation or a causal effect, and readers should not assume effect sizes represent causal relationships.

### E8

- Location: p. 10 (Guideline 2)
- Quote: "Even among measures of student achievement, effect sizes for researcher-designed and specialized topic tests aligned with the treatment are often two to four times larger than effects on broad standardized state tests"
- Key point: Effect sizes on researcher-designed tests closely aligned with the intervention are often two to four times larger than those on broad standardized state tests.

### E9

- Location: p. 11 (Guideline 2)
- Quote: "Outcomes assessed immediately after an intervention ends are likely to show larger effects than outcomes captured months or years later"
- Key point: Outcomes measured right after an intervention ends tend to show larger effects than those measured months or years later.

### E10

- Location: p. 13 (Guideline 3)
- Quote: "Targeted interventions and small-scale efficacy trials generally produce larger effect sizes than universal interventions because they target study participants that are most likely to benefit and because there is less variation in outcomes among smaller, non-representative samples"
- Key point: Targeted interventions and small-scale efficacy trials generally produce larger effect sizes than universal interventions, because they target those most likely to benefit and their samples are more homogeneous.

### E11

- Location: p. 13 (Guideline 3)
- Quote: "Whenever possible, researchers should present effects standardized at the student level, irrespective of the level of treatment or the unit of analysis."
- Key point: Whenever possible, standardize effect sizes at the student level, regardless of the level of treatment or the unit of analysis.

### E12

- Location: p. 16 (Guideline 3)
- Quote: "Expect studies that report the effect of offering an intervention to have smaller effect sizes than studies that report the effect of receiving an intervention."
- Key point: The effect of offering an intervention (intention to treat) is usually smaller than the effect of receiving it.

### E13

- Location: p. 17 (Guideline 4)
- Quote: "Effect sizes from lower-cost interventions are more impressive than similar effects from more costly programs."
- Key point: For similar effects, lower-cost interventions are more impressive.

### E14

- Location: p. 17 (Guideline 5)
- Quote: "One of the most consistent findings in the education literature is that effects decrease when smaller targeted programs are taken to scale"
- Key point: One of the most consistent findings in education research is that effects decrease when small targeted programs are taken to scale.

### E15

- Location: pp. 20–21
- Quote: "I propose the following benchmarks for effect sizes from causal studies of pre-K–12 education interventions evaluating effects on student achievement: less than 0.05 is Small, 0.05 to less than 0.20 is Medium, and 0.20 or greater is Large. These proposed benchmarks are based on the distribution of 1,942 effect sizes from 747 RCTs evaluating education interventions with standardized test outcomes"
- Key point: For causal studies of pre-K–12 interventions with achievement outcomes, Kraft proposes new benchmarks: below 0.05 is small, 0.05 to below 0.20 is medium, and 0.20 or above is large; they are based on 1,942 effect sizes from 747 randomized controlled trials with standardized test outcomes.

### E16

- Location: p. 21
- Quote: "As shown in Table 1, these values divide the overall distribution, with a median of 0.10 SD, into approximate thirds"
- Key point: The median of these effect sizes is 0.10 standard deviations, and the new benchmarks divide them into roughly equal thirds.

### E17

- Location: p. 21
- Quote: "One in four effect sizes from RCTs of education interventions with standardized test outcomes described in Table 1 are zero or negative, with many more small, positive effects that cannot be distinguished from zero."
- Key point: One in four effect sizes in these trials is zero or negative, and many more small positive effects cannot be distinguished from zero.

### E18

- Location: p. 22
- Quote: "Effect sizes from studies with samples greater than 2,000 students are several times smaller than studies with 100 students or fewer (medians of 0.03 vs. 0.24 SD)."
- Key point: Studies with more than 2,000 students have effect sizes several times smaller than those with 100 or fewer (medians of 0.03 vs. 0.24).

### E19

- Location: p. 22
- Quote: "RCTs funded by the U.S. Department of Education, which requires scholars to pre-register their research design and report their findings, have a median effect size of 0.03 across 139 effect sizes from 49 RCTs."
- Key point: Across 139 effect sizes from 49 randomized controlled trials funded by the US Department of Education, which requires preregistration and reporting of results, the median effect size is 0.03.

### E20

- Location: pp. 22–23
- Quote: "These patterns suggest that effects of 0.15 or even 0.10 SD should be considered large and impressive when they arise from large-scale field experiments that are pre-registered and examine broad achievement measures."
- Key point: For preregistered, large-scale field experiments with broad achievement measures, effects of 0.15 or even 0.10 standard deviations should be considered large and impressive.

### E21

- Location: p. 26 (Conclusion)
- Quote: "Effect sizes that are equal in magnitude are rarely equal in importance."
- Key point: Effect sizes that are equal in magnitude are rarely equal in importance.

### E22

- Location: p. 5
- Quote: "Most basically, effect sizes are a measure of differences in means between two subgroups divided by the standard deviation of the measure of interest"
- Key point: Most basically, an effect size is the difference in means between two groups divided by the standard deviation of the measure.
