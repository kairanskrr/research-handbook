# Kleinheksel et al. 2020: Demystifying Content Analysis

- Source: `kleinheksel2020demystifying`, [full text (PubMed Central)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7055418/)
- Version: American Journal of Pharmaceutical Education 84(1), article 7113, 2020 (DOI 10.5688/ajpe7113, checked against Crossref); authors Kleinheksel, Rockich-Winston, Tawfik, Wyatt. An introductory methods article for pharmacy education researchers, from health professions education. Locations give the section headings of the PMC full text.

### E1

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "Content analysis is a method designed to identify and interpret meaning in recorded forms of communication by isolating small pieces of the data that represent salient concepts and then applying or creating a framework to organize the pieces in a way that can be used to describe or explain a phenomenon."
- Key point: Content analysis identifies and interprets meaning in recorded communication by isolating small pieces of data that represent salient concepts, then applying or building a framework that organizes them to describe or explain a phenomenon.

### E2

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "Content analysis was originally introduced as a strictly quantitative method, recording counts to measure the observed frequency of pre-identified targets in consumer research."
- Key point: Content analysis began as a strictly quantitative method that counted how often pre-identified targets appeared.

### E3

- Location: Qualitative Content Analysis
- Quote: "In much of the literature, manifest content analysis is defined as describing what is occurring on the surface, what is and literally present, and as “staying close to the text.”" / "It is content that can be recognized and counted with little training."
- Key point: Manifest content analysis describes what is on the surface and literally present, "staying close to the text"; such content can be recognized and counted with little training.

### E4

- Location: Qualitative Content Analysis
- Quote: "The practical distinction between quantitative and qualitative manifest content analysis is the intention behind the analysis. The quantitative method seeks to generate a numerical value to either cite prevalence or use in statistical analyses, while the qualitative method seeks to identify a construct or concept within the text using specific words or phrases for substantiation, or to provide a more organized structure to the text being described."
- Key point: The practical difference between quantitative and qualitative content analysis is intent: the quantitative kind seeks numbers to show prevalence or feed statistics, while the qualitative kind seeks to identify a concept in the text, backed by the text's own words, or to give the text a clearer structure.

### E5

- Location: Qualitative Content Analysis
- Quote: "Latent content analysis is most often defined as interpreting what is hidden deep within the text. In this method, the role of the researcher is to discover the implied meaning in participants' experiences."
- Key point: Latent content analysis interprets meaning hidden deep in the text; the researcher's job is to uncover what is implied.

### E6

- Location: Creating and Using Codes
- Quote: "If studying a phenomenon that has already been described through an existing framework, codes can be created a priori using theoretical frameworks or concepts identified in the literature. If there is no existing framework to apply, codes can emerge during the analytical process."
- Key point: If the phenomenon has already been described by an existing framework, codes can be created in advance from theory or concepts in the literature; if there is no framework, codes can emerge during analysis.

### E7

- Location: Creating and Using Codes
- Quote: "While there is no one way to decide what qualifies as a meaning unit, researchers typically define units of meaning differently depending on what kind of analysis is being conducted." / "In written text, such as student reflections or course evaluation data, the researcher must decide if the text should be divided into phrases or sentences, or remain as paragraphs."
- Key point: There is no single way to define a meaning unit; it depends on the kind of analysis, and for written text you must decide whether to divide it into phrases, sentences, or paragraphs.

### E8

- Location: Manifest Content Analysis
- Quote: "A codebook (Table 2) is created with the following columns: name of code, code description, and examples of the code. This codebook helps an individual researcher to approach their analysis systematically, but it can also facilitate coding by multiple coders who would apply the same rules outlined in the codebook to the coding process."
- Key point: A codebook has at least three columns, code name, description, and examples; it makes a single researcher's analysis more systematic and lets multiple coders apply the same rules.

### E9

- Location: Manifest Content Analysis
- Quote: "To ensure that all coders are working in tandem, they must establish inter-rater reliability as part of their training process." / "After reviewing the codebook and receiving instruction, everyone on the team individually codes the same piece of data."
- Key point: With multiple coders, inter-rater reliability is established during training: after reviewing the codebook and receiving instruction, everyone independently codes the same piece of data.

### E10

- Location: Manifest Content Analysis
- Quote: "While calculating percentage agreement has sometimes been used to establish inter-rater reliability, most publication editors require more rigorous statistical analysis (eg, Krippendorf's alpha, or Cohen's kappa)." / "the choice depends on the number of coders, the sample size, and the type of data to be analyzed."
- Key point: Percent agreement is sometimes used to establish reliability, but most editors require more rigorous statistics such as Krippendorff's alpha or Cohen's kappa; the choice depends on the number of coders, the sample size, and the type of data.

### E11

- Location: How to Choose a Methodological Approach to Content Analysis
- Quote: "To determine which approach a researcher should take in their content analysis, two decisions need to be made. First, researchers must determine their goal for the analysis. Second, the researcher must decide where they believe meaning is located."
- Key point: Choosing an approach to content analysis takes two decisions: what the goal of the analysis is, and where you believe meaning is located (on the surface or beneath it).

### E12

- Location: Other Aspects to Consider, Navigating Terminology
- Quote: "Hsieh and Shannon proposed three qualitative approaches to content analysis: conventional, directed, and summative." / "In conventional content analysis, the researcher does not use preconceived categories because existing theory or literature are limited. In directed content analysis, the researcher attempts to further describe a phenomenon already addressed by theory, applying a deductive approach and using identified concepts or codes from exiting research to validate the theory. In summative content analysis, a descriptive approach is taken, identifying and quantifying words or content in order to describe their context."
- Key point: Hsieh and Shannon describe three qualitative approaches: conventional (no preconceived categories, because theory is limited), directed (deductive coding with concepts from existing theory, to extend or validate it), and summative (identifying and counting words or content to describe their context).

### E13

- Location: Other Aspects to Consider, Ensuring Quality
- Quote: "In manifest content analysis, reliability is necessary but not sufficient to establish validity. Because there is little judgment required of the coders, lack of high inter-rater agreement among coders will render the data invalid. Additionally, coder fatigue is a common threat to manifest content analysis because the coding is clerical and repetitive in nature."
- Key point: In manifest content analysis, reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity; since coding needs little judgment, low inter-rater agreement makes the data invalid, and because the work is clerical and repetitive, coder fatigue is a common threat.

### E14

- Location: Other Aspects to Consider, Ensuring Quality
- Quote: "For latent pattern content analysis, validity and reliability are inversely related. Greater reliability is achieved through more detailed coding rules to improve consistency, but these rules may diminish the accessibility of the coding to consumers of the research."
- Key point: In latent content analysis, reliability and validity trade off: more detailed coding rules raise consistency but can make the coding less accessible to readers of the research.

### E15

- Location: Other Aspects to Consider, Ensuring Quality
- Quote: "Stability is another threat, which occurs when coders make different judgments as time passes. To reduce this risk, allowing for recoding at a later date can increase the consistency and stability of the codes."
- Key point: Stability is another threat, since coders' judgments drift over time; recoding the material at a later date can improve consistency and stability.

### E16

- Location: Other Aspects to Consider, Ensuring Quality
- Quote: "Researchers can increase the reproducibility of their codebook by creating a detailed audit trail, including descriptions of the methods used to create and define the codes, materials used for the training of the coders, and steps taken to ensure inter-rater reliability."
- Key point: A detailed audit trail makes a codebook more reproducible: record how codes were created and defined, what materials were used to train coders, and what steps were taken to ensure inter-rater reliability.

### E17

- Location: Other Aspects to Consider, Ensuring Quality
- Quote: "The terms “reliability” and “validity” are typically not used because they reflect the positivist quantitative paradigm. In qualitative research, the preferred term is “trustworthiness,” which is comprised of the concepts of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability"
- Key point: Qualitative research usually avoids "reliability" and "validity" and speaks of "trustworthiness," made up of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability.
