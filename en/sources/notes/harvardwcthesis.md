# Harvard College Writing Center: Thesis

- Source: `harvardwcthesis`, [Original](https://writingcenter.fas.harvard.edu/thesis)
- Version: Web page in the Harvard College Writing Center's Strategies for Essay Writing series, no author named; the original URL returned 403 to command-line access, so it was read via an Internet Archive snapshot from February 2024

### E1

- Location: Paragraph 1
- Quote: "A strong thesis is arguable, which means a thoughtful reader could disagree with it and therefore needs your careful analysis of the evidence to understand how you arrived at this claim."
- Key point: A strong thesis is arguable: a thoughtful reader could disagree, so it takes your careful analysis of the evidence to persuade them.

### E2

- Location: Paragraph 3
- Quote: "If your thesis is descriptive, then you will not need to convince your readers of anything—you will be naming or summarizing something your readers can already see for themselves."
- Key point: A descriptive thesis does not need to convince anyone; it just names or summarizes what readers can already see for themselves.

### E3

- Location: Paragraph 3
- Quote: "If your thesis is too narrow, you won’t be able to explore your topic in enough depth to say something interesting about it. If your thesis is too broad, you may not be able to support it with evidence from the available sources."
- Key point: A thesis that is too narrow can't say anything interesting; one that is too broad can't be supported by the available sources.

### E4

- Location: Paragraph 2
- Quote: "When you frame that question or problem for your readers, you are telling them what is at stake in your argument—why your question matters and why they should care about the answer."
- Key point: When you frame the question, you tell readers what is at stake in your argument: why the question matters and why they should care about the answer.

### E5

- Location: Questions to ask about your thesis
- Quote: "Is the thesis supportable with evidence from the text rather than with generalizations or outside research?"
- Key point: Self-check: can the thesis be supported with evidence from the text rather than with generalizations or outside research?

### E6

- Location: Questions to ask about your thesis
- Quote: "Would anyone want to read a paper in which this thesis was developed? That is, can you explain what this paper is adding to our understanding of a problem, question, or topic?"
- Key point: Self-check: would anyone want to read this paper? Can you explain what it adds to our understanding of a problem?
