# Davies & Gray 2015: Don't let spurious accusations of pseudoreplication limit our ability to learn from natural experiments (and other messy kinds of ecological monitoring)

- Source: `davies2015pseudoreplication`, [Original](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.1782)
- Version: Ecology and Evolution 5(22): 5295–5304, 2015, CC BY open access. Text taken from Europe PMC (PMC6102510). This is an opinion article with an online survey the authors circulated through personal networks, ecology mailing lists, and Twitter (103 responses). Locations give subheadings.

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "Pseudoreplication is defined as the use of inferential statistics to test for treatment effects where treatments are not replicated and/or replicates are not statistically independent."
- Key point: Pseudoreplication is the use of inferential statistics to test for treatment effects when treatments are not replicated or replicates are not statistically independent.

### E2

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "The concept of pseudoreplication is being applied too dogmatically and often leads to rejection during review."
- Key point: The authors argue that the concept of pseudoreplication is applied too dogmatically and often leads to rejection in review.

### E3

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "These include carefully defining hypotheses and the population of interest, acknowledging the limits of statistical inference and using statistical approaches including nesting and random effects."
- Key point: Ways ecologists can respond include carefully defining hypotheses and the population of interest, acknowledging the limits of statistical inference, and using statistical approaches such as nesting and random effects.

### E4

- Location: Conclusion
- Quote: "we argue that accusations of pseudoreplication should not be made without appropriate effort to demonstrate the accusation is statistically valid and ecologically meaningful."
- Key point: The authors argue that reviewers should not allege pseudoreplication without first showing that the accusation is statistically valid and ecologically meaningful.

### E5

- Location: Methods and Results: Is it Just us?
- Quote: "There was much interest in the topic, and 103 responses were collected which revealed the following key findings: Fifty‐eight percent of respondents had faced a research question where they felt pseudoreplication was an unavoidable issue"
- Key point: The authors' online survey received 103 responses; 58% of respondents had faced a research question where they felt pseudoreplication was unavoidable.

### E6

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "It is a genuine but controversial issue in ecology particularly in the case of costly landscape‐scale manipulations, behavioral studies where ethics or other concerns may limit sample sizes, ad hoc monitoring data, and the analysis of natural experiments where chance events occur at a single site."
- Key point: Pseudoreplication is a genuine but controversial issue in ecology, especially for costly landscape-scale manipulations, behavioral studies where ethics or other concerns limit sample size, ad hoc monitoring data, and natural experiments where a chance event happens at only one site.

### E7

- Location: Discussion, Question 1
- Quote: "The issue of simple pseudoreplication is very frequent where researchers are trying to understand the effects of unusual individual events such as a flood (Friedman et al. 1996), windstorm (Nagel et al. 2006), wildfire (e.g., Maltby et al. 1990; Johnstone and Kasischke 2005), or volcanic eruption (del Moral and Lacher 2005)."
- Key point: Simple pseudoreplication is very common in studies of single events such as floods, windstorms, wildfires, and volcanic eruptions.

### E8

- Location: Discussion, Question 1
- Quote: "Strictly this is correct as with, for example, a single disturbance and with monitoring plots located on either side of the disturbance's boundary, one cannot statistically separate spatial effects from “treatment” effects."
- Key point: With a single disturbance and monitoring plots on either side of its boundary, strictly speaking you cannot statistically separate spatial effects from "treatment" effects.

### E9

- Location: Discussion, Question 1
- Quote: "More widely, effects may in part be caused by confounding underlying variables such as variation in soil type, soil moisture, aspect, or elevation. We think, however, that the issue of confounded effects should be firmly separated from the issue of pseudoreplication"
- Key point: Differences may also partly come from confounding variables such as soil type, soil moisture, aspect, or elevation; the authors argue the issue of confounding must be kept firmly separate from pseudoreplication.

### E10

- Location: Discussion, Question 1 (the authors' own fencing example)
- Quote: "However, our paper drew a clear line between what we can infer as statisticians and what we can interpret as ecologists."
- Key point: In their paper, the authors drew a clear line between what they could infer statistically and what they could interpret as ecologists.

### E11

- Location: Discussion, Question 1 (the authors' own fencing example)
- Quote: "In our case, we took pains to ensure our grazed and ungrazed plots were close together, located on the same soil type, at the same elevation, and on the same aspect."
- Key point: To reduce confounding, the authors made sure their grazed and ungrazed plots were close together, on the same soil type, at the same elevation, and on the same aspect.

### E12

- Location: Discussion, Question 1
- Quote: "Reviewers should feel at liberty to ask authors to acknowledge the potential issue, to describe any mitigating action they took in their methods, to be cautious in their interpretations, and to phrase conclusions as new hypotheses."
- Key point: Reviewers can ask authors to acknowledge the potential issue, describe in the methods any mitigating steps they took, interpret results cautiously, and phrase conclusions as new hypotheses.
