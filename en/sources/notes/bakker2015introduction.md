# Bakker & van Eerde 2015: An Introduction to Design-Based Research with an Example From Statistics Education

- Source: `bakker2015introduction`, [original PDF (Utrecht University repository)](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/356250), DOI [10.1007/978-94-017-9181-6_16](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-94-017-9181-6_16)
- Version: Chapter 16, pp. 429-466, in Bikner-Ahsbahs et al. (eds.), Approaches to Qualitative Research in Mathematics Education (Springer, 2015); the publisher's PDF is held in the repository of the author's institution, Utrecht University, and was read via the Internet Archive snapshot of 2024-09-11. Locations give section numbers and book page numbers.

### E1

- Location: §16.1.1, p. 429
- Quote: "The purpose of this chapter is to introduce researchers, including Master and PhD students, to design-based research."
- Key point: This chapter introduces design-based research to newcomers, including Master's and PhD students.

### E2

- Location: §16.1.1, p. 430
- Quote: "DBR thus produces both useful products (e.g., educational materials) and accompanying scientific insights into how these products can be used in education"
- Key point: Design-based research produces both useful products (e.g., educational materials) and scientific insights into how these products can be used in education.

### E3

- Location: §16.1.2.1, p. 430
- Quote: "The theoretical yield distinguishes DBR from studies that aim solely at designing educational materials through iterative cycles of testing and improving prototypes."
- Key point: Its theoretical yield is what distinguishes design-based research from development work that only improves educational materials through iterative cycles of testing prototypes.

### E4

- Location: §16.1.2.2, p. 431
- Quote: "DBR typically has an explanatory and advisory aim, namely to give theoretical insights into how particular ways of teaching and learning can be promoted. The type of theory developed can also be of a predictive nature: Under conditions X using educational approach Y, students are likely to learn Z"
- Key point: Design-based research usually aims to explain and advise how particular kinds of teaching and learning can be promoted; the theory can also be predictive, of the form "under conditions X, using approach Y, students are likely to learn Z."

### E5

- Location: §16.1.2.5, p. 434
- Quote: "Hence rather than generalizing from a random sample to a population (statistical generalization), many (mainly qualitative) research approaches aim for generalization to a theory, model or concept (theoretical or analytic generalization)"
- Key point: Many research approaches generalize not from a random sample to a population (statistical generalization) but to a theory, model, or concept (theoretical or analytic generalization).

### E6

- Location: §16.1.2.5, p. 434
- Quote: "Where the use of RCTs can indicate the intervention or treatment being the cause of better learning, DBR cannot claim causality with the same convincing rigor."
- Key point: Randomized controlled trials can show that an intervention caused better learning; design-based research cannot claim causality with the same convincing rigor.

### E7

- Location: §16.1.2.6, p. 435
- Quote: "Furthermore, in DBR design is a crucial part of the research, whereas in action research the focus is on action and change, which can but need not involve the design of a new learning environment. DBR also more explicitly aims for instructional theories than does action research."
- Key point: Compared with action research, design-based research puts design at its core and aims more explicitly at instructional theory; action research focuses on action and change, which need not involve designing a new learning environment.

### E8

- Location: §16.1.2.8, p. 437
- Quote: "Because theories developed in DBR are typically tied to specific learning environments and learning goals, they are humble and hard to generalize."
- Key point: Theories from design-based research are usually tied to specific learning environments and goals, so they are "humble" and hard to generalize.

### E9

- Location: §16.1.2.9, p. 438
- Quote: "The cycles typically consist of the following phases: preparation and design phase, teaching experiment, and retrospective analysis."
- Key point: Each cycle typically has three phases: preparation and design, teaching experiment, and retrospective analysis.

### E10

- Location: §16.1.2.9, p. 438
- Quote: "Yet it must be general enough to be applicable in different contexts such as classrooms in other schools in other countries. In such cases we can speak of transferability."
- Key point: The resulting theory is domain-specific, yet must be general enough to apply in other contexts, such as classrooms in other schools in other countries; this is called transferability.

### E11

- Location: §16.1.3, p. 439
- Quote: "The hypothetical learning trajectory is made up of three components: the learning goal that defines the direction, the learning activities, and the hypothetical learning process--a prediction of how the students' thinking and understanding will evolve in the context of the learning activities."
- Key point: The authors cite Simon's 1995 definition: a hypothetical learning trajectory has three components: the learning goal, the learning activities, and a prediction of how students' thinking and understanding will evolve.

### E12

- Location: §16.1.3.1, p. 439
- Quote: "An elaborated HLT thus includes mathematical learning goals, students' starting points with information on relevant pre-knowledge, mathematical problems and assumptions about students' potential learning processes and about how the teacher could support these processes."
- Key point: An elaborated hypothetical learning trajectory includes learning goals, students' starting points and relevant prior knowledge, the tasks to be used, and assumptions about how students may learn and how the teacher can support that.

### E13

- Location: §16.1.3.2, p. 440
- Quote: "Such adjustments are generally not accepted in comparative experimental research, but in DBR, changes in the HLT are made to create optimal conditions and are regarded as elements of the data corpus. This means that these changes have to be reported well and the information is stronger when changes are supported by theoretical considerations."
- Key point: Adjusting the plan midway through a teaching experiment is generally not accepted in comparative experiments, but in design-based research these changes are part of the data, so they must be reported well, and theoretically grounded changes are more convincing.

### E14

- Location: §16.1.3.3, p. 440
- Quote: "Overall, the idea behind developing an HLT is not to design the perfect instructional sequence, which in our view does not exist, but to provide empirically grounded results that others can adjust to their local circumstances."
- Key point: The aim is not to design the "perfect instructional sequence" (which the authors think does not exist) but to provide empirically grounded results that others can adjust to their local circumstances.

### E15

- Location: §16.1.4.1, p. 441
- Quote: "In the first design phase, it is recommended to collect and invent a set of tasks that could be useful and discuss these with colleagues who are experienced in designing for mathematics education."
- Key point: In the first design phase, collect and invent a set of possibly useful tasks and discuss them with colleagues experienced in design.

### E16

- Location: §16.1.4.2, p. 442
- Quote: "During a teaching experiment, data collection typically includes student work, tests before and after instruction, field notes, audio recordings of whole-class discussions, and video recordings of every lesson and of the final interviews with students and teachers."
- Key point: Data collected during a teaching experiment typically include student work, pre- and post-tests, field notes, audio recordings of whole-class discussions, video of every lesson, and video of the final interviews with students and teachers.

### E17

- Location: §16.1.4.2, p. 442
- Quote: "We further find `mini-interviews' with students, lasting from about twenty seconds to four minutes, very useful provided that they are carried out systematically"
- Key point: The authors find in-class "mini-interviews" with students, lasting 20 seconds to 4 minutes, very useful, provided they are done systematically.

### E18

- Location: §16.1.4.3, p. 442
- Quote: "The left part of the matrix summarizes the HLT and the right part is filled with excerpts from relevant transcripts, clarifying notes from the researcher as well as a quantitative impression of how well the match was between the assumed leaning as formulated in the HLT and the observed learning."
- Key point: Retrospective analysis can use a data analysis matrix: the left side summarizes the hypothetical learning trajectory (how students are expected to respond to each task); the right side holds transcript excerpts, the researcher's notes, and how well the expected learning matched the observed learning.

### E19

- Location: §16.1.4.3, p. 443
- Quote: "With the HLT and research questions as guidelines, conjectures about students' learning and views were generated and documented, and then tested against the other episodes and other data material (student work, field notes, tests). This testing meant looking for confirmation and counter-examples."
- Key point: Guided by the hypothetical learning trajectory and the research questions, generate and document conjectures about students' learning, then test them against other episodes and data (student work, field notes, tests), looking for both confirmation and counter-examples.

### E20

- Location: §16.1.4.3, p. 443
- Quote: "Seemingly crucial episodes were discussed with colleagues to test whether they agreed with our interpretation or perhaps could think of alternative interpretations. This process is called peer examination."
- Key point: Discuss seemingly crucial episodes with colleagues to see whether they agree with your interpretation or can think of alternative interpretations; this is called peer examination.

### E21

- Location: §16.1.5.1, p. 444
- Quote: "The succession of different teaching experiments makes it possible to test the conjectures developed in earlier experiments in later experiments."
- Key point: A succession of teaching experiments lets conjectures from earlier ones be tested in later ones.

### E22

- Location: §16.1.5.1, p. 444
- Quote: "Reports about DBR tend to be long due to the thick descriptions (Geertz 1973) required."
- Key point: Design-based research needs "thick description," so reports tend to be long.

### E23

- Location: §16.1.5.2, p. 444
- Quote: "The challenge is to present the results (instruction theory, HLT, educational activities) in such a way that others can adjust them to their local contingencies."
- Key point: The challenge is to present the results (instruction theory, learning trajectory, activities) so that others can adjust them to their local circumstances.

### E24

- Location: §16.1.5.3, p. 445
- Quote: "Data collection by objective devices such as audio- and video registrations contribute to the internal reliability."
- Key point: Collecting data with objective devices such as audio and video recording contributes to internal reliability (data collection and analysis that do not depend on the individual researcher).

### E25

- Location: §16.1.5.3, p. 445
- Quote: "For measuring interrater reliability, the agreement among independent researchers, it is advised to calculate not only the percentage of agreement but also use Cohen's kappa or another measure that takes into account the probability of agreement by chance"
- Key point: For interrater reliability, calculate not only percentage agreement but also Cohen's kappa or another measure that accounts for chance agreement.

### E26

- Location: §16.1.5.4, p. 445
- Quote: "This means that the reader must be able to track or trace the learning process of the researchers and to reconstruct their study: failures and successes, procedures followed, the conceptual framework used, and the reasons for certain choices must all be reported."
- Key point: Readers must be able to trace the researchers' learning process and reconstruct the study: failures and successes, procedures, the conceptual framework, and the reasons for choices must all be reported.

### E27

- Location: §16.1.2.3, p. 432
- Quote: "The products of DBR are judged on innovativeness and usefulness, not just on the rigor of the research process that is more prominent in evaluating true experiments"
- Key point: Products of design-based research are judged on innovativeness and usefulness, not just on the rigor of the research process, which is more prominent in evaluating true experiments.

### E28

- Location: §16.1.3.2, p. 439
- Quote: "It may happen that the teacher or researcher feels the need to adjust the HLT or instructional activity for the next lesson."
- Key point: During a teaching experiment, the teacher or researcher may feel the need to adjust the learning trajectory or activity for the next lesson.

### E29

- Location: §16.1.2.5, p. 434
- Quote: "A second argument why RCT has its limitations is that a new strategy has to be designed before it can be tested, just like a Boeing airplane cannot be compared with an Airbus without a long tradition of engineering and producing such airplanes. In many cases, considerable research is needed to design innovative approaches."
- Key point: One limitation of randomized controlled trials: a new strategy must be designed before it can be tested, and designing innovative practices often takes considerable research in itself.
