# Flake & Fried 2020: Measurement Schmeasurement: Questionable Measurement Practices and How to Avoid Them

- Source: `flake2020measurement`, [Original PDF (PsyArXiv preprint)](https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/hs7wm/)
- Version: Peer-reviewed preprint, before copyediting; the published version appeared in Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science 3(4):456–465. Locations are given as subheadings.

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "we define questionable measurement practices (QMPs) as decisions researchers make that raise doubts about the validity of the measures, and ultimately the validity of study conclusions."
- Key point: Questionable measurement practices (QMPs): decisions researchers make about measurement that raise doubts about the validity of the measures, and ultimately about the validity of the study's conclusions.

### E2

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "This is difficult because psychological constructs like depression, personality traits, attitudes, or cognitive abilities often do not permit direct observation." / "Neither rigorous research design, advanced statistics, nor large samples can correct such false inferences."
- Key point: Psychological constructs such as depression, personality traits, attitudes, or cognitive abilities often cannot be observed directly. If the measure does not capture what it is supposed to, neither rigorous design, advanced statistics, nor large samples can correct the resulting false inferences.

### E3

- Location: Questioning the Validity of Psychological Science
- Quote: "We can think of no single psychological construct for which there exists exactly one rigorously validated measure that is universally accepted by the field" / "data collected with a 10-item questionnaire can be summarized in one sum-score 1,023 different ways by summing different subsets of items"
- Key point: Hardly any psychological construct has exactly one validated measure that the field universally accepts. Data from a 10-item questionnaire can be summed into one score in 1,023 different ways just by choosing which items to add up, which is itself a "garden of forking paths."

### E4

- Location: Question 6: Do you create a measure on the fly?
- Quote: "Using a measure exactly as described elsewhere, and providing a citation to the original source, decreases ambiguity and the potential for flexibility."
- Key point: Using an existing measure exactly as described elsewhere and citing the original source reduces ambiguity and the room for flexibility.

### E5

- Location: Question 6: Do you create a measure on the fly?
- Quote: "of the 43 item-based scales they reviewed, 34 (79%) appeared to be ad-hoc and were used with no other supporting validity information in the original study."
- Key point: Shaw et al. reviewed the scales used in a large many-labs replication project: 34 of 43 (79%) appeared to be ad hoc, with no other validity evidence in the original study.

### E6

- Location: Table 1
- Quote: "Do you modify the measure? If so, how and why?"
- Key point: One of the transparent-reporting questions the authors list: did you modify the measure? If so, how and why?

### E7

- Location: Question 1: What is your construct?
- Quote: "If the construct is not defined clearly at the outset, the ambiguity in what is being measured will make navigating all the future measurement decisions difficult and present many opportunities to exploit this ambiguity, knowingly or otherwise."
- Key point: If the construct is not defined clearly at the outset, every later measurement decision becomes hard, and the ambiguity offers many chances to exploit it, knowingly or otherwise.

### E8

- Location: Question 2: Why and how do you select your measure?
- Quote: "helps prevent thinking two instruments measure the same construct because they have similar names (i.e., the jingle fallacy), or assuming two measures assess different constructs because they have different names (i.e., the jangle fallacy)"
- Key point: Similar names do not mean two instruments measure the same construct (the jingle fallacy), and different names do not mean they measure different constructs (the jangle fallacy).

### E9

- Location: Question 3: What measure(s) did you use?
- Quote: "Of 433 scales used in studies published in JPSP, 40% had no accompanying information regarding their source, 19% were reported without the number of items, and 9% without the response scale" / "(Flake, et al., 2017)"
- Key point: Flake et al. (2017) reviewed 433 scales in studies published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (JPSP): 40% gave no information about their source, 19% did not report the number of items, and 9% did not report the response scale.

### E10

- Location: Question 4: How do you quantify your measure?
- Quote: "Developing a priori decision rules for removing items or stimuli and/or grouping them is ideal."
- Key point: Ideally, set decision rules for removing items and grouping them in advance.

### E11

- Location: Question 5: Do you modify the scale?
- Quote: "18 measures were the result of combining two separate measures into one on the basis of the reliability coefficient α" / "Flake et al., (2017) noted, in their review of 500 measures used in articles published in JPSP"
- Key point: Of the 500 measures Flake et al. reviewed, 18 were created by combining two separate scales into one on the basis of the reliability coefficient α.

### E12

- Location: Question 5: Do you modify the scale?
- Quote: "the main point is not that such modifications are never called for, but that if they remain undeclared and unjustified, they threaten the validity of the inferences we can draw from a given study."
- Key point: Modifying a scale is not forbidden; the problem is leaving the change undeclared and unjustified.

### E13

- Location: Question 6: Do you create a measure on the fly?
- Quote: "at a minimum, researchers should disclose why they created the measure, and justify why they use an ad-hoc measure over using an existing measure in case such a measure exists. Further, if there is no or little validity evidence for that measure, researchers should discuss it as a limitation of the study."
- Key point: If you build an ad hoc measure, at a minimum say why you created it and why you did not use an existing one; if there is little or no validity evidence for it, discuss that as a limitation.

### E14

- Location: Questioning the Validity of Psychological Science
- Quote: "data collected with a 10-item questionnaire can be summarized in one sum-score 1,023 different ways by summing different subsets of items"
- Key point: Data from a 10-item questionnaire can be summed into one score in 1,023 different ways just by choosing which items to add up.

### E15

- Location: Summary
- Quote: "even if we increase the sample size of our depression trials, adequately power our studies, pre-register our analytic strategies, and stop p-hacking — we can still be left wondering if we were ever measuring depression at all."
- Key point: Even with larger samples, adequate power, preregistration, and no p-hacking, if the measurement is flawed you can still be left wondering whether you were measuring depression at all.

### E16

- Location: Section "Questionable Measurement Practices" (the authors cite the four types of validity in Shadish et al. 2002)
- Quote: "describe four types of validity that contribute to the overall validity of a conclusion: internal validity, external validity, statistical conclusion validity, and construct validity" / "Internal validity captures the aspects of a study’s design that support causal claims about the relations between variables." / "External validity concerns the generalizability of findings across different populations and settings." / "Statistical conclusion validity captures whether conclusions from a statistical analysis are correct." / "construct validity encompasses how the constructs in the study are operationalized"
- Key point: Shadish et al. describe four types of validity that contribute to the overall validity of a conclusion: internal validity (the design features that support causal claims about relations between variables), external validity (whether results generalize across people and settings), statistical conclusion validity (whether conclusions drawn from the statistical analysis are correct), and construct validity (how the concepts in a study are turned into something measurable).
