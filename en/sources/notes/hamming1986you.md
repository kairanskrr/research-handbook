# Hamming 1986: You and Your Research

- Source: `hamming1986you`, [Original](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html)
- Version: Talk at Bellcore on March 7, 1986, transcribed by J. F. Kaiser (the web page has no subheadings, so locations describe the topic in the talk).

### E1

- Location: Main text of the talk, the "important problems" paragraph (after the story of the Bell Labs lunch table)
- Quote: "If you do not work on an important problem, it's unlikely you'll do important work. It's perfectly obvious."
- Key point: If you do not work on an important problem, it is unlikely you will do important work.

### E2

- Location: Same as above
- Quote: "They are not important problems because we do not have an attack. It's not the consequence that makes a problem important, it is that you have a reasonable attack."
- Key point: Problems like time travel or antigravity are not important problems because we have no attack on them; what makes a problem important is not the size of the consequence but having a reasonable attack.

### E3

- Location: Main text of the talk, the paragraph on "ambiguity"
- Quote: "Great scientists tolerate ambiguity very well. They believe the theory enough to go ahead; they doubt it enough to notice the errors and faults so they can step forward and create the new replacement theory."
- Key point: Great scientists believe a theory enough to go ahead and doubt it enough to notice its errors.

### E4

- Location: Same as above
- Quote: "Darwin writes in his autobiography that he found it necessary to write down every piece of evidence which appeared to contradict his beliefs because otherwise they would disappear from his mind."
- Key point: Darwin wrote down every piece of evidence that seemed to contradict his beliefs, because otherwise it would disappear from his mind.

### E5

- Location: Main text of the talk, the paragraph about solving a computer programming problem as a class of problems
- Quote: "You should do your job in such a fashion that others can build on top of it, so they will indeed say, ``Yes, I've stood on so and so's shoulders and I saw further.'' The essence of science is cumulative."
- Key point: Do your work so that others can build on top of it; the essence of science is cumulative.

### E6

- Location: Same as above
- Quote: "Instead of attacking isolated problems, I made the resolution that I would never again solve an isolated problem except as characteristic of a class."
- Key point: Instead of solving an isolated problem, solve it as representative of a class.

### E7

- Location: Main text of the talk, the paragraph on "selling"
- Quote: "I have now come down to a topic which is very distasteful; it is not sufficient to do a job, you have to sell it. [...] But the fact is everyone is busy with their own work."
- Key point: It is not enough to do the work; you also have to explain it and sell it, because everyone else is busy with their own work.

### E8

- Location: Same as above
- Quote: "The average scientist, so far as I can make out, spends almost all his time working on problems which he believes will not be important and he also doesn't believe that they will lead to important problems."
- Key point: The average scientist spends almost all his time on problems he believes are not important and will not lead to important problems.

### E9

- Location: Main text of the talk, the "Great Thoughts Time" paragraph
- Quote: "I finally adopted what I called ``Great Thoughts Time.'' When I went to lunch Friday noon, I would only discuss great thoughts after that."
- Key point: Hamming set aside time after lunch every Friday to think only about great questions (for example, how computers would change science).

### E10

- Location: Main text of the talk, after "Great Thoughts Time"
- Quote: "Most great scientists know many important problems. They have something between 10 and 20 important problems for which they are looking for an attack. And when they see a new idea come up, one hears them say ``Well that bears on this problem.''"
- Key point: Great scientists keep 10 to 20 important problems in mind and are always looking for an attack; when a new idea appears, they recognize at once which problem it bears on.

### E11

- Location: Main text of the talk, the "door open or the door closed" paragraph
- Quote: "He who works with the door open gets all kinds of interruptions, but he also occasionally gets clues as to what the world is and what might be important."
- Key point: Working with the door open brings interruptions but also occasional clues about what might be important; those who work with the door closed are more productive in the short run but may drift off course in the long run.

### E12

- Location: Main text of the talk, between "ambiguity" and "emotional commitment"
- Quote: "Great contributions are rarely done by adding another decimal place."
- Key point: Great contributions are rarely made by adding another decimal place.

### E13

- Location: Main text of the talk, the paragraph about solving a problem as a class of problems
- Quote: "By changing a problem slightly you can often do great work rather than merely good work."
- Key point: Changing a problem slightly can often turn merely good work into great work.

### E14

- Location: The part quoting Bode ("compound interest")
- Quote: "Knowledge and productivity are like compound interest." / "Given two people with exactly the same ability, the one person who manages day in and day out to get in one more hour of thinking will be tremendously more productive over a lifetime."
- Key point: Knowledge and productivity are like compound interest: of two people with the same ability, the one who gets in one more hour of thinking every day will be far more productive over a lifetime.

### E16

- Location: The part on Clogston and Shannon
- Quote: "One of the characteristics of successful scientists is having courage. Once you get your courage up and believe that you can do important problems, then you can. If you think you can't, almost surely you are not going to."
- Key point: Successful scientists have courage: once you believe you can do important problems, you can; if you think you can't, you almost surely won't.

### E17

- Location: Summary
- Quote: "Friday afternoons for years - great thoughts only - means that I committed 10% of my time trying to understand the bigger problems in the field, i.e. what was and what was not important."
- Key point: For years Hamming kept Friday afternoons for "great thoughts," committing 10% of his time to understanding what was and was not important in the field.

### E18

- Location: The part on "door open / door closed"
- Quote: "if you have the door to your office closed, you get more work done today and tomorrow, and you are more productive than most. But 10 years later somehow you don't know quite know what problems are worth working on" / "He who works with the door open gets all kinds of interruptions, but he also occasionally gets clues as to what the world is and what might be important."
- Key point: Working with the door closed gets more done in the short run, but ten years later you may not know which problems are worth working on; working with the door open brings interruptions but also clues about what is important.

### E19

- Location: The part on drive
- Quote: "Just hard work is not enough - it must be applied sensibly."
- Key point: Hard work alone is not enough; it must be applied sensibly.

### E21

- Location: Q&A
- Quote: "Somewhere around every seven years make a significant, if not complete, shift in your field."
- Key point: Make a significant shift within your field roughly every seven years, so you don't run out of ideas.

### E22

- Location: The part on luck
- Quote: "The prepared mind sooner or later finds something important and does it."
- Key point: The prepared mind sooner or later finds something important and does it. Which particular thing is luck; doing something is not.

### E23

- Location: The part on emotional commitment
- Quote: "Most great scientists are completely committed to their problem. Those who don't become committed seldom produce outstanding, first-class work."
- Key point: Most great scientists are completely committed to their problem; those who are not seldom produce first-class work.
