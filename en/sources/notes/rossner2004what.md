# Rossner and Yamada 2004: What's in a picture? The temptation of image manipulation

- Source: `rossner2004what`, [original (PubMed Central)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2172141/)
- Version: Journal of Cell Biology 166(1):11-15, 2004, free full text. The authors were then the journal's managing editor and an editor. Text read from the Europe PMC full-text API, 2026-10-06. Locations give subheadings.

### E1

- Location: introduction
- Quote: "Many such manipulations, however, constitute inappropriate changes to your original data, and making such changes can be classified as scientific misconduct."
- Key point: Many manipulations of digital images are inappropriate changes to original data and can be classified as scientific misconduct.

### E2

- Location: Journal guidelines, The Journal of Cell Biology
- Quote: "No specific feature within an image may be enhanced, obscured, moved, removed, or introduced. The grouping of images from different parts of the same gel, or from different gels, fields, or exposures must be made explicit by the arrangement of the figure (e.g., using dividing lines) and in the text of the figure legend."
- Key point: The Journal of Cell Biology's rule: no specific feature within an image may be enhanced, obscured, moved, removed, or introduced; images grouped from different parts of the same gel, or from different gels, fields, or exposures, must be marked with dividing lines and explained in the legend.

### E3

- Location: Journal guidelines, The Journal of Cell Biology
- Quote: "Adjustments of brightness, contrast, or color balance are acceptable if they are applied to the whole image and as long as they do not obscure or eliminate any information present in the original. Nonlinear adjustments (e.g., changes to gamma settings) must be disclosed in the figure legend."
- Key point: Brightness, contrast, or color balance adjustments are acceptable if applied to the whole image and if they do not obscure or eliminate information in the original; nonlinear adjustments (such as gamma changes) must be disclosed in the legend.

### E4

- Location: Blots and gels, Gross misrepresentation
- Quote: "Deleting a band from a blot, even if you believe it to be an irrelevant background band, is a misrepresentation of your data"
- Key point: Deleting a band from a blot, even one you believe is an irrelevant background band, misrepresents your data.

### E5

- Location: Blots and gels, Cleaning up background
- Quote: "It is very tempting to use the tool variously known as ‘Rubber Stamp’ or ‘Clone Stamp’ in Photoshop to clean up unwanted background in an image (Fig. 4). Don't do it."
- Key point: Do not use image software tools such as "Clone Stamp" to clean up the background.

### E6

- Location: Blots and gels, Splicing lanes together
- Quote: "But it is within usual guidelines to remove a complete lane from a gel and splice the remaining lanes together. This alteration should be clearly indicated, however, by leaving a thin white or black line between the gel pieces that have been juxtaposed." / "it is unacceptable to juxtapose pieces from different gels to compare the levels of proteins or nucleic acids. Rerun all of the samples on the same gel!"
- Key point: Removing a whole lane and splicing the remaining lanes together is generally allowed, but mark the join with a thin white or black line; splicing pieces of different gels to compare protein or nucleic acid levels is not, and all samples should be rerun on one gel.

### E7

- Location: Micrographs, Linear vs. nonlinear adjustments
- Quote: "Some authors mistakenly change the contrast of an experimental compared with a control photo, or change individual panels in a time course, or use different contrast levels when making merged images compared with the original images."
- Key point: Common mistakes: adjusting the contrast of experimental and control photos differently, adjusting single panels in a time course, and using different contrast in merged images than in the originals.

### E8

- Location: Other data-management issues
- Quote: "It is crucially important to keep your original digital or analog data exactly as they were acquired and to record your instrument settings."
- Key point: Keep original image data exactly as acquired, and record instrument settings.

### E9

- Location: Blots and gels, Brightness/contrast adjustments
- Quote: "Think about this when you are doing the experiment and perform multiple exposures to get the bands at the density you want, without having to overadjust digitally the brightness and contrast of the scanned image."
- Key point: Take multiple exposures during the experiment to get bands at the density you want, rather than overadjusting brightness and contrast on the computer afterward.

### E10

- Location: Other data-management issues
- Quote: "Examples include selective acquisition of data by adjusting the settings on your microscope or imager, selecting and reporting a very unusual result as being representative of the data, or hiding negative results that may contradict your conclusions."
- Key point: Other ways of misrepresenting data: acquiring data selectively by adjusting microscope or imager settings, reporting a very unusual result as "representative," and hiding negative results that contradict the conclusions.

### E11

- Location: Conclusion
- Quote: "For every adjustment that you make to a digital image, it is important to ask yourself, ‘Is the image that results from this adjustment still an accurate representation of the original data?’"
- Key point: For every image adjustment, ask yourself: is the adjusted image still an accurate representation of the original data?
