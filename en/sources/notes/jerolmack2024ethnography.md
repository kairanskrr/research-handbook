# Jerolmack, Murphy & Reyes 2024: Ethnography in the open science and digital age (editorial)

- Source: `jerolmack2024ethnography`, [full text on PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10978767/) (Frontiers in Sociology 9:1392012, doi:10.3389/fsoc.2024.1392012)
- Version: Research Topic editorial published March 2024, open access (CC BY). The three authors are US sociologists, introducing a collection of articles on ethnography and open science, digital fieldwork, and field ethics. It is one page long; locations give paragraphs.

### E1

- Location: Para. 1
- Quote: "Academic gatekeepers are increasingly demanding that fieldnotes, interview transcripts, and other data be shared in the name of “open science.” Popular ethnographies have had their scholarly rigor impugned by journalists and the public."
- Key point: Academic gatekeepers increasingly demand that fieldnotes, interview transcripts, and other data be shared in the name of "open science"; journalists and the public have impugned the scholarly rigor of some popular ethnographies.

### E2

- Location: Para. 2
- Quote: "While the “replication crisis” in social science has catalyzed a movement for transparency (e.g., registering hypotheses in advance; sharing data), it is not clear that positivistic standards of verification translate to the interpretive enterprise of ethnography"
- Key point: The "replication crisis" in social science sparked a transparency movement (e.g., registering hypotheses in advance, sharing data), but it is unclear whether positivist standards of verification translate to the interpretive enterprise of ethnography.

### E3

- Location: Para. 2
- Quote: "Some, like Lubet (2018), contend that ethnographers must name sources, fact-check, and perhaps even share raw data to secure readers' trust and facilitate falsifiability"
- Key point: One side (e.g., Lubet 2018) argues that ethnographers must name sources, fact-check, and perhaps even share raw data to earn readers' trust and make conclusions falsifiable.

### E4

- Location: Para. 2
- Quote: "some (e.g., Reyes, 2018; Stuart, 2020) warn that demands for open science may further marginalize scholars who study vulnerable populations (where data transparency is dangerous) or who lack the resources that facilitate data transparency (e.g., hiring fact checkers)."
- Key point: The other side (e.g., Reyes 2018, Stuart 2020) warns that open science demands may further marginalize scholars who study vulnerable populations (for whom data transparency is dangerous) and those who lack the resources for it (e.g., hiring fact-checkers).

### E5

- Location: Para. 2
- Quote: "Between these poles, others have floated flexible “standards for transparency that are consistent” with ethnographers' “commitment to their subjects and interpretive scholarship” (Murphy et al., 2021, p. 41)—e.g., partial disclosure of people or places, sharing the coding scheme, or online appendices with supplementary data"
- Key point: Between these poles, some propose flexible transparency standards consistent with ethnographers' commitments to their subjects and to interpretive scholarship, e.g., partial disclosure of people or places, sharing coding schemes, and supplementary data in online appendices.

### E6

- Location: Para. 3
- Quote: "As social life is increasingly lived online, it becomes unclear where the boundaries of the “field site” should be drawn and whether ethnographic conventions—methodological and ethical—are directly transferrable to the study of digital spaces"
- Key point: As social life moves online, it becomes unclear where to draw the boundaries of the "field site" and whether ethnography's methodological and ethical conventions transfer directly to digital spaces.

### E7

- Location: Para. 5
- Quote: "There can be no “one size fits all” answer to these developments and debates."
- Key point: There is no "one size fits all" answer to these debates.

### E8

- Location: Para. 6
- Quote: "Khan et al. contend that the college students whose sexual practices they studied would be less likely to disclose personal details, and that so much information would have to be masked to maintain confidentiality that the remaining data would be meaningless."
- Key point: In the collection, Khan et al. argue that if data had to be made public, the college students whose sexual practices they studied would be less willing to disclose personal details, and so much would have to be masked for confidentiality that the remaining data would be meaningless.
