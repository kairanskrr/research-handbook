# Epstein & King 2002: The Rules of Inference

- Source: `epstein2002rules`, [original PDF (Gary King's homepage)](https://gking.harvard.edu/files/gking/files/rules.pdf)
- Version: University of Chicago Law Review 69(1) (Winter 2002), pp. 1–133. The authors are a US political scientist and legal scholar (Epstein) and a political scientist (King). The article is about empirical research in **American law reviews**. Locations give the Part and section of the article.

### E1

- Location: abstract
- Quote: "law professors have in fact been conducting research that is empirical--that is, learning about the world using quantitative data or qualitative information--for almost as long as they have been conducting research."
- Key point: The authors define "empirical" as learning about the world using quantitative data or qualitative information; by this definition, law professors have long been doing empirical research.

### E2

- Location: Part I (The Extent of Methodological Problems in Empirical Research)
- Quote: "We thus began by casting the net very widely, reading all 231 articles published in all American law reviews between 1990 and 2000"
- Key point: The authors' survey began with all 231 articles in American law reviews from 1990 to 2000 with "empirical" in the title (they also read the empirical articles in six top law reviews from 1995 to 2000, among others).

### E3

- Location: Part I
- Quote: "every one we have read thus far--every single one--violates at least one of the rules we discuss in the balance of this Article."
- Key point: Every single article the authors read violates at least one of the rules of inference discussed in the article.

### E4

- Location: introduction
- Quote: "While a Ph.D. is taught to subject his or her favored hypothesis to every conceivable test and data source, seeking out all possible evidence against his or her theory, an attorney is taught to amass all the evidence for his or her hypothesis and distract attention from anything that might be seen as contradictory information."
- Key point: PhD training teaches you to subject your favored hypothesis to every conceivable test; legal training teaches you to gather only evidence for your side and deflect attention from contrary information.

### E5

- Location: Part II.B (General Guidelines)
- Quote: "Good empirical work adheres to the replication standard: another researcher should be able to understand, evaluate, build on, and reproduce the research without any additional information from the author."
- Key point: Good empirical work adheres to the replication standard: another researcher should be able to understand, evaluate, build on, and reproduce the research without asking the author for additional information.

### E6

- Location: Part VIII (Selecting Observations)
- Quote: "(1) identify the population of interest; (2) collect as much data as is feasible; (3) record the process by which data come to be observed; and (4) collect data in a manner that avoids selection bias."
- Key point: Four rules for selecting observations: identify the population of interest; collect as much data as is feasible; record the process by which data come to be observed; and avoid selection bias.

### E7

- Location: Part VIII.D
- Quote: "If this is so, then any inferences Kerr reaches solely on the basis of published opinions will be biased, and that bias is in a predictable direction: he will overestimate the effect of Chevron."
- Key point: If judges publish opinions selectively, conclusions drawn only from published opinions will be biased (in the example, they overstate the influence of a particular doctrine).

### E8

- Location: Part II.B
- Quote: "The point is not to qualify every statement--for example, by changing "I am certain" to "I am fairly certain"--but rather to estimate the degree of uncertainty inherent in each conclusion and to report this estimate along with every conclusion."
- Key point: The point is not to qualify every statement with "fairly certain" but to estimate the uncertainty of each conclusion and report it alongside the conclusion.

### E9

- Location: Part II.B
- Quote: "one useful course of action is to find the weakest link in the chain of reasoning--the part of the argument that rests on the least empirical evidence or that is most vulnerable to attack."
- Key point: When uncertainty is hard to quantify in qualitative work, find the weakest link in the chain of reasoning: the part resting on the least evidence or most open to attack.

### E10

- Location: Part II.B
- Quote: "A third sign is the legal community's refusal to subject articles submitted to some of its most prestigious outlets to any form of blind peer review, preferring instead to leave the refereeing task to law students."
- Key point: The authors criticize some of the most prestigious American legal outlets for using no form of blind peer review and letting law students decide what to publish.

### E11

- Location: Part II.A (The Goals)
- Quote: "But possible is different than conclusive, especially since Sklansky provides no argument or evidence about this."
- Key point: Commenting on a study that picked only 11 "prominent" cases, the authors say the conclusion may hold, but possible is different from conclusive, since the author gives no argument about whether the selection affects the result.

### E12

- Location: Part IV.A (Contribute to a Scholarly Literature)
- Quote: "making connections with what has come before helps scholars avoid mistakes, skip arduous reinventions of existing ideas, and find additional observable implications of their theories."
- Key point: Connecting with earlier work helps you avoid mistakes and avoid reinventing existing ideas.

### E13

- Location: Part II.B
- Quote: "Expect everything you do to be scrutinized, any rival explanation you have not explored to be tested, and every possible way you could be proven wrong to be dissected."
- Key point: Expect every step to be scrutinized after publication and every rival explanation you have not explored to be tested.

### E14

- Location: Part VII.A (Measurement)
- Quote: "As a rule, then, human judgment should be removed as much as possible from measurement or, when judgment is necessary, the rules underlying the judgments should be clarified enough to make them wholly transparent to other researchers."
- Key point: Remove human judgment from measurement as much as possible; when judgment is necessary, state the rules behind it so clearly that other researchers can fully follow them.

### E15

- Location: introduction
- Quote: "a large fraction of legal scholarship makes at least some claims about the world based on observation or experience."
- Key point: A large fraction of legal scholarship makes at least some claims about the world based on observation or experience.

### E16

- Location: Part IX.E (Develop Standards for Data Archiving)
- Quote: "Investigators coding cases would deposit their data sets, complete coding rules, and the precise connections between their numerical data and the original cases from which they were coded."
- Key point: Researchers who code cases should deposit their data sets, complete coding rules, and the precise links between their numerical data and the original cases.

### E17

- Location: Part VII.A
- Quote: "asking someone to identify his or her motive is one of the worst methods of measuring motive."
- Key point: Asking people why they did something is one of the worst ways to measure motive (the authors' example is asking justices whether political preferences affect their votes).

### E18

- Location: Part VII.A
- Quote: "So, instead of (or sometimes in addition to) asking respondents to answer research questions directly, it is usually better to look for revealed preferences, which are consequences of theories of motive that are directly observable in real behavior."
- Key point: Instead of (or in addition to) asking respondents directly, it is usually better to look for revealed preferences that can be observed directly in real behavior.

### E19

- Location: Part IX.D (Move to an Alternative Model of Scholarly Journal Management)
- Quote: "In the law world, students run and edit their school's flagship journal (for example, The University of Chicago Law Review, the Yale Law Journal), though they often consult informally with faculty before making decisions about particular articles."
- Key point: In American law, each school's flagship journal is run and edited by students, who often consult faculty informally before deciding.

### E20

- Location: Part VII.A
- Quote: "the justices would say no, that they vote on the basis of some neutral principle(s)."
- Key point: If you asked US Supreme Court justices whether political preferences affect their votes, they would say no, that they vote on the basis of neutral principles.
