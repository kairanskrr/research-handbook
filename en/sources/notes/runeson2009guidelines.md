# Runeson & Höst 2009: Guidelines for conducting and reporting case study research in software engineering

- Source: `runeson2009guidelines`, [original](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10664-008-9102-8) (open access)
- Version: Empirical Software Engineering 14(2):131-164, local PDF `papers/pdf/runeson2009guidelines.pdf`. Page numbers are journal page numbers.
- Note: We use it here only for its classification and definitions of validity. The authors note that this classification is close to the one commonly used for controlled experiments in software engineering.

### E1

- Location: §5.2.3 Validity, p. 153
- Quote: "The validity of a study denotes the trustworthiness of the results, to what extent the results are true and not biased by the researchers’ subjective point of view."
- Key point: Validity is the trustworthiness of the results: to what extent they are true and not biased by the researchers' subjective view.

### E2

- Location: §5.2.3, p. 153
- Quote: "It is, of course, too late to consider the validity during the analysis. The validity must be addressed during all previous phases of the case study."
- Key point: It is too late to think about validity at the analysis stage; address it in every earlier phase.

### E3

- Location: §5.2.3, Construct validity
- Quote: "This aspect of validity reflect to what extent the operational measures that are studied really represent what the researcher have in mind and what is investigated according to the research questions."
- Key point: Construct validity: whether what you actually measure really represents the concept you intend to study.

### E4

- Location: §5.2.3, Internal validity
- Quote: "When the researcher is investigating whether one factor affects an investigated factor there is a risk that the investigated factor is also affected by a third factor."
- Key point: Internal validity: when studying whether A affects B, B may also be affected by a third factor; failing to detect or control that factor threatens internal validity.

### E5

- Location: §5.2.3, External validity
- Quote: "This aspect of validity is concerned with to what extent it is possible to generalize the findings, and to what extent the findings are of interest to other people outside the investigated case."
- Key point: External validity: to what extent the findings can be generalized beyond the case studied.

### E6

- Location: §4.2 Interviews
- Quote: "the interview questions are based on the formulated research questions (but they are of course not formulated in the same way)."
- Key point: Interview questions are derived from the research questions but are not worded the same way.

### E7

- Location: §4.2 Interviews
- Quote: "In a semi-structured interview, questions are planned, but they are not necessarily asked in the same order as they are listed." / "Additionally, semi-structured interviews allow for improvisation and exploration of the studied objects."
- Key point: In a semi-structured interview the questions are planned but not necessarily asked in order; it allows improvisation and exploration.

### E8

- Location: §4.2 Interviews
- Quote: "Questions can be open, i.e. allowing and inviting a broad range of answers and issues from the interviewed subject, or closed offering a limited set of alternative answers."
- Key point: Questions can be open (inviting a broad range of answers) or closed (offering a limited set of alternatives).

### E9

- Location: §4.2 Interviews
- Quote: "First the researcher presents the objectives of the interview and the case study, and explains how the data from the interview will be used. Then a set of introductory questions are asked about the background etc. of the subject, which are relatively simple to answer."
- Key point: Start an interview by presenting its objectives and how the data will be used, then ask easy introductory questions about background.

### E10

- Location: §4.2 Interviews
- Quote: "It is not recommended to start the interview with these questions or to introduce them before a climate of trust has been obtained. It is recommended that the major findings are summarized by the researcher towards the end of the interview, in order to get feedback and avoid misunderstandings."
- Key point: Do not open with sensitive questions; wait until trust is established. Toward the end, the researcher should summarize the main findings and ask for feedback to avoid misunderstandings.

### E11

- Location: §4.2 Interviews
- Quote: "The funnel model begins with open questions and moves towards more specific ones."
- Key point: A funnel interview starts with open questions and moves toward more specific ones.

### E12

- Location: §4.2 Interviews
- Quote: "During the interview sessions it is recommended to record the discussion in a suitable audio or video format. Even if notes are taken, it is in many cases hard to record all details, and it is impossible to know what is important to record during the interview."
- Key point: Recording audio or video is recommended; notes alone rarely capture every detail, and during the interview you cannot know what will turn out to be important.

### E13

- Location: §4.2 Interviews
- Quote: "This is a time consuming task, but in many cases new insights are made during the transcription, and it is therefore not recommended that this task is conducted by anyone else than the researcher. In some cases it may be advantageous to have the transcripts reviewed by the interview subject."
- Key point: Transcription is time consuming, but new insights often arise during it, so researchers should transcribe themselves; sometimes interviewees can be asked to review the transcript.

### E14

- Location: §4.2 Interviews
- Quote: "it is recommended to select subjects based on" [...] "differences instead of trying to replicate similarities" / "The number of interviewees has to be decided during the study. One criterion for when sufficient interviews are conducted is “saturation”, i.e. when no new information or viewpoint is gained from new subjects"
- Key point: Select interviewees for their differences, not to replicate similarities; decide the number of interviews during the study, with "saturation" as one criterion: new interviewees bring no new information or viewpoints.

### E15

- Location: §5.2 Qualitative Data Analysis
- Quote: "Analysis must be carried out in parallel with the data collection since the approach is flexible and that new insights are found during the analysis."
- Key point: Qualitative analysis must run in parallel with data collection, because analysis produces new insights that call for new data.

### E16

- Location: §5.2 Qualitative Data Analysis
- Quote: "In order to reduce bias by individual researchers, the analysis benefits from being conducted by multiple researchers."
- Key point: Analysis by several researchers reduces individual bias.

### E17

- Location: §5.2.1 General Techniques for Analysis
- Quote: "The data can, for example be organized in a table where the rows represent codes of interest and the columns represent interview subjects."
- Key point: Coded data can be organized in a table with codes as rows and interviewees as columns.

### E18

- Location: §3.3 Ethical considerations
- Quote: "Participants must not necessarily agree in the outcome of the analysis, but feeding back the analysis results increases the validity of the study."
- Key point: Participants need not agree with the analysis, but feeding the results back to them increases the study's validity.

### E19

- Location: §1 Introduction, p. 132
- Quote: "The term “case study” appears every now and then in the title of software engineering research papers. However, the presented studies range from very ambitious and well organized studies in the field, to small toy examples that claim to be case studies."
- Key point: Papers with "case study" in the title range from well-organized field studies to small toy examples that call themselves case studies.

### E20

- Location: §2.1, p. 134
- Quote: "More strictly, a case study is purely observational while action research is focused on and involved in the change process."
- Key point: Strictly, a case study only observes, while action research takes part in and drives change.

### E21

- Location: §2.1, p. 134
- Quote: "We prefer to consider ethnographic studies as a specialized type of case studies with focus on cultural practices (Easterbrook et al. 2008) or long duration studies with large amounts of participant-observer data (Klein and Myers 1999)."
- Key point: The authors treat ethnography as a specialized type of case study: one focused on cultural practices, or of long duration with large amounts of participant-observation data.

### E22

- Location: §2.2, p. 136
- Quote: "Case studies are by definition conducted in real world settings, and thus have a high degree of realism, mostly at the expense of the level of control."
- Key point: Case studies take place in real-world settings, so they have high realism, usually at the expense of control.

### E23

- Location: §2.2, p. 137
- Quote: "Hence, a case study will never provide conclusions with statistical significance. On the contrary, many different kinds of evidence, figures, statements, documents, are linked together to support a strong and relevant conclusion."
- Key point: A case study never gives statistically significant conclusions; instead it links many kinds of evidence (figures, statements, documents) to support its conclusions.

### E24

- Location: §2.2, p. 137
- Quote: "its conclusions are based on a clear chain of evidence, whether qualitative or quantitative, collected from multiple sources in a planned and consistent manner"
- Key point: A key feature of case studies: conclusions rest on a clear chain of evidence collected from multiple sources in a planned and consistent way.

### E25

- Location: §2.4, p. 138
- Quote: "However, there is a limit to the flexibility; the case study should have specific objectives set out from the beginning. If the objectives change, it is a new case study rather than a change to the existing one"
- Key point: Case study design is flexible, but the objectives should be set from the start; if they change, it is a new case study.

### E26

- Location: §2.5 Definitions, p. 138
- Quote: "The case is referred to as the object of the study (e.g. a project), and it contains one or more units of analysis (e.g. subprojects)."
- Key point: The case is the object of study (e.g., a project), and it contains one or more units of analysis (e.g., subprojects).

### E27

- Location: §3.1, p. 139
- Quote: "Objective—what to achieve? & The case—what is studied? & Theory—frame of reference & Research questions—what to know? & Methods—how to collect data? & Selection strategy—where to seek data?"
- Key point: A case study plan contains at least: objective, case, theory (frame of reference), research questions, data collection methods, and selection strategy.

### E28

- Location: §3.1, p. 139
- Quote: "Studies on “toy programs” or similarly are of course excluded due to its lack of real-life context. Yin (2003) distinguishes between holistic case studies, where the case is studied as a whole, and embedded case studies where multiple units of analysis are studied within a case"
- Key point: Studies on toy programs are not case studies, since they lack a real-life context; a holistic case study treats the case as a whole, while an embedded case study examines several units of analysis within one case.

### E29

- Location: §3.1, p. 140
- Quote: "In case studies, the case and the units of analysis should be selected intentionally. This is in contrast to surveys and experiments, where subjects are sampled from a population to which the results are intended to be generalized."
- Key point: Cases and units of analysis should be selected intentionally, not sampled from a population as in surveys and experiments.

### E30

- Location: §3.1, p. 140
- Quote: "The purpose of the selection may be to study a case that is expected to be “typical”, “critical”, “revelatory” or “unique” in some respect [...] However, in practice, many cases are selected based on availability"
- Key point: Cases can be chosen as "typical," "critical," "revelatory," or "unique"; in practice, many are chosen based on availability.

### E31

- Location: §3.2 Case Study Protocol, p. 141
- Quote: "The case study protocol is a container for the design decisions on the case study as well as field procedures for its carrying through. The protocol is a continuously changed document that is updated when the plans for the case study are changed."
- Key point: The case study protocol records design decisions and field procedures, and is a continuously updated document that changes as plans change.

### E32

- Location: §3.2, p. 141
- Quote: "Firstly, it serves as a guide when conducting the data collection, and in that way prevents the researcher from missing to collect data that were planned to be collected. [...] Thirdly, other researchers and relevant people may review it in order to give feedback on the plans. [...] Finally, it can serve as a log or diary where all conducted data collection and analysis is recorded together with change decisions based on the flexible nature of the research. [...] In order to keep track of changes during the research project, the protocol should be kept under some form of version control."
- Key point: The protocol guides data collection and prevents missing planned data; other researchers and relevant people can review it and give feedback; it can serve as a log of all data collection, analysis, and change decisions; and it should be version-controlled to track changes.

### E33

- Location: §3.2, p. 141
- Quote: "Case study protocols cannot be published in extenso since they contain confidential information. However, parts of the protocol can be published, such as interview instruments"
- Key point: The protocol contains confidential information and cannot be published in full, but parts such as interview instruments can be published.

### E34

- Location: §3.3 Ethical Considerations, p. 142
- Quote: "In software engineering, case studies often include dealing with confidential information in an organization. If it is not clear from the beginning how this kind of information is handled and who is responsible for accepting what information to publish, there may be problems later on."
- Key point: Case studies often involve an organization's confidential information; if it is not clear from the start how it is handled and who approves what is published, problems follow.

### E35

- Location: §3.3, p. 142
- Quote: "It may be tempting for the researcher to collect data e.g. through indirect or independent data collection methods, without asking for consent. However, the ethical standards must be maintained for the long term trust in software engineering research."
- Key point: With indirect data collection it may be tempting to skip consent, but ethical standards must be kept for long-term trust.

### E36

- Location: §3.3, p. 143
- Quote: "Issues on confidentiality and publication should also be regulated in a contract between the researcher and the studied organization. However, not only can information be sensitive when leaking outside a company. Data collected from and opinions stated by individual employees may be sensitive if presented e.g. to their managers"
- Key point: Confidentiality and publication should be regulated in a contract between researcher and organization; individual employees' data and opinions can also be sensitive if shown to their managers.

### E37

- Location: §3.3, p. 143
- Quote: "Companies may not know academic practices for publication and dissemination, and must hence be explicitly informed about those."
- Key point: Companies may not know academic publication practices and must be informed of them explicitly.

### E38

- Location: §3.3, p. 143
- Quote: "However, it is important to remember that it is not always sufficient to remove names of companies or individuals. They may be identified by their characteristics if they are selected from a small set of people or companies."
- Key point: Removing company and personal names is not always enough; subjects from a small group may be identifiable by their characteristics.

### E39

- Location: §3.3, p. 143
- Quote: "Results may be sensitive to a company, e.g. by revealing deficiencies in their software engineering practices, or if their product comes out last in a comparison (Amschler Andrews and Pradhan 2001). The chance that this may occur must be discussed upfront and made clear to the participants of the case study."
- Key point: Results may be unfavorable to a company (e.g., revealing deficiencies in its practices); discuss this possibility and make it clear in advance.

### E40

- Location: §3.3, p. 143
- Quote: "It is preferable to make the inducements explicit, i.e. specify what the incentives are for the participants. Thereby the inducement’s role in threatening the validity of the study may also be analyzed."
- Key point: Make the inducements for participants explicit, so their effect on validity can also be analyzed.

### E41

- Location: §3.3, p. 143
- Quote: "Firstly, transcript of interviews and observations should be sent back to the participants to enable correction of raw data. Secondly, analyses should be presented to them in order to maintain their trust in the research."
- Key point: Send interview and observation transcripts back to participants for correction, and present the analysis to them, to maintain trust.

### E42

- Location: §3.3, pp. 143-144 (the authors' examples from three of their own studies)
- Quote: "These agreements state that the university researchers are obliged to have publications approved by representatives of the companies before they are published, and that raw data must not be spread to any but those signing the contract. [...] we were explicitly told that raw numbers of defects could not be published, but percentages over phases could, which was acceptable for the research purposes."
- Key point: The authors' example: confidentiality agreements require publications to be approved by company representatives first and forbid spreading raw data; one company would not allow raw defect counts to be published but did allow percentages per phase, which was enough for the study's purpose.

### E43

- Location: §4.1, p. 144
- Quote: "It is important to use several data sources in a case study in order to limit the effects of one interpretation of one single data source. If the same conclusion can be drawn from several sources of information, i.e. triangulation (Section 2.2), this conclusion is stronger than a conclusion based a single source."
- Key point: Use several data sources to limit the effect of a single interpretation of a single source; conclusions drawn from several sources (triangulation) are stronger.

### E44

- Location: §2.2, p. 136
- Quote: "Data (source) triangulation—using more than one data source or collecting the same data at different occasions. [...] Observer triangulation—using more than one observer in the study. [...] Theory triangulation—using alternative theories or viewpoints."
- Key point: Forms of triangulation: data source, observer, methodological, and theory (perspective).

### E45

- Location: §4.1, p. 145
- Quote: "Third degree: Independent analysis of work artifacts where already available and sometimes compiled data is used. [...] For third degree methods it should also be noticed that the data has been collected and recorded for another purpose than that of the research study"
- Key point: Third-degree methods analyze existing work artifacts (documents, defect reports, etc.); note that these data were recorded for some other purpose.

### E46

- Location: §4.2 Interviews, pp. 146–147
- Quote: "Semi-structured interviews are common in case studies. [...] One criterion for when sufficient interviews are conducted is “saturation”, i.e. when no new information or viewpoint is gained from new subjects"
- Key point: Semi-structured interviews are common in case studies; "saturation" (new interviewees bring no new information or viewpoints) is one criterion for whether you have done enough interviews.

### E47

- Location: §4.2, p. 147
- Quote: "This means that it is good to try to involve different roles, personalities, etc in the interview."
- Key point: Interviewees should cover different roles and personalities.

### E48

- Location: §4.3 Observations, p. 148
- Quote: "Further, it is particularly relevant to use observations, where it is suspected that there is a deviation between an “official” view of matters and the “real” case"
- Key point: Observation is especially relevant when you suspect a gap between the "official" view and what "really" happens.

### E49

- Location: §4.3, p. 148
- Quote: "The observer spent 1 week with an XP team, taking part in everyday activities, including pair programming, i.e. an approach like Case 1 above. Data collected consisted of field notes, audio recordings of meetings and discussions, photographs and copies of artifacts."
- Key point: Sharp & Robinson's example: the observer spent a week with an Extreme Programming (XP) team, taking part in daily activities; data included field notes, meeting recordings, photos, and copies of work artifacts.

### E50

- Location: §5.1, pp. 150–151
- Quote: "The basic objective of the analysis is to derive conclusions from the data, keeping a clear chain of evidence. The chain of evidence means [...] that a reader should be able to follow the derivation of results and conclusions from the collected data"
- Key point: A chain of evidence means a reader can follow the derivation from the collected data to the results and conclusions.

### E51

- Location: §5.2, p. 151
- Quote: "Negative case analysis tries to find alternative explanations that reject the hypotheses."
- Key point: Negative case analysis: actively look for alternative explanations that would reject the hypotheses.

### E52

- Location: §5.2, p. 151
- Quote: "First the data is coded, which means that parts of the text can be given a code representing a certain theme, area, construct, etc."
- Key point: The first step of qualitative analysis is coding: labeling text segments with codes that represent a theme or concept.

### E53

- Location: §5.2, p. 152 (the authors' XP study example)
- Quote: "Each statement in the transcribed interviews was given a unique identification, and classified by two researchers."
- Key point: Example: each statement in the transcripts got a unique identifier and was classified by two researchers.

### E54

- Location: §5.2, p. 153
- Quote: "This means, for example, in all cases that a pre-planned approach for analysis must be applied, all decisions taken by the researcher must be recorded, all versions of instrumentation must be kept, links between data, codes, and memos must be explicitly recorded in documentation, etc."
- Key point: Qualitative analysis needs structure: plan the analysis approach in advance, record every decision, keep all versions of instruments such as interview guides, and record the links between data, codes, and memos explicitly.

### E55

- Location: §5.2, p. 153
- Quote: "To our experience editing approaches and template approaches are most suitable in software engineering case studies. It is hard to present and obtain a clear chain of evidence in informal immersion approaches."
- Key point: In the authors' experience, editing approaches (codes emerge during analysis) and template approaches (more codes set in advance) suit software engineering case studies best; informal immersion approaches make a clear chain of evidence hard to present.

### E56

- Location: §5.2.3, p. 154
- Quote: "There is no population from which a statistically representative sample has been drawn. However, for case studies, the intention is to enable analytical generalization where the results are extended to cases which have common characteristics and hence for which the findings are relevant, i.e. defining a theory."
- Key point: A case study has no statistically representative sample; it aims at analytical generalization, extending results to other cases with common characteristics, that is, building theory.

### E57

- Location: §5.2.3, p. 154
- Quote: "Internal validity: This aspect of validity is of concern when causal relations are examined."
- Key point: Internal validity is a concern only when causal relations are examined.

### E58

- Location: §5.2.3, p. 154
- Quote: "Reliability: This aspect is concerned with to what extent the data and the analysis are dependent on the specific researchers."
- Key point: Reliability: to what extent the data and analysis depend on the specific researchers doing them.

### E59

- Location: §5.2.3, p. 154
- Quote: "Examples of ways to improve validity are triangulation, developing and maintaining a detailed case study protocol, having designs, protocols, etc. reviewed by peer researchers, having collected data and obtained results reviewed by case subjects, spending sufficient time with the case, and giving sufficient concern to analysis of “negative cases”, i.e. looking for theories that contradict your findings."
- Key point: Ways to improve validity: triangulation, maintaining a detailed protocol, having peers review the design, having subjects review the data and results, spending enough time with the case, and analyzing negative cases carefully.

### E60

- Location: §6.1, p. 155
- Quote: "Describing the case might be more sensitive, since this might enable identification of the case or its subjects. For example, “a large telecommunications company in Sweden” is most probably a branch of the Ericsson Corporation."
- Key point: Describing the case may reveal identities; for example, "a large telecommunications company in Sweden" is almost certainly Ericsson.

### E61

- Location: §6.1, p. 155
- Quote: "Internal characteristics, like size of the studied unit, average age of the personnel, etc may be more interesting than external characteristics like domain and turnover."
- Key point: When describing the case, internal characteristics (size of the unit, average age of staff, etc.) may be more useful, and less identifying, than external ones (domain, turnover).

### E62

- Location: §6.1, p. 155
- Quote: "If they are changed substantially over the course of the study, this should be reported to help understanding the case."
- Key point: If the research objectives and questions change substantially during the study, say so in the report.

### E63

- Location: §6.1, p. 156
- Quote: "Providing a “history of the inquiry” requires a level of substantially more detail than pure reporting of used methodologies, e.g. “we launched a case study using semi-structured interviews”."
- Key point: The report should give a "history of the inquiry": who did what and when, not just "we ran a case study using semi-structured interviews."

### E64

- Location: §6.1, p. 156
- Quote: "However, to establish trust in the study, the reader needs relevant snapshots from the data that support the conclusions. These snapshots may be in the form of e.g. citations (typical or special statements), pictures, or narratives with anonymized subjects."
- Key point: To earn the reader's trust, show snapshots of the data that support the conclusions, such as quotations, pictures, or anonymized narratives.

### E65

- Location: §5.2.3, p. 153
- Quote: "Some researchers have argued for having a different classification scheme for flexible design studies (credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability), while we prefer to operationalize this scheme for flexible design studies, instead of changing the terms (Robson 2002)."
- Key point: Some argue that flexible design studies need a different validity scheme (credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability); the authors prefer to keep the established terms and operationalize them for flexible design studies.
