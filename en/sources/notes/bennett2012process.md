# Bennett & Checkel 2012: Process Tracing: From Philosophical Roots to Best Practices

It notes that it is a pre-publication version of Chapter 1 of Bennett & Checkel (eds.), Process Tracing in the Social Sciences: From Metaphor to Analytic Tool (Cambridge University Press, 2015). Locations give working paper page numbers.

### E1

- Location: I. Introduction, p. 5
- Quote: "Contemporary political scientists and sociologists have converged on the view that these puzzles, and many more on the current scholarly and policy agendas, demand answers that combine social and institutional structure and context with individual agency and decisionmaking."
- Key point: Political scientists and sociologists agree that many puzzles demand answers combining social and institutional structure and context with individual agency and decision-making.

### E2

- Location: II. Defining Process Tracing, p. 7
- Quote: "The term process tracing originated in the field of cognitive psychology in the United States in the late 1960s or early 1970s." / "In 1979, the Stanford University political scientist Alexander L. George appropriated the term process tracing to describe the use of evidence from within case studies to make inferences about historical explanations"
- Key point: The term process tracing originated in US cognitive psychology in the late 1960s or early 1970s; in 1979 the political scientist Alexander George borrowed it for using within-case evidence to infer historical explanations.

### E3

- Location: II. Defining Process Tracing, p. 10
- Quote: "define process tracing as the analysis of evidence on processes, sequences, and conjunctures of events within a case for the purposes of either developing or testing hypotheses about causal mechanisms that might causally explain the case."
- Key point: Process tracing is the analysis of evidence on processes, sequences, and conjunctures of events within a case, to develop or test hypotheses about causal mechanisms that might explain the case.

### E4

- Location: II. Defining Process Tracing, p. 10
- Quote: "process tracing examines the deductive observable implications of hypothesized causal mechanisms within a case to test whether these might in fact explain the case" / "Or, it inductively uses evidence from within a case to develop hypotheses that might explain the case"
- Key point: Process tracing can be deductive (testing the observable implications of hypothesized mechanisms within a case) or inductive (developing hypotheses from within-case evidence).

### E5

- Location: II. Defining Process Tracing, p. 11
- Quote: "Data set observations are "an array of scores on specific variables for a designated sample of cases," and these observations provide the basis for statistical analyses. Causal process observations are "observations on context, process, or mechanism" and are used in within-case analyses such as process tracing"
- Key point: Data set observations are scores on variables for a sample of cases, used in statistical analysis; causal process observations are observations on context, process, or mechanism, used in within-case analyses such as process tracing.

### E6

- Location: II. Defining Process Tracing, p. 11
- Quote: "Process tracing is closely related to historical explanation, as that term is used by the historian Clayton Roberts."
- Key point: Process tracing is closely related to "historical explanation" as the historian Clayton Roberts uses the term.

### E7

- Location: II. Defining Process Tracing, p. 11
- Quote: "We emphasize instead the importance of making explicit the hypotheses about underlying causal mechanisms that are theorized to have caused an historical outcome so that these can be rigorously assessed, even at the expense of political science narratives that are more clogged"
- Key point: Unlike historians, the authors stress making hypotheses about causal mechanisms explicit so they can be rigorously assessed, even at the cost of more cluttered narratives.

### E8

- Location: II. Defining Process Tracing, p. 12
- Quote: "It is not simply glorified historiography, nor does it proceed by the logic of frequentist statistics."
- Key point: Process tracing is not just glorified historiography, nor does it follow the logic of frequentist statistics.

### E9

- Location: III. Bayesianism and Process Tracing, p. 19
- Quote: "each approach emphasizes that the probative value of evidence relative to competing explanations is more important than the number of pieces of evidence."
- Key point: The probative value of evidence relative to competing explanations matters more than the number of pieces of evidence.

### E10

- Location: III. Bayesianism and Process Tracing, p. 19
- Quote: "Hoop tests involve evidence that is certain, but not unique; failing a hoop test disqualifies an explanation, but passing it does not greatly increase confidence in that explanation." / "Van Evera's example of a hoop test is: "Was the accused in the state on the day of the murder?" Failing this hoop test falsifies the hypothesis."
- Key point: Hoop test: the evidence is certain but not unique to the hypothesis; failing the test disqualifies the explanation, but passing it does not greatly increase confidence. Van Evera's example: was the accused in the state on the day of the murder? If not, they are ruled out.

### E11

- Location: III. Bayesianism and Process Tracing, p. 19
- Quote: "Whether passing a hoop test constitutes strong evidence of the truth of the hypothesis depends on how frequently the "pass" condition occurs naturally"
- Key point: Whether passing a hoop test is strong evidence depends on how common the "pass" condition is in the first place.

### E12

- Location: III. Bayesianism and Process Tracing, p. 20
- Quote: "Smoking gun tests are unique, but not certain. Passing a smoking gun test is sufficient for strongly affirming an explanation, but passing such a test is not necessary to build confidence in an explanation."
- Key point: Smoking-gun test: the evidence is unique to the explanation but not certain; passing it is sufficient to strongly affirm the explanation, but failing it does not rule the explanation out.

### E13

- Location: III. Bayesianism and Process Tracing, p. 20
- Quote: "Doubly decisive tests use evidence that is both unique and certain, or that is necessary and sufficient to provide great confidence in an explanation." / "Van Evera uses the example of a bank camera that catches the faces of all those involved in robbing the bank."
- Key point: Doubly decisive test: the evidence is both unique and certain, so passing gives great confidence in the explanation. Example: a bank camera that catches the faces of all the robbers.

### E14

- Location: III. Bayesianism and Process Tracing, p. 20
- Quote: "Any one such test is not very decisive, but it is possible that a series of such tests might increase confidence in one explanation and decrease that in others, just as a suspect might be convicted on the basis of many pieces of circumstantial evidence"
- Key point: A single straw-in-the-wind test is not very decisive, but a series of them can raise confidence in one explanation, just as a suspect can be convicted on many pieces of circumstantial evidence.

### E15

- Location: III. Bayesianism and Process Tracing, p. 21
- Quote: "When evidence on a particular step of a process becomes sufficiently repetitive that it is to be expected, it loses any ability to further discriminate among competing hypotheses."
- Key point: Once evidence on a particular step becomes so repetitive that it is expected, it can no longer discriminate among competing hypotheses.

### E16

- Location: IV. Techniques and Standards, pp. 21–22
- Quote: "For phenomena on which there is little prior knowledge and for cases that are not well-explained by extant theories, process tracing proceeds primarily through inductive study." / "Here, one immerses oneself in the details of the case and tries out protohypotheses that may either quickly prove to be dead ends or become plausible and worthy of more rigorous testing."
- Key point: For phenomena with little prior knowledge and cases that existing theories explain poorly, process tracing proceeds mainly inductively: you immerse yourself in the details of the case and try out proto-hypotheses.

### E17

- Location: IV. Techniques and Standards, p. 22
- Quote: "A key step here is to develop case-specific observable implications of the theories in question" / "as theories are seldom specified in such precise ways that they offer tight predictions on the observable implications that should be evident in particular cases."
- Key point: A key step is translating theories into case-specific observable implications, since theories are seldom precise enough to say what should be seen in a particular case.

### E18

- Location: IV. Techniques and Standards, p. 22
- Quote: "researchers should seek to identify additional observable implications or what Imre Lakatos called "new facts" to test each modification to a hypothesis in order to avoid confirmation bias."
- Key point: Test each modification of a hypothesis against additional observable implications ("new facts") to avoid confirmation bias.

### E19

- Location: IV. Techniques and Standards, p. 23
- Quote: "it is only necessary that any new evidence from within the case be independent of the evidence that generated the hypothesis."
- Key point: Evidence testing a new hypothesis can come from the same case, as long as it is independent of the evidence that generated the hypothesis.

### E20

- Location: IV. Techniques and Standards, p. 23
- Quote: "One useful technique here is to predict what the unavailable evidence will indicate once it becomes available; such predictions, if borne out, provide strong confirmatory evidence."
- Key point: For evidence not yet available (e.g., classified archives), predict what it will show once released; if the prediction is borne out, that is strong confirmation.

### E21

- Location: IV. Techniques and Standards, p. 23
- Quote: "On the basis of interviews and a reading of primary documentation, predictions were made about socialization dynamics; these were subsequently confirmed through the release of previously classified meeting minutes"
- Key point: Example: Checkel made predictions from interviews and primary documents that were later confirmed by declassified meeting minutes.

### E22

- Location: IV. Techniques and Standards, p. 23
- Quote: "The bigger the modification, the more important it is to generate and test new observable implications to guard against 'just so' stories that explain away anomalies one at a time."
- Key point: When revising an explanation in the face of contrary evidence, the bigger the change, the more important it is to generate and test new observable implications, to guard against "just so" stories that explain away anomalies one at a time.

### E23

- Location: IV. Techniques and Standards, pp. 23–24
- Quote: "If we expect evidence to be hard to find or access, as when thinking social actors have gone to great lengths to hide evidence of their actions" / "or motives, then absence of evidence might not greatly lower our expectation that an entity or relationship exists."
- Key point: If evidence is expected to be hard to find (e.g., actors deliberately hid it), absence of evidence does not greatly lower the probability that it exists.

### E24

- Location: IV. Techniques and Standards, p. 24
- Quote: "it can be applied to a few cases selected from a statistical analysis to clarify whether the direction of causal influence is indeed from the independent variable to the dependent variable, and not the reverse, and to help assess whether any observed correlations might be spurious."
- Key point: In nested analysis, process tracing on a few cases selected from a statistical analysis can clarify the direction of causation and help judge whether a correlation is spurious.

### E25

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, p. 26
- Quote: "Failing to consider a potentially viable explanation that readily occurs to the readers and critics of a case study can make the process tracing unconvincing."
- Key point: Overlooking a viable explanation that readers and critics will readily think of makes process tracing unconvincing.

### E26

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 1, p. 26
- Quote: "researchers should at a minimum assess the process tracing evidence on the explanations that political scientists, sociologists, historians, regional specialists, and functional experts have offered for the specific case under study" / "In addition, it is often useful to render in theoretical terms and do process tracing upon the understandings of actor behavior offered by participants and journalists."
- Key point: At a minimum, assess the explanations that political scientists, sociologists, historians, regional specialists, and functional experts have offered for the case; it is also often useful to render the actors' and journalists' understandings of the behavior into theoretical explanations and test them.

### E27

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 1, p. 26
- Quote: "These include explanations based on actors' material power, institutional constraints and opportunities, and social norms or legitimacy"
- Key point: Check that no major category of explanation is missing: material power, institutional constraints and opportunities, and social norms or legitimacy.

### E28

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 1, p. 27
- Quote: "Some may be irrelevant to the case at hand, and others may be easily dismissed in the footnotes on the basis of one or a few pieces of obvious or wellknown process-tracing evidence."
- Key point: Not every alternative explanation needs detailed tracing; some are irrelevant to the case, and others can be dismissed in a footnote on one or two pieces of obvious evidence.

### E29

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 2, p. 28
- Quote: "Some case studies accord an unduly privileged status to one explanation by granting it 'first mover advantage'" / "A far better procedure is to outline the process tracing predictions of a wide range of alternative explanations of a case in advance, and then to consider the actual evidence for and against each explanation."
- Key point: Some case studies give one explanation "first mover advantage" and turn to others only when it hits anomalies; a far better procedure is to set out the predictions of a wide range of alternative explanations in advance and then weigh the evidence for and against each.

### E30

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 3, p. 28
- Quote: "When those providing evidence may have instrumental motives for putting forth particular explanations of their own or others' behavior, researchers should apply a two-step Bayesian analysis." / "First, they should attach Bayesian priors to the possible instrumental motives of those providing evidence and weigh the evidence they give in light of those priors. Then, in a second step, researchers should use the evidence that sources provide to update prior expectations on these sources' motives"
- Key point: When those providing evidence may have instrumental motives to push a particular explanation, first discount the evidence by estimating their motives, then use the evidence they provide to update your judgment of those motives.

### E31

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 3, p. 29
- Quote: "Spontaneous statements have a different evidentiary status from prepared remarks. Public statements have a different evidentiary status from private statements or statements that will remain classified for a period of time."
- Key point: Spontaneous and prepared statements have different evidentiary status, as do public statements and private statements or those that will stay classified for a time.

### E32

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 3, p. 29
- Quote: "Newly empowered actors in control of the archives are likely to make available only negative information about their opponents and positive information about themselves."
- Key point: Newly empowered actors in control of the archives are likely to release only negative information about their opponents and positive information about themselves.

### E33

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 3, p. 29
- Quote: "Historians are always at risk of selectively choosing the primary and secondary sources that confirm their arguments. For this reason, it is important to consider a wide range of secondary accounts representing contending historiographical schools and explanations"
- Key point: Historians always risk selecting sources that confirm their arguments, so consult a wide range of secondary accounts from contending historiographical schools and explanations.

### E34

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 4, p. 29
- Quote: "Prior expectations on the strength and scope conditions of a theory require the most updating when it fails to explain a case in which it is most likely to apply, or succeeds in explaining a case in which it is least likely to apply."
- Key point: Views of a theory need the most updating when it fails in a case where it is most likely to apply, or succeeds in a case where it is least likely to apply.

### E35

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 5, p. 30
- Quote: "There is no universal answer to such questions, as a justifiable starting point depends on how a researcher defines the puzzle or question they are trying to explain"
- Key point: There is no universal answer to where to start tracing; it depends on how the researcher defines the puzzle to be explained.

### E36

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 5, p. 30
- Quote: "A reasonable place may be a critical juncture at which an institution or practice was contingent or open to alternative paths, and actors or exogenous events determined which path it would take."
- Key point: A reasonable starting point is a critical juncture, when an institution or practice was open to alternative paths and actors or exogenous events determined which path it took.

### E37

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 6, p. 31
- Quote: "A single meeting or memo may prove to be the crucial piece of evidence that instantiates one explanation or undermines another. The more probative we expect evidence to be, the more time and effort we should be willing to expend to obtain it."
- Key point: A single meeting or memo can be the crucial evidence; the more probative you expect evidence to be, the more time and effort it is worth spending to obtain it.

### E38

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 6, p. 32
- Quote: "Seemingly "diverse" sources of evidence (documents, media coverage, interviews) could actually all originate from one or a few individuals with instrumental reasons to convince observers of a particular explanation."
- Key point: Seemingly diverse sources (documents, media coverage, interviews) may all originate from one or a few people with instrumental reasons to promote a particular explanation.

### E39

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 6, p. 32
- Quote: "Elizabeth Wood's excellent study of the Salvadoran civil war, for example, advances a rich, process-based argument that draws on an enormous amount of information. Yet, it was also fifteen years in the making" / "Carefully executed process tracing thus requires that researchers think at an early point about their own financial limits and temporal constraints."
- Key point: Example: Elizabeth Wood's study of the Salvadoran civil war drew on an enormous amount of information and took fifteen years; so think early about your own funding and time constraints.

### E40

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 6, p. 32
- Quote: "One stops when repetition occurs. That is, a researcher should stop pursuing any one stream of evidence when it becomes so repetitive that gathering more of that same kind of evidence has a low probability of revising their estimate of the likely accuracy of alternative explanations."
- Key point: When to stop: stop pursuing a stream of evidence once it becomes so repetitive that more of the same is unlikely to change your assessment of the alternative explanations.

### E41

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 7, p. 33
- Quote: "process tracing within single cases can in fact lead to convincing explanations if appropriate evidence is accessible." / "Yet for many inferential purposes, comparative case studies can be more powerful sources of inference than single case designs."
- Key point: With appropriate evidence, process tracing in a single case can produce convincing explanations, but for many inferential purposes comparative case studies are more powerful.

### E42

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 7, p. 33
- Quote: "As most-similar cases rarely control for all but one potentially causal factor, process tracing can also establish that other differences between the cases do not account for the difference in their outcomes."
- Key point: Most-similar cases rarely differ in only one factor; process tracing can show that the other differences do not account for the difference in outcomes.

### E43

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 8, p. 34
- Quote: "it is important to pay attention to the feeling of surprise and to follow it up with efforts to explain surprising facts theoretically."
- Key point: Pay attention to the feeling of surprise, and follow it up by trying to explain surprising facts theoretically.

### E44

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 9, p. 34
- Quote: "Which actors should have known, said, and did what, and when? Who should have interacted with, worried about, or allied with whom?"
- Key point: Write out in advance: if an explanation is true, which actors should have known, said, and done what, and when; who should have interacted with, worried about, or allied with whom.

### E45

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, standard 10, p. 35
- Quote: "When the evidence does not allow high levels of confidence in supporting some hypotheses and discounting others, it is important to acknowledge the level of uncertainty that remains."
- Key point: When the evidence does not allow high confidence, acknowledge the uncertainty that remains.

### E46

- Location: Standards of Good Process Tracing, p. 35
- Quote: "For sure, not all ten criteria elaborated above may be relevant for any given study. However, they should serve as a starting point and checklist, thus maximizing the likelihood of conducting good process tracing."
- Key point: Not all ten standards will apply to every study, but they serve as a starting point and checklist.

### E47

- Location: V. Process Tracing on General Categories of Theories, p. 37
- Quote: "One option here is to compare actors' public statements with available private deliberations that they expected would not be revealed for some time."
- Key point: To judge actors' real beliefs, compare their public statements with private deliberations they expected would not be revealed for some time.

### E48

- Location: V. Process Tracing on General Categories of Theories, p. 38
- Quote: "it is important to establish the provenance and authorship of public statements, and to give spontaneous and unplanned statements more weight than planned ones as indicators of genuine beliefs." / "recollections of beliefs held in the past that are backed up by documentary evidence are more credible than those lacking such supporting evidence."
- Key point: Establish the provenance and authorship of public statements; give spontaneous statements more weight than planned ones as indicators of genuine beliefs; and treat recollections backed by documents as more credible than unsupported ones.

### E49

- Location: V. Process Tracing on General Categories of Theories, p. 38
- Quote: "we should expect evidence that an actor holds socially stigmatized beliefs to be harder to find than evidence that an actor shares widely accepted beliefs, so we should treat absence of evidence on the former differently from absence of evidence on the latter."
- Key point: Evidence of socially stigmatized beliefs is harder to find than evidence of widely accepted ones, so absence of evidence means something different in the two cases.

- Location: VI. Conclusion, p. 40

- Key point: The central challenge is to avoid "lazy mechanism-based storytelling."
