# Northwestern Pritzker Legal Research Center: Researching Case Law

- Source: `northwesterncaselaw`, [web page: overview](https://library.law.northwestern.edu/cases/overview), [Finding Cases page](https://library.law.northwestern.edu/cases/finding)
- Version: case law research guide of the legal research center (law library) of Northwestern University Pritzker School of Law, accessed 2026-10-06. It covers finding case law in the **United States** with three commercial databases: Westlaw, Lexis, and Bloomberg Law. Locations give page names. The "Determining if Case Law is Still Good" page (key: `northwesterngoodlaw`) is another page of the same guide.

### E1

- Location: Researching Case Law (overview page)
- Quote: "Keep records of your work throughout the process. Make notes of where you searched and your results from each step." / "This will keep you from having to repeat steps and will help you recall what search terms you used. If you cannot find a definitive answer, you will be also able to report on what you did find and where you looked."
- Key point: Keep records throughout: where you searched and the results of each step. That way you don't repeat work and you remember which search terms you used; if you can't find a definitive answer, you can still report what you found and where you looked.

### E2

- Location: Finding Cases
- Quote: "Unlike searching for secondary sources, you really want to make sure you find every case that may be relevant to your argument (either for or against)."
- Key point: Searching for case law is unlike searching for secondary sources: you want to find every case that may be relevant to your argument, whether for or against it.

### E3

- Location: Finding Cases
- Quote: "Secondary sources can lead you to the leading cases, plus related propositions that you need to show to bridge to your main claim."
- Key point: Secondary sources can lead you to the leading cases and to the related propositions you need to bridge to your main claim.

### E4

- Location: Finding Cases — One Good Case Method
- Quote: "Looking back: What cases are cited by the court in your One Good Case?" / "Looking forward: Using a citator such as Key Cite/Citing References (Westlaw) and Shepard’s (Lexis), what later cases interpret the cases you intend to rely on?" / "Looking out: Topical organizations (headnotes) categorize all published decisions, so a digest will give you good coverage of the universe of cases that exist."
- Key point: The "One Good Case" method: look back (which cases does it cite?); look forward (use a citator to find later cases that interpreted it); look across (use a topical case digest system covering all published decisions).

### E5

- Location: Finding Cases — When Are You Done?
- Quote: "The prevailing description is that when you keep seeing the same cases over and over again without anything new, you’re done."
- Key point: The prevailing description: when you keep seeing the same cases over and over with nothing new, you're done.

### E6

- Location: Finding Cases — When Are You Done?
- Quote: "Use at least one source/search that involves an editor attempting to assemble like cases together." / "Use at least one online Boolean search to see if cases exist that were not identified in your first source or were too recent to be indexed in any other system you used."
- Key point: Use at least one source in which an editor groups like cases together, and run at least one Boolean search (combining terms with AND, OR, etc.) to catch cases the first source missed or that are too new to have been added.

### E7

- Location: Finding Cases — When Are You Done?
- Quote: "Read entirely every case that might provide new cases, that you might cite or that your opponent might cite, and that is from the highest court (within reason depending on how many exist)."
- Key point: Read in full every case that might lead to new cases, that you or your opponent might cite, or that comes from the highest court (within reason if there are many).

### E8

- Location: Researching Case Law (overview page)
- Quote: "What overarching principles can you synthesize into a framework to demonstrate to a court, even if the framework is not specifically articulated by courts?"
- Key point: Consider whether you can synthesize the cases into an overarching framework, even if the courts have not articulated it themselves.

### E9

- Location: Finding Cases
- Quote: "Annotated codes provide many key cases that interpret a statute. However, they generally are not 100% comprehensive."
- Key point: Annotated codes list many key cases interpreting a statute, but they are generally not comprehensive.
