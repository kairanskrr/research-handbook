The authors are sociologists at Stanford University and Northwestern University in the US. Locations are given as section headings. ### E3

- Location: Forms of Replication in Quantitative Social Science
- Quote: "(1) Verifiability involves taking the results of an original study as the object of inquiry and typically asks limited questions regarding whether the same results are obtained by doing the same analyses on the same data. (2) Tests of robustness conduct a reanalysis on the original data using alternative specifications to see if the target finding is merely the result of analytic decisions. (3) Tests of repeatability involve collecting new data to determine whether key result of a study can be observed by using the original procedures to collect new data. Finally, (4) for inquiries into generalizability, the original study provides a premise for other work that trying to evaluate whether similar findings may be consistently observed across different methods or settings."
- Key point: Quantitative social science distinguishes four kinds of replication: verifiability (do the same analyses on the same data give the same results), robustness (do results hold under alternative analytic choices), repeatability (can the result be observed again with new data collected by the original procedure), and generalizability (do results hold across different methods or settings).

### E4

- Location: Verifiability
- Quote: "In quantitative social science, data are usually not collected by a study’s authors, and a complicated chain of actors may separate the origins of data from its analysis. For instance, skepticism of findings by McPherson et al. (2006) about increasing social isolation in the United States led to the discovery of 41 people which the survey center providing the data had incorrectly coded as having no confidants (McPherson et al 2008)."
- Key point: In quantitative social science, data are usually not collected by a study's authors, and a long chain of actors can separate where data come from and how they are analyzed. Example: skepticism about findings of increasing social isolation in the United States led to the discovery that the survey center supplying the data had miscoded 41 people as having no one to confide in.

### E5

- Location: Verifiability
- Quote: "The convention in sociology has largely been to treat the availability of data for verification as an ethical matter to be handled by individual requests." / "In sociology, Young and Horvath (2015) reported more than half of authors have refused to provide data or code to graduate students trying to verify 53 findings as a class exercise."
- Key point: The convention in sociology has largely been to treat making data available for verification as an individual ethical matter handled by requests. Young and Horvath (2015) reported that when graduate students in a class exercise tried to verify 53 results, more than half of the authors refused to provide data or code.

### E7

- Location: Verifiability
- Quote: "The larger point is that good reasons may be given for why data cannot be made publicly available, but it is hard to argue against expecting researchers to be transparent about what the conditions of data availability are, so that readers can know whether work might be verified independently in principle when assessing its credibility."
- Key point: There can be good reasons why data cannot be made public, but researchers should at least be transparent about the conditions of data availability, so readers know whether the work could in principle be verified independently.

### E8

- Location: Verifiability
- Quote: "documentation that makes work transparent to others is likely the best way of ensuring it will be able to be understood by collaborators and, perhaps most urgently, by oneself later in the long gestation that characterizes many research projects"
- Key point: Documentation that makes work transparent to others is also the best way to make sure collaborators, and especially you yourself, can still understand it later in the long gestation of many research projects.

### E9

- Location: Robustness
- Quote: "An abiding concern is that published findings represent a best-case scenario among all the arbitrary and debatable decisions made over the course of analyzing data. This might be due to “phacking,” where a researcher runs different analyses until they find support for their preferred hypothesis (Simonsohn et al. 2014), or to “HARKing” in which hypotheses presented as a priori were actually developed after results were known (Kerr 1998)."
- Key point: An abiding concern is that published findings are the best-case scenario among many debatable analytic decisions, whether through p-hacking (running different analyses until one supports the preferred hypothesis) or HARKing (hypothesizing after the results are known and presenting the hypothesis as if it came first).

### E10

- Location: Generalizability

- Key point: The US General Social Survey keeps the wording of its core items the same year after year, and the International Social Survey Programme asks the same questions across member nations. Both follow a dictum attributed to Duncan: "If you want to measure change, don't change the measure."

### E12

- Location: Generalizability
- Quote: "Our own impression is that, when psychologists are faced with divergent results from studies asking the same question, they tend to first consider how the designs of the two studies are different, whereas sociologists in the same situation tend to first consider how the samples for the two studies differ."
- Key point: The authors' impression: faced with divergent results on the same question, psychologists tend to first ask how the two studies' designs differ, while sociologists tend to first ask how their samples differ.

### E13

- Location: Generalizability
- Quote: "Experimental psychologists regularly use deliberately non-representative samples that are only interesting because they are presented as broadly illuminating about generalized human psychological processes; sociology focuses more on population samples or historical cases for which results might be taken as instructive even if they do not generalize farther than their target population."
- Key point: Experimental psychology regularly uses deliberately non-representative samples to study general human psychological processes; sociology focuses more on population samples or historical cases, and results are considered illuminating even if they do not generalize beyond the target population.

### E14

- Location: Generalizability
- Quote: "To the extent this is true, what empirical work tests is not so much propositions as such but ideas about the scope in which the propositions apply."
- Key point: If general sociological propositions hold under some conditions and not others, then empirical work tests not so much the propositions themselves as ideas about the scope in which they apply.

### E15

- Location: Replication in Qualitative Social Science
- Quote: "Qualitative social science is often presented as firstly an interpretive enterprise. In sociology, it is typically concerned with issues of motivation and meaning that give the verstehen approach its lasting appeal. It may be that “replication” is simply the wrong language to apply to qualitative studies."
- Key point: Qualitative social science is often presented as first of all an interpretive enterprise, which in sociology is usually concerned with motivation and meaning; "replication" may simply be the wrong language for qualitative research.

### E19

- Location: Replication in Qualitative Social Science
- Quote: "He advocates an iterative procedure in which qualitative researchers “replicate” observations through a number of distinct cases (following language of Yin [2002]). After each case, the research questions evolve to become more sophisticated. The process ends once the researcher subjectively feels “saturated” and believes that additional sites will yield little new information."
- Key point: Small (2009) advocates an iterative procedure: "replicate" observations across several distinct cases, refining the research questions after each case, and stop when the researcher subjectively feels "saturation," that is, when additional sites would yield little new information.
