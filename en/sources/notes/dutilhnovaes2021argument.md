# Dutilh Novaes 2021: Argument and Argumentation (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)

- Source: `dutilhnovaes2021argument`, [Original](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/argument/)
- Version: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry by Catarina Dutilh Novaes, first published July 16, 2021. Locations give section numbers. This file is a local excerpt made for task no. 106.

### E1

- Location: Opening
- Quote: "Argument is a central concept for philosophy. Philosophers rely heavily on arguments to justify claims"
- Key point: Argument is a central concept in philosophy; philosophers rely heavily on arguments to justify claims.

### E2

- Location: §2.4 Analogy
- Quote: "Analogical arguments continue to occupy a central position in philosophical discussions, and a number of the most prominent philosophical arguments of the last decades are analogical arguments"
- Key point: Analogical arguments remain central in philosophical discussion; some of the most prominent philosophical arguments of recent decades are analogical (e.g., Thomson's violinist argument and Searle's Chinese Room argument).

### E3

- Location: §2.4 Analogy
- Quote: "thought experiments are often based on analogical principles when seeking to make a point that transcends the thought experiment as such."
- Key point: Thought experiments often rely on analogy when they aim at a conclusion beyond the thought experiment itself.

### E4

- Location: §2.4 Analogy
- Quote: "both arguments have been criticized on the grounds that the purported similarity between the source and the target domains is not sufficient to extrapolate the property of the source domain"
- Key point: The weak point of analogical arguments: the similarity between source and target may not be enough to carry a property of the source over to the target.

### E5

- Location: §3.1 Adversarial and cooperative argumentation
- Quote: "Such aggressive practices reflect a vision of argumentation as a kind of competition or battle, where the goal is to "score points" and "beat the opponent"."
- Key point: Aggressive practices treat argumentation as a competition or battle, where the goal is to "score points" and "beat the opponent."

### E6

- Location: §3.1 Adversarial and cooperative argumentation
- Quote: "these practices exclude a number of people from participating in argumentative encounters, namely those for whom displays of aggression do not constitute socially acceptable behavior"
- Key point: Critics argue that aggressive argumentation excludes people for whom displays of aggression are not socially acceptable behavior.

### E7

- Location: §3.1 Adversarial and cooperative argumentation
- Quote: "adversariality conceived and practiced in different ways need not have the detrimental consequences of more extreme versions of belligerent argumentation."
- Key point: Other scholars argue that adversariality, conceived and practiced differently, need not have the harmful consequences of extreme belligerent argumentation.

### E8

- Location: §3.1 Adversarial and cooperative argumentation
- Quote: "It may be argued for example that actual argumentative encounters ought to be adversarial or cooperative to different degrees, as different types of argumentation are required for different situations"
- Key point: One can also argue that different situations call for different degrees of adversariality or cooperation.
