# Lazic, Clarke-Williams, and Munafò 2018: What exactly is 'N' in cell culture and animal experiments?

- Source: `lazic2018what`, [full text](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.2005282)
- Version: PLOS Biology 16(4):e2005282, 2018, open access. Text read from the article XML provided on the PLOS website, 2026-10-06. Locations give the subheading.

### E1

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "The wrong choice of replicate cannot be fixed by a clever statistical analysis after the experiment is completed (e.g., using multilevel or hierarchical models); it needs to be planned at the design stage."
- Key point: Replication at the wrong level cannot be fixed by a clever statistical analysis after the experiment is done; it has to be planned at the design stage.

### E2

- Location: Introduction
- Quote: "we argue that the frequent and common distinction made between ‘biological’ and ‘technical’ replication is unhelpful because they are inconsistently defined, do not capture the important characteristics of an experiment, and do not clarify what to replicate."
- Key point: The authors argue that the common "biological vs. technical replicate" distinction is unhelpful: it is defined inconsistently, misses what matters in the experiment, and does not say what should be replicated. They use three concepts instead: biological unit, experimental unit, and observational unit.

### E3

- Location: Box 2. Types of units
- Quote: "Experimental unit (EU): the entity that is randomly and independently assigned to experimental conditions. The sample size (N) is equal to the number of EUs." / "Observational unit (OU): the entity on which measurements are taken, which may be different from the EUs and BUs of interest. Increasing the number of OUs does not increase the sample size."
- Key point: The experimental unit is the entity randomly and independently assigned to experimental conditions, and the sample size N equals the number of experimental units; the observational unit is what gets measured, and adding observational units does not increase the sample size.

### E4

- Location: Requirements for genuine replication
- Quote: "First, EUs must be independently allocated to experimental conditions or interventions." / "The second requirement is that the experimental intervention must be applied independently to each EU." / "The third requirement is that EUs must not influence each other, especially on the measured outcome variables."
- Key point: Genuine replication requires three things: experimental units are independently allocated to conditions; the intervention is applied independently to each experimental unit; and experimental units do not influence each other, especially on the measured outcome.

### E5

- Location: Cell culture experiments
- Quote: "Hence, the third criterion is not met for using cells as the EU. Thus, a well, culture dish, or another plastic container is the appropriate EU for cell culture experiments."
- Key point: Cells in the same well are treated together and influence each other, so they cannot be the experimental unit; in cell culture experiments the experimental unit is the well, culture dish, or other container.

### E6

- Location: Cell culture experiments
- Quote: "For this reason, in vitro experiments are usually repeated on multiple days, and the number of wells, aliquots, or culture dishes within a day are treated as subsamples." / "This is a scientific judgement about the relevant unit that we would like to make inferences about, and although opinions may differ, using more stringent criteria makes the results more believable."
- Key point: In vitro experiments are sensitive to conditions, so the whole experiment is usually repeated on different days and the wells, aliquots, or dishes within a day count only as subsamples. This is a scientific judgment that people may disagree on, but the stricter the standard, the more credible the results.

### E7

- Location: Requirements for genuine replication
- Quote: "If we hypothesise that breast cancer cell lines proliferate at a faster rate than lung cancer cell lines, we need multiple breast and lung cell lines, as the hypothesis is about differences between the tissue of origin."
- Key point: Replicate at the level your hypothesis is about. If the hypothesis is that breast cancer cell lines proliferate faster than lung cancer cell lines, you need multiple breast and lung cancer cell lines.

### E8

- Location: Analysis of hierarchical data
- Quote: "a simple solution to the problem of pseudoreplication is to reduce the data to a single value for each EU."
- Key point: A simple way to avoid pseudoreplication: reduce the repeated measurements of each experimental unit to a single value (e.g., the mean) before the statistical analysis.

### E9

- Location: What is the extent of the problem?; Box 1
- Quote: "We found that only 22% of studies (95% CI = 17%–29%) had genuine replication and thus made valid statistical inferences. Nearly half of the studies (46%, 95% CI = 38%–53%) had pseudoreplication" / "restricting the search to non-review articles between January 2011 and September 2016"
- Key point: The authors sampled 200 animal studies from PubMed published January 2011 to September 2016 (treatment applied to parents, outcomes measured in offspring): only 22% had genuine replication, 46% had pseudoreplication, and the rest gave too little information to tell.

### E10

- Location: Requirements for genuine replication
- Quote: "to increase the sample size we need to increase the number of EUs (a useful phrase to remember is ‘sample size is where you randomise’)"
- Key point: To increase sample size you must increase the number of experimental units; a useful phrase to remember: "sample size is where you randomise."
