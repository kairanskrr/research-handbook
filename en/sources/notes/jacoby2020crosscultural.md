# Jacoby et al. 2020: Cross-Cultural Work in Music Cognition: Challenges, Insights, and Recommendations

- Source: `jacoby2020crosscultural`, [original (University of Amsterdam repository, published version)](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/45923408/185.full.pdf), DOI [10.1525/mp.2020.37.3.185](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2020.37.3.185)
- Version: Music Perception 37(3):185–195, 2020, a position paper by 20 authors from the Max Planck Institute for Empirical Aesthetics in Germany and universities in the US, Canada, the UK, the Netherlands, Australia, Japan, and elsewhere. The publisher's page lists it as CC BY open access; we used the published-version PDF in the University of Amsterdam repository, read on 2026-10-08. Locations give page numbers and subheadings.

### E1

- Location: p. 186, Introduction
- Quote: "Music psychology shares this sampling bias: participants are almost always recruited from WEIRD societies, experimental materials are usually drawn from Western music, and studies tend to investigate constructs such as harmonic progressions that are disproportionately relevant to Western music."
- Key point: Music psychology shares the sampling bias: participants almost always come from WEIRD (Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic) societies, materials are usually drawn from Western music, and studies tend to investigate constructs such as harmonic progressions that are disproportionately relevant to Western music.

### E4

- Location: p. 191, Empirical Methods: Issues
- Quote: "Every mode of data collection represents tradeoffs among many factors, including insider knowledge or lack thereof, reproducibility, precision, bias, accuracy, logistical feasibility, cost, invasiveness, ecological validity, and pragmatics." / "In every case (e.g., human participant observation, behavioral experimentation, neuroimaging), empiricism aims for data that are as reproducible and valid as possible."
- Key point: Every mode of data collection trades off factors such as reproducibility, precision, bias, cost, and ecological validity; whether the method is participant observation, behavioral experiments, or neuroimaging, the goal is data that are as reproducible and valid as possible.

### E5

- Location: p. 191, Empirical Methods: Issues
- Quote: "It can be problematic to impose external concepts, notation, or measures on participants from a different musical culture. For instance, it may be problematic to ask for perceived emotions expressed by music without having established that music is used to express emotions in a given cultural context."
- Key point: Imposing external concepts, notation, or measures on participants from another musical culture can be problematic; for example, asking for the emotions music expresses before establishing that music is used to express emotion there.

### E6

- Location: p. 191, Empirical Methods: Recommendations
- Quote: "When rating scales are used, the number of scale steps could be limited to three or four to enable clear labeling of each scale step with appropriate adjectives or pictures. Where such scales are used, a control experiment is necessary to validate understanding of the scale" / "These and other materials used across cultures should be pilot tested in collaboration with experts in the culture."
- Key point: When using rating scales across cultures, you can limit them to three or four steps, each clearly labeled with suitable adjectives or pictures; a control experiment is needed to check that participants understand the scale, and materials used across cultures should be piloted with experts familiar with the culture.

### E7

- Location: p. 191, Empirical Methods: Recommendations
- Quote: "One strategy for minimizing misinterpretation is to avoid the use of language and musical notation, with their attendant translation issues. Numerous approaches could accomplish this goal such as the use of physiological measures" / "or production tasks like tapping along with a rhythm or singing a melody"
- Key point: One way to reduce misinterpretation is to avoid language and musical notation, for example with physiological measures or production tasks such as tapping along to a rhythm or singing a melody.

### E8

- Location: p. 188, Music: Recommendations
- Quote: "All scientific research involves a tradeoff between experimental control and ecological validity. The latter can be amplified by turning to paradigms that incorporate diverse musics and performance scenarios rather than relying on the most readily available and commonly used materials."
- Key point: All scientific research trades experimental control against ecological validity; including diverse musics and performance scenarios, rather than only the most readily available and commonly used materials, can increase ecological validity.
