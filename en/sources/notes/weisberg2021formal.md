# Weisberg 2021: Formal Epistemology (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)

- Source: `weisberg2021formal`, [original](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/formal-epistemology/)
- Version: Entry in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (hosted by Stanford University, USA, open access) by Jonathan Weisberg (University of Toronto, Canada), first published 2015, substantively revised March 2021; accessed 2026-10-08. Locations give the entry section. This is one author's survey written for the encyclopedia, not the rules of a society or journal.

### E1

- Location: Opening
- Quote: "Formal epistemology explores knowledge and reasoning using “formal” tools, tools from math and logic. For example, a formal epistemologist might use probability theory to explain how scientific reasoning works. Or she might use modal logic to defend a particular theory of knowledge."
- Key point: Formal epistemology studies knowledge and reasoning with tools from math and logic, for example using probability theory to explain scientific reasoning, or modal logic to defend a theory of knowledge.

### E2

- Location: §2 The Problem of Induction, end of §2.2
- Quote: "Formal epistemology gives us precise ways of stating how induction works. But these precise formulations do not themselves solve a problem like Hume’s, for they rely on assumptions like the probability axioms, Carnap’s assignment of prior probabilities, and Conditionalization. Still, they do help us isolate and clarify these assumptions, and then formulate various arguments in their defense. Whether formal epistemology thereby aids in the solution of Hume’s problem depends on whether these formulations and justifications are plausible, which is controversial."
- Key point: Formalization gives precise statements, but by itself it does not solve philosophical problems, because it relies on assumptions such as the probability axioms; its role is to isolate and articulate these assumptions and then argue for them. Whether this advances the problem depends on whether the formulations and their justifications hold up, which is contested.

### E3

- Location: §5.1 The Zollman Effect
- Quote: "Since the above model is both simple and idealized, this lack of robustness should give us pause, they argue, about its likely applicability in real-world scenarios."
- Key point: (Reporting the view of Rosenstock, Bruner, and O'Connor) When a model is both simple and idealized and its results are not robust (appearing only under fairly specific conditions), we should doubt whether it applies to real-world situations.

### E4

- Location: §6.3 The Meaning of 'If…Then…' (belief revision theory)
- Quote: "Of course, real people don’t believe everything their beliefs entail, but it helps keep things simple to make this assumption. You can think of it as an idealization: we’re theorizing about what your beliefs should look like if you were a perfect logician."
- Key point: Formal models often make idealizing assumptions, such as that people believe everything their beliefs entail; this keeps things simple, and the theory describes what the beliefs of a "perfect logician" should look like.
