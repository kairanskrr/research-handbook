A review of experimental design written for agronomists and published in an agronomy journal. Locations are given as subheadings or paragraph content.

### E1

- Location: Abstract
- Quote: "Four basic tenets or pillars of experimental design— replication, randomization, blocking, and size of experimental units— can be used creatively, intelligently, and consciously to solve both real and perceived problems in comparative experiments."
- Key point: Experimental design rests on four pillars: replication, randomization, blocking, and the size of experimental units. Use them deliberately to solve problems in comparative experiments.

### E5

- Location: Replication section, the prairie-burning example
- Quote: "the researcher chooses to conduct the experiment on two 1-ha plots or experimental units, one burned and one an unburned control. [...] Even though the statistical analysis makes sense and is completely valid, the science is flawed—the experimental design does not allow an unequivocal interpretation of the results."
- Key point: Example: two 1-ha plots, one burned and one unburned, with many soil samples taken from each. The statistical analysis is valid, but the science is flawed: the design does not allow an unambiguous interpretation of the results.

### E6

- Location: Replication section, the prairie-burning example
- Quote: "Hurlbert (1984) coined the term pseudoreplication for this situation in which experimental treatments are replicated but at a scale that is not sufficient to provide a valid scientific interpretation of the results, i.e., complete confounding of treatments with experimental units"
- Key point: Hurlbert (1984) called this pseudoreplication: treatments are replicated, but not at a scale sufficient for valid interpretation, so treatments are completely confounded with experimental units.

### E7

- Location: Replication section, the prairie-burning example
- Quote: "replication in time, e.g., across years, could be conducted at the proper scale but only if the treatments are independently randomized to experimental units in each year, i.e., the experiment is repeated on a new section of prairie in each year."
- Key point: Replication in time (across years) can work, but only if treatments are independently randomized to experimental units each year, that is, the experiment is repeated on a new section of prairie each year.

### E8

- Location: Number of replicates section, resource allocation
- Quote: "It is always clear from these exercises that the number of locations and/or years should be maximized relative to the number of replicates (e.g., McCann et al., 2012). The only situation that favors maximizing the number of replicates is when all the treatment × environment interactions are zero, which is rarely the case."
- Key point: Resource allocation calculations based on variance components show that the number of locations and years should be maximized relative to the number of replicates per location; maximizing replicates pays off only when there are no treatment-by-environment interactions at all, which is rare.

### E9

- Location: Replication section
- Quote: "This is analogous to the very common practice of repeating experiments across multiple locations and years for the purpose of assessing treatment differences under different environmental circumstances. As long as no attempt is made to attribute simple effects of interactions to unreplicated environmental variables, which cannot be conclusively resolved, the researcher is relatively safe from criticism."
- Key point: Repeating experiments across multiple locations and years to see how treatments differ across environments is very common. As long as you do not attribute interactions to unreplicated environmental variables (which cannot be resolved conclusively), you are relatively safe from criticism.

### E11

- Location: Replication section, special cases
- Quote: "Many on-farm experiments represent a classic example of this situation. Farmers and outreach personnel see value in trying different treatments on a very large scale but see little value in replication of those treatments. Researchers organizing these types of field experiments have three options: (i) conduct the experiments on multiple farms, using farms as blocks; (ii) use control-plot designs in which one control treatment is interspersed with the other treatments, optimally in an every-other-plot pattern; or (iii) use a combination of both approaches."
- Key point: In on-farm trials, farmers and extension staff are willing to try treatments on a large scale but see little value in replication. Researchers have three options: run the trial on several farms, using farms as blocks; use a control-plot design with the control interspersed among the other treatments, ideally in every other plot; or combine the two.

### E12

- Location: Replication section, participatory research
- Quote: "The most traditional application involves the use of a “mother” trial, conducted with proper replication and randomization on an experimental research station, accompanied by numerous “daughter” trials conducted by various landholders (Fig. 7)."
- Key point: The "mother-daughter" design in participatory research: a "mother" trial with proper replication and randomization on a research station, plus many "daughter" trials run by individual farmers.

### E13

- Location: Randomization section
- Quote: "Randomization helps to ensure that those unknown or unexpected sources of variation do not introduce biases, confounding, or elimination of valid hypothesis tests during the course of the experiment."
- Key point: Randomization ensures that unknown or unexpected sources of variation do not introduce bias or confounding, or rule out valid hypothesis tests.

### E14

- Location: Blocking section
- Quote: "Blocking is utilized in experimental designs for one or both of two purposes: (i) for precision, to create groups of experimental units that are more homogeneous than would occur with random sampling of the entire population of experimental units; or (ii) for convenience, to allow different sizes of experimental units when larger plots or larger experimental areas are required for the application of one factor compared with other factors."
- Key point: Blocking serves two purposes: precision, by grouping more homogeneous experimental units together; and convenience, by allowing larger plots for a given factor.

### E15

- Location: Blocking section
- Quote: "I can cite examples of the use of randomized complete block designs (RCBD) being used to successfully recover from post-establishment disturbances to colleagues' experiments, including military maneuvers (three different incidents), a house mover, wayward herbicide sprayers (twice), a motorcycle gang, an angry and vengeful former employee, and partying students."
- Key point: The author cites randomized complete block designs that saved colleagues' experiments from all kinds of disturbances: military maneuvers, a house mover, misapplied herbicide, a motorcycle gang, a vengeful former employee, and partying students. Blocking works like insurance.

### E16

- Location: Blocking section, split-plot designs
- Quote: "They are frequently used strictly for convenience, when the whole-plot factor requires larger experimental units than the subplot factor(s). Common examples include tillage treatments, irrigation treatments, and planting dates."
- Key point: Split-plot designs are often used for convenience, when one factor (the whole-plot factor) needs larger units. Common examples are tillage, irrigation, and planting date.

### E17

- Location: Blocking section
- Quote: "Because many experimental research stations consist of large and visually uniform fields, bidirectional blocking is an excellent insurance policy against guessing wrong on the blocking arrangement."
- Key point: Many research stations have large, visually uniform fields; blocking in both directions insures against guessing the blocking direction wrong.

### E18

- Location: Blocking section
- Quote: "when the number of treatments is >20, all researchers should consider the general principle that more blocks and smaller blocks are nearly always more efficient than complete blocks"
- Key point: With more than 20 treatments, more and smaller blocks are nearly always more efficient than complete blocks.

### E23

- Location: Conclusions
- Quote: "Rarely can these questions be answered when P values are high, resulting in unpublishable results and wasted time and money."
- Key point: When P values are high, it is rarely possible to tell whether the treatments truly do not differ, the design was flawed, or the experiment was disturbed; the results are often unpublishable, wasting time and money.

### E24

- Location: Replication section, blocks and years as random effects
- Quote: "Alternatively, care should be taken in designing such experiments to allow the blocking factor to be treated as a random effect, e.g., in which each block could be considered a random observation from a larger population of levels."
- Key point: Design the experiment so that blocks (such as locations or years) can be treated as random effects, that is, each block as a random draw from a larger population.

### E32

- Location: Randomization section
- Quote: "Randomization is a strict mathematical process in which a random number generator is used to order the treatments. Interspersion is a subjective concept in which the researcher strives to achieve balance and avoid “clumping” of certain treatments or replicates of treatments."
- Key point: Randomization is a strict process of ordering treatments with a random number generator; interspersion is the researcher's subjective effort to achieve balance and avoid clumping of treatments.

### E33

- Location: Randomization section
- Quote: "Sometimes randomization can result in experimental designs that appear to have undesirable clumping or patterns. These patterns can result in potential bias or confounding to treatment effects if there are underlying spatial variation patterns that are correlated with the randomization plan"
- Key point: Randomization sometimes produces clumped layouts; if spatial variation in the field happens to correlate with the layout, this can cause bias or confounding.

### E34

- Key point: Spatially balanced complete block designs were developed to solve this problem, combining randomization and interspersion.

### E35

### E36

- Location: Size of experimental units section (Smith's Law)
- Quote: "The concept is based on Smith's Law (Smith, 1938), which derived from the general observation of a negative asymptotic relationship between variance (on a per-unit or single-plot basis) and plot size"
- Key point: Smith's Law: the variance of a single plot decreases as plot size increases, leveling off asymptotically.

### E37

- Location: Size of experimental units section
- Quote: "For small initial plot sizes, any change in plot size is expected to have a large effect on mean unit variance. Conversely, for researchers who are using relatively large plots, relatively large changes in plot size may have little or no impact on the mean unit variance."
- Key point: With small plots, any change in size has a large effect on variance; with large plots, even big changes in size have little effect.

### E39

- Location: Blocking section, split-plot designs
- Quote: "A common misconception of the split-plot randomization is that they represent an experimental design per se, e.g., a split-plot with Factor A as the whole-plot factor and Factor B as the subplot factor. In fact, this represents an incomplete description of the experimental design because it is missing the basic design for Factor A, the whole-plot factor."
- Key point: Writing only "a split-plot with A as the whole-plot factor and B as the subplot factor" is incomplete; it leaves out the basic design used for whole-plot factor A (for example, a randomized complete block).

### E40

- Location: Replication section, special cases
- Quote: "These designs fell out of favor when it became clear that devoting half of the experimental units to a single treatment or cultivar was highly inefficient (Melton and Finckner, 1967), and modern spatial analysis methods were just emerging (Gaeton and Guyon, 2010). Nevertheless, they remain a very viable option for small on-farm experiments."
- Key point: Control-plot designs (a control in every other plot) devote half the units to the control, which is very inefficient, so they fell out of favor; but they remain quite feasible for small on-farm trials.

- Key point: In augmented designs, check cultivars are added at random to blocks to estimate error and spatial effects at a finer scale.

### E43

- Location: Replication section, on-farm trials
- Quote: "Researchers organizing these types of field experiments have three options: (i) conduct the experiments on multiple farms, using farms as blocks; (ii) use control-plot designs in which one control treatment is interspersed with the other treatments, optimally in an every-other-plot pattern; or (iii) use a combination of both approaches."
- Key point: Three options for on-farm trials: use several farms as blocks; intersperse a control plot among the other treatments; or combine the two.
